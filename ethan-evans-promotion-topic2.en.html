A promotion is not a reward, it is a deal

The Magic Loop, relationships, a one-line case, and a shrinking supply of seats

Topic How promotion works · Articles 19 free Level Up posts read, 13 cited (2023–2026), plus 1 guest post as side evidence · Interview The Peterman Pod, first episode (Aug 2025) · Video YouTube

His claims

1. Promotion is a deal between you and your manager

Evans starts from an idea borrowed from Amazon. In May 2023 he wrote that Bezos did not fixate on outputs like sales and profit; he fixated on price, selection and convenience, the things a team can change every day: the controllable inputs. Careers work the same way. Pay and title are outputs you cannot move today. What you can move is whether your hours go to work that matters, and whether you build a network before you need it.

A month later the idea became the framework he has taught (and sold courses on) ever since, the Magic Loop. The core is four steps: do your current work very well; ask your manager where you can help them or the group; do that well too; go back to step two. Advanced versions move from asking to suggesting, and finally to seeing what is needed and just doing it. He calls it the most effective repeatable path to promotion he has found in decades.

The point is not working more. The point is that step two flips the direction of the relationship. He says managers he has polled report that their reports rarely ask what they can do to help. The 2023 post sells the loop as win/win; in the 2025 interview he shows the cards and calls it a deal: I do everything you need, and you do one critical thing, which is make sure I am rewarded.

I can either work for you as a standard cog in a box or I can really push and go above and beyond, but there's got to be a payoff— Ethan Evans · [00:23:43]

That is how he made VP: it took two and a half years before his boss was ready to put his own credibility on the line. Deals can also default. Early at Amazon he struck the same bargain with a junior engineer and then failed to deliver because he did not know the twice-a-year review cycle (No. 1 covers this). His lesson: before you make the deal, find out whether your manager knows the promotion process.

2. Relationships are not extra credit

The Magic Loop answers "what to do." Evans spends more ink on "who will speak for you." His October 2023 post Relationships Drive Promotion opens without hedging:

Many purists believe that only performance should factor into promotion. The Straight Truth is that this is an idealized fantasy that denies brain science.— Ethan Evans · Relationships Drive Promotion, 2023

The "brain science" is really an attribution bias. When someone we like makes a mistake, we find reasons for them: traffic, a sick spouse, too much work. When someone we dislike makes one, we find proof that they are lazy or careless. He told the same example almost unchanged in a talk in early 2026, a sign of how central it is to him.

Where does the relationship come from? Two months earlier, his "formula" for trust laid out a spectrum. The rational end is reliability: trust as consistency over time. The emotional end is likability: genuinely caring about the other person. In the middle is sacrifice: we trust most those who did hard things for us, like soldiers who served together. By 2025 he had compressed it to one line:

Be valuable and be compatible.— Ethan Evans · Who managers promote and why, 2025

Two trade-offs follow: the more valuable you are, the less compatibility matters, and the reverse. That, he says, explains people in high roles whom others cannot account for: the leader above found someone they could trust and rely on. The claim only hardens over three years. In 2024 he relayed an Amazon VP who said attitude had become a deciding factor in a low-growth market; in 2026 he asks from the executive's chair why anyone would pull closer a person they neither enjoy nor trust.

Note how he handles "isn't that just kissing up?" He does not argue. He says you may keep that view, but accept the result: being overlooked. That is the signature move of his straight-talk brand, describing the rules like weather and declining to judge whether they should be so.

3. Promotion is a trial, and you need a one-line case

Relationships make your manager willing to speak; then they need something to say. In 2024 he published a promotion document skeleton with two parts: the scope and challenge of the role, and whether it warrants a leader at the next level; and the candidate's performance. The most Evans-like line: if you have a weakness everyone knows about, write it in yourself, with a mitigation plan and progress, rather than let someone raise it in the room and put your manager on the defensive.

By 2026 he had made it shorter. Executives do not first read a long document; the person arguing for you needs a "tweet":

Because she did X, we got Y. That’s why she deserves this role.— Ethan Evans · How to Break Into Senior Leadership, 2026

His own tweet came from early Prime Video. TiVo wanted a partnership; his SVP thought it a distraction; Evans said he would do it unless ordered not to. It worked, and Samsung, Sony and other TV makers came asking for the same. In his Director packet, the line that mattered was from the marketing VP: without Ethan, no TiVo. In the interview he splits promotions into two kinds, the "lifetime achievement award," where years of good work eventually grind through the process, and the one earned by an outsized event. [00:16:26] He is clearly teaching the second.

A trial also needs witnesses. He describes defending a report's Director promotion before 20-plus AWS VPs who were picking the document apart, until an SVP who did not know the candidate cut in: you're doing this all wrong, can I do it? With no stake in the outcome, the SVP counted as an independent judge, and the promotion passed. Evans's conclusion is that such a voice can be arranged: before his own VP promotion, his boss had another SVP mentor him for a year, and that SVP was in the room.

For the engineer's version, see Ryan Peterman's guest post in the same newsletter (written by Peterman, not Evans): big-tech promotions are lagging, and you must sustain next-level work for six to twelve months before a committee will sign off.

4. Where the seats come from: from "patience" to "make them worry"

The first three claims are about becoming eligible; this one is about the open seat. It is where his advice has shifted most in three years.

In July 2023 his prescription was patience, opportunism and a plan. Most people work forty-plus years, he reminded readers:

being frantic to achieve everything in your 20s or even mid-30s will burn you out AND hold you back.— Ethan Evans · Path to Promotion, 2023

From 2024, as growth slowed, his attention moved to the seats themselves. Klingon Promotions is about stepping up when the leader above you is removed; the name comes from the Star Trek warriors who take command by killing their captain, and he insists he means nothing violent. The method: before the change, fill the gaps your struggling leader leaves, so that when they are moved you are the obvious replacement. In 2025 he wrote:

Half of my own career growth, roughly, happened in reorganizations.— Ethan Evans · Reorgs are the new promotion, 2025

Promotions go through committees and paperwork; reorgs are often one or two leaders in a room putting names into boxes on a new chart.

By September 2026, patience has almost left the prescription. Picture a manager with two equally hard workers: X waits quietly, Y makes clear they expect to move up. The manager thinks X will understand and can wait a cycle, while Y might leave.

You want to be Y, not X.— Ethan Evans · How to Break Into Senior Leadership, 2026

The script he teaches is no ultimatum, just "my career is very important to me, and I need to know if it is as important to the company," the very words he used for his own Director promotion (episode 1 covers that interview). From "this is a decades-long game" to "don't be the one who waits," what changed is not his values but the supply of seats: in a growth era you can wait; in a flat one, those who wait go to the back. Yet his 2024 post on limited Big Tech promotions still carries the opposite advice: do not optimize for the fastest next promotion; find work you are great at and love. The two sit side by side, and he has never reconciled them.

How the claims fit together

Put together, his model is a three-way relationship with the manager as the hub. Your inputs are raw material; the manager is the only one who can start the process. As he wrote in 2025, if the manager does not undertake the paperwork, nothing happens. Whether they will depends on whether you are valuable to them and whether they like working with you, and the Magic Loop feeds both at once: helping the manager creates value and builds the relationship. The document and the "tweet" translate that into language a committee understands; independent witnesses and the skip level answer the suspicion that a manager is biased. The seats are the external constraint: everything above only sets your place in line, and whether the front of the line has an opening depends on growth, reorgs and other people leaving.

This ties straight back to the four discounts in No. 1. Twice-yearly cycles, documents and committees are what the company discount targets; the turn from patience to pressure is the era discount playing out live; and every "here is what managers really think" sits inside the seat discount. Looking ahead, "Stuck at L7" will ask why this machinery suddenly jams at senior manager, and "Managing up" expands the second claim.

Boundaries

"Performance-only promotion is an idealized fantasy that denies brain science." — Relationships Drive Promotion, 2023

Supported

A 2016 meta-analysis by Martin and colleagues pooled 146 samples and found leader–member exchange (LMX) quality correlates about 0.30 with task performance, and 0.24 with objective measures, with evidence that good relationships lead to performance rather than the reverse. So relationships matter, but not only as bias: people in strong relationships are trusted and motivated, and actually do better work.

"The most effective repeatable path to promotion I have found… This process works for everyone." — The Magic Loop, 2023

Supported, but overstated

Seibert and colleagues (2001) followed 180 employees for two years: proactive people, through career initiative, political knowledge and innovation, saw more salary growth and promotions, which points the loop's way. But in the same study, voice (speaking up with challenges) was negatively related to career progression. "Most effective" and "works for everyone" rest only on his and his students' experience.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Raft leader election: in Raft, a voter grants its vote only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own, so when the leader drops out, the follower who kept up best is most likely to win. A Klingon Promotion has the same shape: before the leader is replaced you are already covering what they cannot do, keeping your log current. The difference: Raft elections are symmetric and automatic, triggered by timeouts; a company has no timer, and who replaces whom, and when, is decided behind a closed door.
  • Game theory · Axelrod and the shadow of the future: in The Evolution of Cooperation, Robert Axelrod showed cooperation is stable only in repeated games where the future matters enough. The Magic Loop turns a one-shot "work for pay" exchange into a repeated game. Unlike the model, managers change often and the shadow can be cut off; Evans's VP promotion was six months from meeting a new boss.
  • Economics · Spence's signaling: Michael Spence (1973) argued a signal is credible only if it is costly to send. The SVP who speaks for you carries weight because he stakes his reputation and has nothing to gain.
  • Psychology · Thorndike's halo effect: in 1920 Edward Thorndike found officers rating soldiers let a good impression on one trait spill into the others. Evans's "we make excuses for people we like" is that effect inside a promotion committee.
  • Philosophy · Epictetus's dichotomy of control: the Stoic Enchiridion opens by dividing things into those up to us and those not, the same cut as controllable inputs. The difference: Epictetus asks for indifference to the second kind; Evans tends the inputs with his eyes on the output, the title.

Ten takeaways

  1. Ethan Evans borrows Amazon's "controllable inputs": pay and title are outputs; impactful work and a network are the inputs you can change daily.
  2. The Magic Loop has four steps: do your job well, ask your manager how to help, do that well, and return to step two.
  3. In his 2025 interview Evans framed the Magic Loop as a deal: I go above and beyond, and my manager makes sure there is a payoff.
  4. Before striking the Magic Loop deal, check that your manager understands the company's promotion process and review cycle.
  5. Evans argues relationships shape a manager's attributions: a liked person's mistake is excused, a disliked person's is taken as character.
  6. Evans's trust formula has three layers: reliability at the rational end, likability at the emotional end, and sacrifice in between.
  7. Evans compresses promotion criteria into "be valuable and be compatible," with each able to compensate for the other.
  8. A promotion case needs a "tweet": because this person did X, we got Y; Evans's own was "without Ethan, no TiVo."
  9. An executive with no stake who speaks for you in the committee outweighs your manager, and Evans advises arranging such a witness in advance.
  10. Evans's promotion advice moved from "patience" in 2023 to "don't be the one who waits" in 2026, with reorgs as a main route up.
