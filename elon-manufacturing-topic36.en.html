Elon Musk Series · No. 3

The factory is the product

Prototypes are easy, production is hard: why he says building the factory is far harder than building the thing, what he thinks a good factory looks like, and where his own output numbers ended up

Topic The factory is the product: manufacturing, cost and iteration speed · Sources 7 interview transcripts (2021–2026), Tesla Master Plan Part Deux (2016), Tesla quarterly updates 2017–2024 · Interviews Starbase tour, parts 1 and 2 (Everyday Astronaut, 2021) · Lex Fridman #252 (2021) · Ron Baron Investment Conference (2022) · Starfactory tour (Everyday Astronaut, 2024) · Peter Diamandis (2026) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

What he claims

When people talk about Musk, they talk about products: a car, a rocket, a robot. In his own telling, though, the main character is often something else, the factory that turns those products out one after another. In the summer of 2021, Tim Dodd had barely mentioned at Starbase how much manufacturing matters when Musk picked up the thread with a judgment he would repeat for years:

Generally, manufacturing is underrated and design is overrated.— Musk · Starbase tour, part 1, 2021 [00:02:44]

This page follows that sentence: why he thinks volume production is so much harder than a prototype, what he thinks a good factory looks like, and why he weighs the speed of iteration above any single design.

1. Prototypes are easy, production is hard

On that tour he gave a heavy ratio. Designing the system that manufactures the Raptor engine, he said, takes ten to a hundred times more effort than designing the engine; next to that, the design work rounds down to zero. People who have never worked in manufacturing, he said, imagine that the eureka moment is the whole job and that production is like a copier. His rebuttal was blunt: if design were the hard part, he could simply ask for a thousand Raptors and get them, and he cannot. He added that the Russians had done hundreds of staged-combustion designs decades ago; Raptor's higher chamber pressure and full-flow cycle are a relatively small step beyond that. The genuinely hard problem is making a Raptor for under a thousand dollars per ton of thrust.

At the end of that year, Lex Fridman asked him which single engineering problem on Starship he would solve perfectly if he could. The thing absorbing most of his time, he said, was not the design of the engine but its production:

I've often said prototypes are easy, production is hard.— Musk · Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [00:07:38]

The reason he gave was complexity: many components, several alloys invented specifically for this engine, and a full-flow staged-combustion cycle in which gas flows feed back on one another, so that changing one thing changes something else.

The idea did not start in 2021. In 2016 he wrote the line this page is named after:

That is why Tesla engineering has transitioned to focus heavily on designing the machine that makes the machine -- turning the factory itself into a product.— Musk · Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

He followed it with a timetable. A first-principles physics analysis of car production suggested a five- to tenfold improvement, reachable by version 3 on a roughly two-year cycle; the first Model 3 factory line was version 0.5, with version 1.0 probably in 2018. The same document also explained, looking back, why the first master plan began with an expensive sports car. Low volume meant a small, simple factory with most things done by hand; without economies of scale anything would be expensive, and at least some buyers would pay a high price for a sports car. In his account, the size of the factory decided the price of the product from the very start. How that timetable played out is checked under "Limits" below.

2. What a good factory looks like: tempo, flow, and tents first

When Dodd returned to Starbase in June 2024, the old tents had given way to a nearly finished building, the Starfactory. Musk said it was built for serialized production of Starship, capable of perhaps a hundred ships a year, with a thousand a year as the long-run goal because a city on Mars will need them. Walking in, he laid out what makes a factory good. People hear "production line" and think of Henry Ford's moving assembly line, and that, he said, is not the point:

What actually matters is linear adjacent flow, as opposed to a production line that moves.— Musk · Starfactory tour, 2024 [00:20:59]

A conveyor belt is secondary. What matters is tempo: every station has roughly the same amount of work, things move from one station to the next, and each station has its own specialization. The new building would have many stations, he said, so the work in progress would sit in plain view and blockages would be obvious, ready to be fixed.

Then why not build the factory first? Dodd supplied the answer himself: in tents you can rearrange stations at will and see what order things actually need to go in. Musk agreed, saying that back then they did not even know what the rocket would look like. Automating at that stage would break his own rule that automation comes last (the five steps themselves are covered in First Principles and the Five-Step Algorithm). Tents are inefficient, but a building only stops wasting money once you know what you are building.

He had a more specific warning about production lines, from 2021. When a line is first set up, nobody knows where things are breaking, so teams add test steps midway to isolate the faults. Once the problems are found, the engineers debugging the line often forget to take those in-process tests out. The test station then becomes the bottleneck on cycle time, and it produces false results, rejecting good parts. His rule: if parts pass end-of-line testing at a high rate, move almost all testing to the end of the line and keep perhaps one or two steps in the middle.

Design has to bend to manufacturing too. In 2021 he showed off Starship's new nose cone: the old one was pieced together from three rows of stamped sections, the new one from two rows of stretch-formed panels, made by pulling a whole sheet of steel over a giant mandrel, far larger than any stamping press could hold, and much smoother. In 2024 he said the next-generation Raptor would replace many bolted flanges with welded joints and build cooling circuits into the parts themselves, because bolts, flanges and seals are the worst headache, especially when hot. The price is that a broken part has to be cut open.

3. Iteration speed: an image that sharpens with each pass

The building is made for volume, yet Starship itself keeps changing. Asked whether version 1 was settled, Musk said they were probably on "version 1.67" by now, and offered an analogy:

One way to look at technology is to look at it as, like, you're rendering an image in successive levels of detail.— Musk · Starfactory tour, 2024 [00:03:18]

The first pass is blurry and things are out of place; the next pass is a little sharper; pass after pass, it becomes refined enough to work. The payload for all that year's flights, he said, was data. Barely two months separated two test flights, yet the hardware changes across ship, booster and engines ran into the thousands, most of them small, though a small change can be a big deal. The early hop tests, he said, looked to outsiders like a question of whether the vehicle landed; the team was really learning how to work with stainless steel as the primary structure and methane as the fuel (how steel was chosen is covered in First Principles and the Five-Step Algorithm).

Once the pace picks up, how does output climb? Talking to Dwarkesh Patel in 2026 about building Optimus, he said manufacturing output per unit time always follows an S-curve: agonizingly slow at first, then exponential, then linear, then flattening until it approaches some number [01:26:29]. He expects Optimus to ramp more slowly than other products, because its actuators and electronics are all designed from scratch; nothing can be picked out of a catalog, and there is no existing supply chain.

He even credits his ability to work across industries to manufacturing. In early 2026, when Peter Diamandis visited him at the Austin Gigafactory and asked how he thinks about his range, he said that what is ordinary in one field becomes a superpower in another:

If you apply automotive manufacturing technology to satellites and rockets, it's like being Superman.— Musk · Peter Diamandis, 2026 [01:52:47]

Bring rocket materials science into cars and you are Superman again. Rockets have always been built a few at a time, which is why car-style volume production looks so powerful there. In 2024 he said SpaceX was making nearly two hundred Falcon 9 upper stages a year, a number also checked under "Limits."

How the claims interlock

The three claims form one chain, and it starts with cost. He told Lex that Starship's fundamental optimization is cost per ton to orbit, and ultimately per ton to the surface of Mars; at Starbase he said Raptor's real problem is getting below a thousand dollars per ton of thrust. Costs like that exist only in volume production, so beyond the product you have to design a second machine, the one that makes the machine. An efficient factory needs even tempo and continuous flow, and even tempo assumes a stable design, which his products never have. His answer is to go in stages: work out the sequence in tents, then put up the building, and automate last. Extend the chain further and you reach Optimus building Optimus, robots filling the labor gap, which is the subject of Four Master Plans.

The weakest link is the tail of the curve. He says output eventually approaches some number, but he does not say what sets that number. Tesla offers a ready example. In November 2022, at Ron Baron's conference, he said the target he had stated publicly was 40,000 cars a week by the end of that year [00:48:30]. Tesla's reports show 439,701 vehicles produced that fourth quarter, about 34,000 a week; the best quarter, Q4 2023, annualized to nearly 2.0 million, about 38,000 a week. Full-year output then went to 1.77 million in 2024 and 1.65 million in 2025, not higher. The numbers alone cannot tell whether the factories hit their ceiling or demand did. "The factory is the product" assumes that if you build the factory well, output follows; once the curve flattens, the constraint may no longer be the factory.

Limits

"The first Model 3 factory machine should be thought of as version 0.5, with version 1.0 probably in 2018," with a five- to tenfold improvement by version 3. — Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

Supported, but overstated

Tesla's Q2 2017 update set a 5,000-per-week Model 3 run rate for the end of 2017; it was reached at the end of June 2018. The Q2 2018 update conceded that the main assembly line relied on hundreds of robotic lifters and was too dense, with too much downtime. The rescue was a second line built quickly in a tent, with processes like the older Model S/X line and similar quality and cost, which the letter credited to the Model 3's designed simplicity. The factory did keep improving: Fremont built nearly 560,000 vehicles in 2023, far above the previous owner's record. But no public metric shows a five- to tenfold gain, and that rescue came from design.

SpaceX is making nearly two hundred Falcon 9 upper stages this year, probably over two hundred next year. — Starfactory tour, 2024 [00:01:32]

Supported, but overstated

An upper stage is thrown away on every flight, so the launch count is the most direct proxy for output. Public launch records show 134 Falcon launches in 2024 (132 Falcon 9, 2 Falcon Heavy) and 165 Falcon 9 launches in 2025. No one has flown at that cadence before; in 2025 an upper stage was used up roughly every two days. Still, neither year reached his figures, falling about a third and about a fifth short.

Crossovers

  • Operations management · The Toyota Production System: Taiichi Ohno's system is built on takt time and one-piece flow: pace every station to customer demand, equalize the work, keep inventory in process low. Musk's "linear adjacent flow" is nearly the same idea. Toyota also practices jidoka, often rendered "automation with a human touch": a machine that detects an abnormality stops and hands the problem to a person rather than trusting full automation, which fits the Model 3 lesson. Where they differ: Toyota assumes stable standard work improved in small steps, while Starship changes in thousands of places every two months, so his factory has to hold a steady tempo and constant redesign at once.
  • Aeronautical engineering · Wright's learning curve: In 1936 Theodore Wright, studying aircraft production, found that each doubling of cumulative output cut the labor per plane by a roughly constant percentage. Musk's claim that volume pushes cost toward raw-material value rests on this kind of regularity. The difference is that Wright's curve ties cost to how many you have built, experience earned plane by plane, while Musk believes that deriving the ideal factory from physics can skip part of that road.
  • Quality management · Deming's third point: The third of W. Edwards Deming's fourteen points is to cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality and build quality into the product in the first place. Musk's call to strip out in-process tests points the same way, but Deming wanted processes stable enough not to need mass inspection at all, not merely inspection moved to the end of the line.
  • Theory of computation · Von Neumann's self-reproducing machines: John von Neumann imagined a universal constructor that could read a description and build any machine, including itself; his lectures were published in 1966. One step beyond the machine that makes the machine is Musk's Optimus building Optimus. But von Neumann's model has unlimited raw material, while Musk himself concedes that the real bottlenecks are often ore, refining and supply chains.

Ten takeaways

  1. Musk says manufacturing is underrated and design overrated, claiming Raptor's production system took ten to a hundred times more effort than the engine design.
  2. Musk told Lex Fridman in 2021 that prototypes are easy and production is hard; engine production, not engine design, took most of his time.
  3. Tesla's 2016 Master Plan Part Deux called for designing "the machine that makes the machine," treating the factory itself as a product.
  4. Master Plan Part Deux predicted a five- to tenfold gain by factory version 3 on two-year cycles, calling the first Model 3 line version 0.5.
  5. Musk says the key to a good factory is stations in a linear, evenly paced flow; whether the line physically moves is secondary.
  6. SpaceX built early Starships in tents to learn the sequence of work, then put up the Starfactory building, and automated last.
  7. Musk argues in-process tests should be removed once a line is debugged, since they slow cycle time and reject good parts; test at the end of line.
  8. Musk compares technology development to rendering an image in passes; Starship saw thousands of hardware changes between test flights whose payload was data.
  9. Musk says manufacturing output follows an S-curve, slow then fast then flat, and Optimus will ramp slowly because it has no existing supply chain.
  10. Toyota's takt time and one-piece flow resemble Musk's linear adjacent flow, but Toyota builds on stable standard work rather than constant redesign.
