Lex Fridman Podcast · 2022

People as predictable systems, and the same cold eye turned on a war

Andrew Bustamante × Lex Fridman · “Andrew Bustamante: CIA Spy | Lex Fridman Podcast #310”

Show Lex Fridman Podcast · #310 · 2022-08-08 · Length 233 min · Guest Andrew Bustamante · Host Lex Fridman · Original YouTube

The moment

About an hour and fifteen minutes in, Lex Fridman lays out his personal creed. He tries to be the same person in every setting and not to lie, so people can trust him; he has just come back from the front in Ukraine, where a lot of heavily guarded doors opened for him. He believes sincerity transmits itself in subtle ways that can’t be faked.

Across the table, former CIA officer Andrew Bustamante doesn’t nod along. He points out a bug:

So one of the flaws in your argument is that you keep referring to how you feel.— Andrew Bustamante · [01:18:01]

Trusting your own feelings, he explains, is a predictable trait everyone shares, and what the CIA teaches is how to use it to build relationships “artificially,” so the other person trusts you and is even willing to hand you their own government’s secrets. Lex is describing a virtue; Bustamante sees an entry point. That exchange is close to a miniature of the whole episode.

Who’s talking, and when

Bustamante’s path was something of an accident. He graduated from the Air Force Academy, by his own account barely, with a 2.4 GPA, then became an ICBM commander in Montana responsible for two hundred nuclear weapons. He says he hated shaving, short hair and shined shoes. Leaving the Air Force in 2007, he was filling out an online Peace Corps application when a blinking red page asked whether “other government positions” could contact him first. He went on to spend seven years as a covert CIA officer; his wife was also undercover, and the two worked missions abroad as a tandem couple. After their son was born, the agency wouldn’t adjust anything for two operators with a child, and in 2014 they both left.

Lex Fridman is an MIT-trained AI researcher whose interviews move slowly, circle back to love and death, and tend to steer talk of power toward kindness. This one was recorded in August 2022, six months into Russia’s full-scale war, just after Lex returned from Ukraine. The conversation runs from the President’s Daily Brief through disguise, polygraphs, the NSA, Epstein and UFOs. Three threads actually carry an argument.

The episode, worked through

1. People are predictable, so people can be led

Bustamante keeps coming back to one sentence: human nature is predictable. When the CIA taught him that, he says, it simply made sense. Every tool in his kit rests on it.

The first is the baseline. He doesn’t believe micro-expressions or eye direction alone can catch a lie and calls that “baloney.” His method is to learn what a person is normally like through small talk, then ask pointed questions and watch where they drift from normal. He explains the polygraph the same way: it doesn’t detect lies, it detects sensitivity to certain questions, and that sensitivity can come from dishonesty or simply from vulnerability.

The second is control of a conversation. Lex says that as host he’s the quiet one listening. Bustamante corrects him: the person asking the questions sets the direction.

Every time I answer a question, I am creating a pattern of obedience to you, which subliminally, subconsciously makes me that much more apt to answer your questions.— Andrew Bustamante · [01:25:30]

His own recruitment had the same shape. A call from an unfamiliar 703 number, a FedEx the next day with a plane ticket, hotel and rental car, then one interview after another. No single step was big, so he kept taking them.

I just kept doing the next thing, which I found out later on is a form of control.— Andrew Bustamante · [01:34:46]

Lex isn’t convinced. People whose job is reading people will smell a fake, he says, so how does an artificial relationship get past them? Bustamante’s answer is crude but precise: you can smell of anything, as long as it isn’t that.

If you come in smelling like goat shit, you still smell like shit, but you don’t smell like bullshit. So they don’t count you out right away.— Andrew Bustamante · [01:21:11]

People always sort the people they meet into some bin, he says; the officer’s job is to choose the bin, like his favorite disguise, the “dilapidated brown guy” nobody ever takes for a spy.

But the thread doesn’t end on manipulation. Asked what surprised him about human nature, Bustamante says predictability wasn’t the surprise. The surprise was finding the same painful longing to be with other people everywhere he went, across every class. That, he adds, is why taking connection away is the strongest punishment we have: prison, sanctions, sending a child to their room. The “spy trick” he leaves listeners with at the end is to move from your own perception to the other person’s perspective. The same model of people can be used to manipulate them or to understand them.

2. Intelligence is a product with a customer

Bustamante talks about the CIA in business terms. The president is the only customer; the director is the CEO. The President’s Daily Brief runs fifty to a hundred and twenty-five pages, and what goes on page one isn’t necessarily the most important thing in the world but whatever the president asked to see the day before. The director is a presidential appointee, which he calls “a massive flaw in the system”: the pick rests on the president’s trust, not on merit.

By the same logic he reads the Trump years as a “disinterested customer”: a president who didn’t want the CIA’s product and went to private intelligence firms instead. What disciplines private intelligence, in his view, isn’t law but the market. A better product finds a buyer.

A product with a customer has a reputation to manage. On runs through Bangkok with General Petraeus, Bustamante asked how he kept alive the troops’ myth of his superhuman fitness.

Myths are born not from somebody orchestrating the myth but from the source of the myth simply being secretive.— Andrew Bustamante, relaying Petraeus · [01:01:53]

That, he says, is exactly what the CIA does: answer no rumor and let the myth grow. Lex asks why not publicize foiled attacks. Bustamante invokes loss aversion: if the Austin police told you every day how many crimes they had stopped, you wouldn’t feel safer, you’d wonder how dangerous the place must be. He defends the NSA’s bulk phone-metadata program with the same logic: the proof of its successes is classified, so the public will never see it. Whether that can be tested is taken up under “Claims, checked.”

3. What the cold eye sees, and where it’s blind

Bustamante stresses that he doesn’t offer opinions, only assessments, made through a “cold, fact-based lens.” On Ukraine that lens produced a hard call: Russia is winning. Putin had succeeded with the same playbook in Georgia in 2008 and Crimea in 2014, and has now shifted to a third approach; you don’t need Kyiv or hearts and minds, only resources in the east and the coast in the south. Lex, who had just talked with a couple hundred Ukrainians, including on the Kherson front, says morale there is extremely high. Bustamante’s reply:

Autocracies don’t need to win hearts and minds. That’s a staunchly democratic point of view.— Andrew Bustamante · [00:22:33]

He predicted Russia would take the whole southern coast from Mariupol to Odesa before that fall, and put Ukraine’s odds of losing at 60 to 70 percent. Three weeks after the episode aired, Ukraine launched the Kherson counteroffensive and retook Kherson city in November; Odesa never fell. The point here isn’t scoring the forecast but seeing its shape: he treats war as a contest of economics and resources, and morale, aid and attention, the things Lex raises, all get weighted at zero.

On conspiracy theories the same lens works well. On the CIA killing JFK, he says he has seen no evidence; on 9/11 as an inside job, he answers with “never ascribe to conspiracy what can be explained by incompetence,” Hanlon’s razor, which he calls a working rule for intelligence professionals. He also lays out how a conspiracy grows: a true fact, then a gap in information, which people fill with an idea, and then further reasoning built on that idea, each step farther from the fact.

Yet on Epstein he says it’s “a hundred percent” certain some intelligence service was involved. His reason: no service could fail to approach a man with that kind of network. That is reasoning from motive, not from evidence. He does grant that building such a person from scratch is very unlikely, and that he was more likely approached after his rise. The cold eye is best at using incentives to take other people’s stories apart; its blind spot is that it sometimes uses incentives to fill in a story of its own.

Claims, checked

“There is no support other than the Lend-Lease Act, which is putting Ukraine in massive debt to the West.” [00:17:33]

Contradicted by the evidence

A June 2022 Congressional Research Service brief shows aid flowing through presidential drawdowns of U.S. stocks, the Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative and Foreign Military Financing, with more than $23 billion appropriated for FY2022, none of it via lend-lease. The Lend-Lease Act expired in 2023 without being used.

“We were safer” when the NSA could collect bulk metadata; “we are now less secure because they can’t do that.” [02:29:21]

Contradicted by the evidence

He says the proof is classified. But the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board reviewed classified material for its 2014 report and found not a single instance involving a threat to the U.S. in which the program made a “concrete difference” in the outcome of a counterterrorism investigation.

The MBTI is “the only one that I count with any significance,” useful for judging a person’s fit for professions and their capacity to handle stress. [01:41:26]

Supported, but overstated

The MBTI is meant to help people understand their preferences. But the Myers & Briggs Foundation states plainly that it is not designed for hiring and does not measure ability, competence or skill. Using it to judge what someone can do, or how well they’ll hold up, goes past the line its own stewards draw.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Anomaly detection: Bustamante’s “baseline” is anomaly detection in ops: you don’t judge a metric as good or bad, only how far it has drifted from this machine’s normal. They share a weakness: an alert says “abnormal,” not “why,” and a racing heart may mean a lie or just fear. The difference is that a machine’s baseline takes a few weeks of data, while a person’s takes coffee after two-hour coffee.
  • Social psychology · Foot-in-the-door: Freedman and Fraser’s 1966 experiments found that people who had agreed to a small request were more likely to agree to a larger one. “Just do the next thing” and “answering builds obedience” are the same mechanism. The difference: in the experiments the requests came from strangers; Bustamante describes an institution deliberately designing the whole staircase.
  • AI · Sycophancy: Lex asks whether Putin only hears what he wants to hear, and Bustamante describes the president deciding what goes on page one of the brief. It is the same structure as language models trained on human preferences learning to flatter users: the customer’s approval is the reward signal, and the product learns to say what the customer likes to hear.
  • Buddhism · Taṇhā (craving): Bustamante praises a moment from Joe Rogan, who said he turned skeptical on UFOs at the moment he realized how badly he wanted them to be real. Buddhism puts craving at the root of suffering, and insight practice trains exactly this noticing of wanting. Alike in seeing how desire bends judgment; unlike in purpose: Bustamante wants an informational edge, Buddhism wants letting go.

Ten takeaways

  1. Andrew Bustamante argues that trusting one’s own feelings is a predictable human trait, and that the CIA uses it to build “artificial relationships.”
  2. Bustamante says catching a lie starts with a person’s baseline and watching which questions push them off it; micro-expressions or eye direction alone are unreliable.
  3. In Bustamante’s account, a polygraph measures sensitivity to particular questions rather than lies, and that sensitivity can simply reflect vulnerability.
  4. Bustamante holds that the person asking questions controls a conversation, and each answer builds a little more habit of obedience.
  5. Bustamante’s CIA recruitment was a chain of small steps, and only later did he realize that “just doing the next thing” is itself a form of control.
  6. Bustamante says his biggest surprise worldwide was a universal longing for company, which is why cutting people off is the strongest punishment.
  7. Bustamante describes the CIA as a firm with one customer: the president decides what goes on page one of the brief and appoints the director.
  8. Petraeus told Bustamante that myths aren’t orchestrated but grow when their source stays silent, and Bustamante says the CIA treats rumors the same way.
  9. Bustamante uses Hanlon’s razor against 9/11 conspiracy theories, yet reasons from motive alone to call intelligence involvement with Epstein “a hundred percent” certain.
  10. Bustamante’s “baseline” mirrors anomaly detection in operations: both can flag a departure from normal but cannot explain its cause.
