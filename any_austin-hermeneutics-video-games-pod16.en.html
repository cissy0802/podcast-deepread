Conversations with Tyler · 2025

The power lines are a pretext; the lesson is how to look

any_austin × Tyler Cowen · "@any_austin on the Hermeneutics of Video Games"

Show Conversations with Tyler · released 2025-06-11 · Length 67 min · Guest any_austin (Austin, video-game YouTuber) · Host Tyler Cowen · Video YouTube

The moment

Midway through, Tyler Cowen asks his guest for his favorite infrastructure. Cowen's own answer is bridges, ports and harbors. Austin agrees about bridges, then swerves: what really gets him is the stuff we don't see, like sewage, and nuclear power plants. The cooling towers, he says, are so good-looking that we should build them purely for the aesthetics. No power, no turbines, just towers that vent steam for no reason. [00:31:20]

It's a joke, but they immediately start arguing inside it. One of them points out that the towers mean something because we know what nuclear power is, and we're scared of it. Austin objects: raise a baby in a box until eighteen, bring them out, and they would love the towers with no historical context at all. Cowen's reply: they would love it less than you. That exchange is the skeleton of the whole episode. How much of a thing's value lives in the thing, and how much in what you know about it?

Who's talking, and when

Austin goes by any_austin on YouTube, and his videos dwell on the corners of games nobody looks at: whether Liberty City's power lines in Grand Theft Auto connect to anything, whether the residents of a game world can find a job, what the background pedestrians are actually doing all day. Cowen introduces him as the best in the world at the hermeneutics of infrastructure in video games, and also the only person doing it. Austin happily takes that: being the best at something nobody else does is, he thinks, exactly where you want to be in the modern world. He is from Minneapolis and now lives in New Orleans.

Tyler Cowen is an economist at George Mason University who has written the blog Marginal Revolution for over two decades. His show's motto is "the conversation I want to have": short questions, quick jumps, and yes, near the end he asks what the best food in New Orleans is. He also admits he doesn't play video games at all, beyond some arcade games when he was young. So this becomes an economist carrying intuitions from art history and sports into a conversation with someone who thinks through games.

The episode came out in June 2025 and was recorded live, in person. Cowen mentions he has done nine interviews in nine days, and explains why he is accelerating: he is putting himself on the record for the AIs.

you will be more real to them and they will like you more and take better care of you.— Tyler Cowen · [01:03:48]

Glossary

  • Hermeneutics: the study of how we interpret a text or a work. It began as a method for reading scripture and later stretched to anything that needs interpreting. The episode title uses it half as a joke and half in earnest.
  • AAA games: big-budget titles from large studios, the gaming equivalent of Hollywood blockbusters.

The episode, in depth

1. The power lines are a pretext; looking is the point

Cowen asks whether Austin notices strange details when he walks around a real city. Austin says his brain is actually foggy most of the time; he is somehow in a fog while noticing the fog. He does admit he sees far more utility poles since making the power-line video. When he wonders where the habit came from, he lands on childhood. His family allowed twenty minutes of games a day, and games back then had no saves, so every session restarted from the beginning. He ran through the same few opening locations over and over and had to make his own fun in that tiny space. Maybe, he guesses, that is where he learned to look at a space in detail.

What the looking turned up is a little surprising. When we judge whether a utility pole in a game is realistic, we compare it with an ideal pole, so a mis-hung wire or an odd fitting reads as the game getting it wrong. Austin, after reading papers and talking to people, found the opposite.

And so the infrastructure you look at in real life is usually wrong.— Austin · [00:08:31]

The reason is coordination. Getting a paved road with a utility pole to your house means the contractor who dug the hole has to talk to someone, who has to finish something else first and call someone else. Cities and games are both assembled that way, so both carry the same burrs.

But Austin says the poles and rivers are a veneer. He complains that video-game commentary has a single value system: everyone talks about frame rates and settings, every review says it is like this game plus that game, and almost nobody says what a game made them feel, or what is literally on the screen. He wants people to engage with a game the way they would with a sculpture.

But that's all second order. First order is learn to look at things and use your brain and see what you're seeing— Austin · [00:51:10]

He calls it a kind of mindfulness exercise. He once built a whole video arguing for the hydrology of Morrowind, then ended by telling viewers that with a few different interpretations, an equally good argument exists for the exact opposite. Getting it right was never the point. The trouble is that imitators copied the veneer: there are now channels everywhere asking what housing costs in a game or how its economy works. Austin says his next move is to shift the weight back from what to look at toward how to look.

2. How much of a work's value lives in its history?

The second thread is where the two genuinely disagree, and it starts with AI images. Austin says he has nearly lost interest in AI image tools, because they got less interesting as they got more competent.

all of the AI image generating things have trended towards competency and cleanliness— Austin · [00:17:12]

That, he says, is the same obsession games had for twenty years: chase the most realistic, most perfect thing, which has never been where the frontier of interesting art is. The strange halfway states in AI output were briefly fun, but the whole goal is to remove them so the tools can replace the fifty-dollar sketch. Cowen offers the economist's fix: once tokens are cheap enough, have the AI run a million storylines and judge them itself, sending you the one you'll most like, so only one in a million has to be good. Austin grants that on a long enough timescale this will work, but says good artists are fast, and their first idea is often the coolest one. If he can think of something faster than the AI can, the AI isn't useful to him.

Then Cowen states his own position. A new movie made partly or wholly by AI wouldn't interest him any less; it's all artifice anyway. Old works are different, because their role in history matters. On Michelangelo's David:

If I didn't understand Christianity, Florence, the Renaissance, I think it would lose more than half its value.— Tyler Cowen · [00:23:06]

He even sorts the arts. For abstract music, history is maybe ten percent of the value; for opera it matters again, and Don Giovanni is funnier once you know the Enlightenment and Casanova. Austin finds the sorting odd. He sets a cup down gently and says: the cup is the art, this is me, and the value is somewhere in between. Knowing where the cup came from is fun, but it lives in a separate part of the brain and doesn't change what it means to him. Cowen admits he appreciates Chinese art considerably less, simply because he knows less about it. Austin proposes a test: put Cowen among a set of brand-new AI-generated sculptures; do they mean nothing? Much less, Cowen says.

Austin's strongest point comes last. We can't actually tell authentic art from inauthentic art. Plenty of albums celebrated as heartfelt turn out, when you read the history, to have been made under duress by a band that didn't care. If we can't detect sincerity in human work, we won't be good at telling AI work from human work either. At which point he loses his train of thought entirely, and the two of them laugh that this is a live glitch.

3. Games aren't getting better, and they aren't dying

Cowen asks how much better games will get over the next twenty years. Not at all, says Austin. Every medium has a nascent period of figuring out its basic tools, he argues, and once that resolves, art doesn't get qualitatively better. He recently played most of the old Atari games and they are mostly bad; but since Nintendo revived the industry in the 1980s, games have been consistently good. Red Dead Redemption 2 feels different in tone from The Legend of Zelda on the NES, but not much more impactful.

I totally reject the notion that video games used to be better. I totally reject the notion that they used to be worse.— Austin · [00:34:20]

Cowen relays what others tell him: canonical games are rarer now, just as rock music changed enormously between 1966 and 1968 but sounds much the same across a decade today. Austin pushes back: how old does a landmark have to be before it doesn't count? Minecraft is from 2011, Breath of the Wild from 2017, and both already feel long ago. As for "gaming is in decline", he says the falling lines on the charts come almost entirely from AAA games, a bubble that inflated for ten or fifteen years and is now deflating. The deeper problem is the category itself.

calling those video games and calling Zelda video games would be kind of like grouping football in with theater.— Austin · [00:41:01]

"Those" are the long-running competitive games like Fortnite and Counter-Strike. He admits he used to repeat that games are bigger than movies combined, and now thinks a statistic quoting only revenue, never player counts, was itself a red flag. Interactivity requires you to learn to physically do something, so the barrier to entry is higher, and the serious audience for games looks more like the audience for books than for television.

Both end up agreeing that commentary about games may now be bigger than the games, like the person who spent six hours on NBA podcasts in a week without watching a single game. Near the end Austin turns the questions around: do people who make great work also tend to be interesting to talk to? Cowen answers with selection. Someone who isn't famous only gets on the show if they have something, which is why the best interviews are often with less public people; just as in the NBA, taller players are worse at free throws, because being tall gets you into the league anyway.

Fact check

Austin says roughly 90% of his audience comes through the YouTube algorithm, with sharing and word of mouth happening on the margins. [00:02:27]

Supported

The 90% is his own estimate for his channel and can't be checked, but the direction has platform data behind it. In 2018 YouTube's product chief Neal Mohan said more than 70% of watch time sitewide comes from recommendations rather than search.

Austin cites a figure he says came out recently: only 12% of people who call themselves gamers will actually buy a video game this year. [00:38:51]

Insufficient evidence

No public figure like this predates the episode. The closest is a Circana survey of US players released four months later, where 12% is the group buying about one new game a year; only 33% buy fewer than one, so about two-thirds buy at least one a year.

Discussing German nuclear power, the conversation says the shutdown is still underway and the new government is talking about rebooting the plants. [00:32:00]

Contrary to evidence

Germany's last three reactors shut down in April 2023. A month before the episode aired, new economy and energy minister Katherina Reiche said the phase-out "has been completed"; the coalition agreement doesn't mention a return, only a fusion reactor.

Crossing over

  • Distributed systems · Conway's law: Austin's explanation for why real poles look "wrong" (layers of contractors coordinating) is Conway's law: a system's structure mirrors the communication structure of the organization that built it. Microservice boundaries fall on team boundaries; misalignments on a pole fall on the handoffs between crews. The difference: software can be refactored until the org chart disappears from it, while a city's burrs mostly stay, which is why Austin can read backward from them to who had to talk to whom.
  • Buddhism · sati and papañca: Austin's "see what you're seeing" resembles sati, mindfulness: seeing what is there before judging it. The difference is that he immediately builds an elaborate explanation on top, which Buddhist psychology would call papañca, the proliferation of thought over what is perceived.
  • Philosophy · Gadamer's fusion of horizons: Cowen puts value in the work's history, Austin in the space between viewer and work. Gadamer's hermeneutics joins them: understanding happens where the interpreter's horizon meets the work's historical horizon, and neither can be dropped. On his account, each of them is half right.
  • AI · best-of-N with a judge: Cowen's "run a million storylines and let the AI pick" is today's sample-many-then-rank-with-a-reward-model. Austin's objection lands on its bottleneck: the judge's taste sets the ceiling, and preference tuning tends to shrink output diversity.
  • Statistics · Berkson's paradox: Cowen's free-throw example is classic selection bias. Among players already in the NBA, height and skill can look negatively correlated even if they aren't in general. His point that less famous guests often make better interviews has the same structure.

Ten takeaways

  1. YouTuber any_austin makes videos about infrastructure inside video games, such as whether Liberty City's power lines connect to anything.
  2. any_austin traces his habit of studying spaces to childhood, when he was allowed twenty minutes of games a day and games had no saves.
  3. any_austin found that real-world infrastructure is usually "wrong", because even one utility pole involves layers of coordination, and cities and games are both assembled that way.
  4. any_austin says infrastructure is only the veneer; what he wants to teach is how to see what you are looking at and think it through yourself.
  5. any_austin criticizes video-game commentary for a single value system centered on frame rates, settings and "this game plus that game" comparisons rather than feeling.
  6. any_austin thinks AI image tools grew less interesting as they became more competent and clean, echoing gaming's long obsession with realism.
  7. Tyler Cowen holds that more than half the value of an old artwork comes from its history, while any_austin places value in the interaction between viewer and work.
  8. any_austin argues games have been consistently good since Nintendo revived the industry and will not get better, nor were they better before.
  9. any_austin says counting Fortnite-style competitive games and Zelda as the same medium is like grouping football with theater.
  10. Tyler Cowen uses a selection effect to explain why less famous guests often give better interviews, just as tall NBA players are worse at free throws.
