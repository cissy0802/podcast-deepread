Paul Conti: a map of the mind that gets simpler as you climb, and "a pill can't replace understanding"

Paul Conti × Andrew Huberman · "Dr. Paul Conti: How to Understand & Assess Your Mental Health"

Show Huberman Lab Guest Series · mental health series, episode 1 · 2023-09-06 · Length 222 min · Guest Paul Conti · Host Andrew Huberman · Video YouTube

The moment

About three and a half hours in, Huberman tells a story he says he has never shared publicly. As a postdoc, he once reached the stairwell of his building and simply could not make himself climb it. Driving home that day, one thought kept running: none of this matters. He saw a psychiatrist, who prescribed a low dose of a serotonergic antidepressant; he thinks it may have been citalopram.

"…spent that evening staring at my plate of Thai noodles for about two hours. It hit me really hard."— Andrew Huberman · [03:27:20]

He stopped the drug and got out mainly through talk therapy. Conti doesn't go into pharmacology. He asks a plainer question: a man who climbs those stairs every day suddenly can't. Isn't that the thing to understand first?

"The idea that a pill will fix that is like, that's insane."— Paul Conti · [03:30:29]

He follows with a real ER case. A young woman comes in anxious and unable to sleep, goes home with a sleeping pill, comes back, gets a higher dose, comes back again, and is finally labeled drug-seeking. In fact she was being hurt at home and was terrified to go back. The whole three and a half hours build the foundation for that position: understand the person first, then decide how to treat.

Who's talking, and when

Paul Conti trained in medicine at Stanford, became chief resident in psychiatry at Harvard Medical School, and later founded Pacific Premier Group, a practice of psychiatrists and therapists. In 2021 he published a book on trauma, Trauma: The Invisible Epidemic. On the show he mentions growing up in central New Jersey, and says the part of his education that helps him most as a psychiatrist is, of all things, his math minor.

Andrew Huberman is a Stanford neurobiologist whose show is known for protocols: when to get sunlight, how cold the water, how many sets. Here too he keeps asking for "action steps," and every time Conti steers back to the same place: first see your own inner structure clearly. In an ad read Huberman also mentions he has been in weekly therapy for more than thirty years, which began as a condition for staying in high school.

This is the first of a four-part mental health series released in September 2023, after an earlier Conti episode on trauma. A year before, the umbrella review by Joanna Moncrieff's group, which found no support for the idea that low serotonin causes depression, had set off a large public fight. So the passages here about medication and "chemical imbalance" landed at a sensitive moment.

Glossary

  • Defense mechanisms: a psychoanalytic term for automatic reactions that keep painful feelings like fear or shame at bay, usually outside our awareness.
  • Projective identification: expressing an inner emotion so that it spreads to the people around you, who end up carrying the feeling for you.
  • Demoralization: a state of powerlessness, isolation and hopelessness. It is not a diagnosis in the manual and not the same as depression.

The episode, in depth

1. A map that gets simpler as you climb

Huberman opens with the physical-health analogy: blood pressure, heart rate and endurance have norms, so what is the norm for mental health? Conti answers in two words.

"A healthy self approaches life through the lens of agency and gratitude."— Paul Conti · [00:08:31]

Agency and gratitude sit at the top. Below them are empowerment and humility, and below those two pillars he calls the structure of self and the function of self, each with five "cupboards." Structure is the nouns: the huge underwater part of the iceberg is the unconscious, maybe 95 percent of it, he says; above the water is the conscious mind; tendrils reaching up from below to guard it are the defense mechanisms; the "nest" that wraps all three and meets the world is character structure; and growing out of the nest is the self we experience. Function is the verbs: awareness that there is an "I," defenses in action, salience, behavior, and strivings toward the future.

He keeps stressing one feature: the lower you go the more complex it gets, the higher the simpler. Two people's heart and lungs can differ wildly, but when both train well, what they get is endurance. So when someone feels inexplicable dread walking into a familiar office, he doesn't just write an order for cognitive behavioral therapy. He checks the cupboards one by one: a born-anxious temperament, unprocessed trauma, the inner voice saying "this will go badly," a habit of avoidance, or decisions about work that are already shifting.

He is also blunt that this is slow. An "I'm not good enough" repeated thousands of times is a four-lane highway; a new thought is a trail just cut beside it, which has to be walked again and again before the old road grows over. So he has no patience for the phrase "the patient failed therapy":

"I think therapy failed that person."— Paul Conti · [01:02:46]

2. Defense mechanisms: you have to see them before you can change them

The most accessible part of the episode is Conti using himself as the example. Stuck in traffic, he used to feel beleaguered, as if "traffic" were out to thwart him. In his own therapy he realized the only one angry was him; he was throwing his anger outward. That is projection. Coming home and kicking the dog is displacement. When he lost his keys he'd fret out loud, the people around him got tense and helped him search, and his feeling got shared out. That is projective identification. When Huberman offers academia's "trickle-down anxiety," where a stressed lab head piles busywork on students, Conti corrects him: that is closer to projective identification than displacement.

Humor works the same way. Trip, make a joke, and people laugh with you: a healthy defense. Biting sarcasm aimed at someone is, in his view, acting out aggression. Cynicism is a whole worldview, sour grapes: think little of everything and you can never be disappointed.

The best stretch comes from a question Huberman hands over about himself. He never stays long in a bad professional setting, yet in one kind of relationship he walks in again and again, knowing better. Conti won't call it pathology; he calls it humanness. His mathematical instinct: if someone is rational everywhere else and keeps erring in one place, that place is the X on the treasure map, and digging there will turn something up. A person who has been through five nearly identical abusive relationships and is entering a sixth is often trying to rewrite the past by making this one work.

"Trauma doesn't care about the clock or the calendar."— Paul Conti · [01:53:00]

But walking into the sixth also requires denial. His method is to bring these unconscious reactions into awareness one at a time. Once seen, they stop being automatic.

3. Three drives, and his bet on the "generative drive"

In the back half Conti turns to drives. Psychoanalytic tradition has two: aggressive and pleasure. Aggression, he says, isn't only violence; wanting to change the world is part of it. Pleasure isn't only indulgence; it includes relief from pain. But those two can't explain learning for its own sake, or kindness with nothing in it for us, or why humans build more than they destroy. So he adds a third, the generative drive, the push to make things better. He concedes it is contested, and that parts of the field don't accept it.

His test is which drive is in charge. When aggression or pleasure overrides the generative drive, a person slides toward envy. Huberman brings up American Psycho, where the protagonist kills a man over a nicer business card, and Conti goes further: most destruction, traced to its root, is envy. Asked whether school shootings fit, he says yes. That is his view; no evidence is offered on the show. At the other end, drives that are too weak lead to demoralization, like swearing off romance after a few bad breakups, which he describes in psychoanalytic terms as inviting a little death into life.

The thread lands on another Huberman story. As a postdoc, his project collided with a powerful lab. He wanted to switch, and his adviser Ben Barres refused, reminding him how much he loved the questions. He succeeded, but spent those five years running a competition script in his head and ended up exhausted. Conti's reading: you can get the work done on aggression, but not as well, because plotting against rivals is itself a drain.

Claim check

Separating demoralization from depression, Conti says "we know" that with depression "there's a neurochemical imbalance." [02:55:32]

Insufficient evidence

The most-studied version is low serotonin. Moncrieff and colleagues' 2022 umbrella review of 17 studies found no consistent association between serotonin and depression; the largest genetic study (about 115,000 people) found none either. "We know" says far too much.

Conti says insurers often allow 10 sessions of CBT, and if the goal is changing beliefs, "it's a guarantee of failure." [01:01:08]

Contrary to the evidence

Cuijpers and colleagues' 2013 meta-regression of 70 trials of psychotherapy for adult depression (5,403 patients) found only a weak link between number of sessions and effect, which vanished after adjustment; what mattered was frequency, with twice weekly beating once weekly. Those trials measure symptoms, not deep beliefs, but "guaranteed failure" does not hold.

Conti says socioeconomic status and similar factors "are not factors" in whether someone is happy; agency and gratitude are. [00:08:31]

Contrary to the evidence

Killingsworth (2021) analyzed over 1.7 million in-the-moment reports from 33,000 employed US adults: well-being rose steadily with log income, with no plateau above $75,000. His 2023 adversarial collaboration with Kahneman and others found a plateau only among the least happy. Income is at least a factor.

Crossovers

  • Statistical physics · coarse-graining: "Simpler as you climb" resembles going from countless molecular microstates to a few macroscopic quantities like temperature and pressure: however messy the bottom, only a few numbers survive at the top. The difference is that in physics the macro quantities can be derived from the micro. How Conti's ten cupboards roll up into "agency and gratitude" is asserted from clinical experience, not derived, and nobody can independently reproduce that step. The map persuades because it is useful, not because it has been proven.
  • Neuroscience · extinction learning: The overgrown highway is a memorable image. But Mark Bouton's work on fear extinction shows extinction is a new rule learned alongside the old memory, which isn't erased; change the context or wait, and the old response returns. On that evidence the highway is closed, not overgrown.
  • Distributed systems · cascading failure: Trickle-down anxiety and projective identification look like one overloaded service slowing down, its callers retrying, and the pressure spreading along the call chain until the whole cluster slows. The difference: servers don't help you find your keys after catching your anxiety.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: The arrow simile in the Samyutta Nikaya says pain is the first arrow and our reaction to it is a second one we shoot ourselves. Conti's traffic example has exactly this shape: the jam is the first arrow, "traffic is out to get me" the second.
  • History of psychotherapy · Jerome Frank: In Persuasion and Healing, Frank argued that what brings people into therapy is usually not a specific symptom but demoralization. Conti's split between demoralization and depression follows the same path, though he doesn't mention Frank.

Ten takeaways

  1. Paul Conti holds that the endpoint of mental health is living through agency and gratitude, supported by empowerment and humility.
  2. Conti's structure of self has five layers: unconscious, conscious mind, defense mechanisms, character structure and self, like an iceberg wrapped in a nest.
  3. Conti's function of self has five parts: awareness of an "I," defenses in action, salience, behavior and strivings.
  4. Conti's map of the mind is complex at the bottom and simple at the top, the way different training routines all yield endurance.
  5. Conti compares a negative thought repeated thousands of times to a four-lane highway; a new belief is a trail that takes a long time to replace it.
  6. Projection treats your emotion as someone else's, displacement aims it at a safer target, and projective identification makes others carry it for you.
  7. Conti argues that when someone is rational everywhere but keeps erring in one area, that area is the one most worth digging into.
  8. Conti adds a "generative drive" to the aggressive and pleasure drives, and admits the idea is contested in his field.
  9. Conti holds that overpowering drives slide toward envy and weak drives toward demoralization, which is not the same as depression.
  10. After an antidepressant left Andrew Huberman staring at his noodles for two hours, Conti argued that medication should serve understanding, not replace it.
