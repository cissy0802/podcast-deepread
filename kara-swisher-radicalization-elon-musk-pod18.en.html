The Ezra Klein Show · 2025

By the time the courts catch up, it's already done

Kara Swisher × Ezra Klein · Kara Swisher on the Radicalization of Elon Musk | The Ezra Klein Show

Show The Ezra Klein Show · 2025-02-08 · Length 67 min · Guest Kara Swisher · Host Ezra Klein · Episode YouTube

The moment

About forty-six minutes in, Kara Swisher recounts three conversations she had with Musk about AI. The first came around the founding of OpenAI, and it was pure Terminator: AI wakes up one day and turns on us. The next time she saw him, the idea had become more sophisticated:

They're not going to kill us, they're going to treat us like house cats.— Kara Swisher, relaying Musk · [00:46:24]

By the third conversation the metaphor had shifted again. AI was now a highway crew and humanity an anthill in the roadbed; nobody building a road stops to think about the ants. Swisher says she heard him describing his own way of operating, a slide from caring what happens to humanity to not thinking about it at all. Ezra Klein picks it up with a line that works as a gloss on the whole episode:

What you put into the metaphor reveals what you can and not see out of the metaphor.— Ezra Klein · [00:47:41]

Who's talking, and when

Kara Swisher has covered tech for decades and ran the Code conference, where Musk once explained the simulation hypothesis on her stage. She says the two of them texted a lot for years, and she still gets attacked for it by people who think she "made" him. She now hosts On with Kara Swisher and co-hosts Pivot with Scott Galloway. The episode opens with a bit of sparring: she reminds Klein that she is the one who told him to start podcasting.

Ezra Klein is a New York Times columnist whose habit is to build the other side's argument as strong as he can before pressing on it. He tries that twice here: once on behalf of Musk's "cut first" approach, and once by assembling Swisher's scattered observations into a single theory.

The conversation was taped in early February 2025, barely two weeks into Trump's second term. Musk's Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) had moved into the Treasury payment system and the Office of Personnel Management, USAID had been taken over, federal workers had just received an email inviting them to resign, and a deadly air crash over Washington was days old. Nobody yet knew how far the partnership would go. Four months later Musk left the government and fell out with Trump in public.

The episode, unpacked

1. The playbook: a story, the choke points, and the legal time lag

Swisher says Musk runs the same moves wherever he goes. First comes a story, always with him at the center. She remembers the worst stretch at Tesla, when Musk told her privately that if Tesla didn't survive, the human race was doomed. That struck her as what a man in his forties who thinks he's the center of the universe would say. In the story, whoever came before is evil, every crisis is civilizational, and he is the hero sent to fix it. Someone once described him to her as living in Ready Player One, with everyone else an NPC.

The second step is mechanical. Klein points out that DOGE went first for the choke points of information and money, the Treasury payment system and OPM, and fanned out from there. Swisher adds that the people sent in are young, idolize him, and don't much care about the rules: walk in, and wait for someone to sue.

Here Klein makes the hardest argument of the hour. At the top of society, breaking a rule rarely gets you marched out in handcuffs. It gets you sued, and a lawsuit needs standing, process, months or years:

It moves slowly, and so a lot of law following and rule following is just a norm at that level.— Ezra Klein · [00:07:22]

So much of what constrained earlier administrations was never really a constraint: by the time the courts catch up, you have done what you set out to do. Swisher calls it a profound insight and adds a layer. Musk has the money to fight forever, so most opponents decide it isn't worth it. She sums up his temperament with rockets:

Let's blow up 90 rockets cuz the 91st will work, and that's his attitude towards pretty much everything.— Kara Swisher · [00:08:52]

Klein grants that the method produced some amazing rockets. But NASA, Swisher notes, can't blow one up and keep going, and neither can a government. Twitter was the dress rehearsal: the business was damaged, but Musk came away with a megaphone that belonged to him alone. Klein notices that the resignation email sent to federal workers carried the same subject line as the one Musk sent Twitter staff, "Fork in the Road." Swisher calls it a signature: he wants everyone to know it was him.

The thread ends on risk. In his first term, Klein says, Trump always seemed to stand outside the state he ran:

He spoke as if, you know, he was up in the balcony jeering at the opera.— Ezra Klein · [00:59:15]

This time they are tearing the government apart with their own hands, so every future failure will be theirs. Swisher disagrees. They will never own it, she says; they will call it cleaning up their predecessors' mess.

2. How an Obama-era liberal ended up here

Klein finds Musk's turn genuinely puzzling. Peter Thiel was on the right as far back as Stanford, but Musk ran companies devoted to the problems Obama liberals cared about and lived off government contracts and EV subsidies. In 2017 he joined Trump's advisory council and quit when Trump pulled out of the Paris climate accord.

Swisher's explanations are personal. First, COVID: California shut Tesla's factory, and to a man who believes his company matters to the survival of the species, that felt like persecution. In one interview he insisted only a few thousand people would die (she doesn't recall the exact number) and said he had read all the studies. Second, his transgender daughter. Third, the ketamine use reported by the Wall Street Journal. The fourth is the smallest and the most overlooked. In 2021 Biden held an EV summit, left Musk off the list because of the unions, and invited GM's Mary Barra. Swisher called a Biden aide to say they'd made a big mistake. The answer was that it was about the unions and Musk was a big boy. Years later, running into her at a movie premiere, the aide admitted she had been right.

Klein adds Twitter. Musk's tweet count climbed year after year, and he fell into the right-wing subcultures of the platform. He didn't turn into an ordinary Republican; he increasingly sounded like a believer in great replacement theory. Klein floats one more thread, without being sure how much weight it bears: Musk, Thiel and David Sacks all grew up or lived in South Africa and watched its white minority go from ruling the country to fearing for its place in it.

Then Klein pulls it together and asks whether Mars is still the goal. Yes, Swisher says: the belief that civilization is doomed and we have to get off the planet is the one constant since she met him. Klein's synthesis is that what changed is Musk's conviction that DEI and the "woke mind virus" are in the way; Swisher adds that in his view the lesser people are in charge. Klein then offers the strongest defense he can. Musk has said regulations should be gone by default and added back if it turns out we missed one. Institutions really are hard to reform, so could this be the only way? Swisher rejects it flatly. That logic requires sacrificing people, and they don't care about those people:

They don't have a theory of building, they have a theory of destruction.— Kara Swisher · [00:49:11]

3. What the Silicon Valley CEOs wanted back

Klein reads the Twitter takeover as something bigger than cost cutting: a CEO reasserting control over a workforce that had grown too willing to talk back. Swisher says the companies brought it on themselves. Google let employees question executives every Friday, Facebook had Zuckerberg's Q&As, and Slack and Teams let workers organize their voice without a union. Indulge children long enough, she says, and they talk back. When Musk swung the axe, every boss in the Valley wanted to be him.

Bound up with control is a story about manhood. When Musk and Zuckerberg talked about a cage fight, Marc Andreessen wrote that it was great, since we had lost the masculine virtues of the Greeks. Klein sees in this a narrative running through the right's intellectual subculture: the world has been feminized, aggression and daring and decisiveness are fading, and someone needs to correct course.

Going one level deeper, Klein argues that what Musk and Trump share most is a willingness to be truly hated, which most CEOs can't stomach. Swisher pushes back: they care enormously what people think, and that caring is what fuels the rage. Klein gives half a step, then offers his definition of radicalization:

At a certain point you lose the belief that these people are even friends you still want to have, and that's, I think, what real radicalization is.— Ezra Klein · [00:37:38]

The thread lands on a contrarian forecast. Klein asks whether there's a countercurrent in the Valley. Swisher says she never thought of it as liberal in the first place:

I have never thought Silicon Valley was liberal. I thought they were utilitarian, I guess.— Kara Swisher · [01:03:25]

The loudest voices are a small gang around Musk and Sacks; everyone else is paying the vig. If Trump tanks the market, they will switch sides instantly, because what they follow is the market.

Fact check

Klein: Tesla was saved by an Obama loan guarantee. [00:11:44]

Supported, but overstated

The Energy Department did lend Tesla $465 million in 2010 to build its Model S plant and a battery and powertrain facility. But it was a direct loan, not a guarantee, and Tesla repaid it in 2013, nine years early. It helped build the Model S; "saved" is a stretch.

Klein: after the acquisition, Twitter's advertising collapsed. [00:47:41]

Supported

The most direct evidence is Musk himself. In July 2023 he posted that the company was still cash-flow negative because of a roughly 50% drop in ad revenue plus a heavy debt load. He didn't say what period the drop was measured against.

Swisher: Tesla's stock may be rising, but sales are falling because the cars aren't as good. [00:49:11]

Supported, but overstated

Tesla delivered about 1.79 million vehicles in 2024, 1.1% fewer than the year before and its first annual decline. But the dip was small, and Reuters listed several causes, including BYD's competition, shrinking European subsidies and a US shift to hybrids. An aging lineup was only one of them.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · optimistic concurrency: Klein's legal time lag is optimistic concurrency control. Transactions write without locks and check for conflicts only at commit, rolling back if they collide. Make conflict detection slow enough and writing first almost always wins. The difference is that a database rollback is free, while fired experts and dismantled agencies can't be rolled back. In this system the cost of optimism lands entirely on someone else.
  • Complexity · Chesterton's fence: G.K. Chesterton advised that if you find a fence across a road and can't see its use, don't tear it down until you know why it was put there. Musk's "default gone, add it back later" is the exact inverse. In complex systems a component's purpose often shows only when it fails, and some failures can't be added back.
  • AI alignment · indifference, not malice: Musk's slide from Terminator to anthill retraces a path in AI-safety thinking. Yudkowsky wrote in 2008 that the AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made of atoms it can use for something else. The danger is indifference, not hostility. Swisher's move is to turn the metaphor back on the person using it.
  • Psychology · variable-ratio reinforcement: Both speakers mention the thrill small accounts feel when Musk replies, and his manic late-night posting. Skinner found that rewards of unpredictable timing and size are the hardest behaviors to extinguish, the design principle of the slot machine. A social platform is a slot machine for the people posting, and Musk eventually bought the machine.

Ten takeaways

  1. Kara Swisher says Musk opens every campaign with a story: a civilizational crisis, evil predecessors, and himself as the hero.
  2. DOGE entered the federal government through the choke points of information and money, the Treasury payment system and OPM, and fanned out from there.
  3. Ezra Klein argues that at the top of society breaking rules gets you sued rather than arrested, and the courts are slow enough that law-following becomes a norm.
  4. Kara Swisher sums up Musk's temperament as "blow up 90 rockets"; trial and error worked at SpaceX, but NASA and the government can't afford it.
  5. Ezra Klein reads the Twitter purchase as a dress rehearsal: the business was damaged, but Musk gained a global megaphone of his own.
  6. Kara Swisher's personal causes for Musk's turn: Tesla's COVID shutdown, his transgender daughter, ketamine, and being left out of Biden's EV summit.
  7. Ezra Klein adds Twitter's subcultures, great replacement theory, and the South African background shared by Musk, Thiel and Sacks.
  8. Kara Swisher thinks Mars is still Musk's goal; what changed is that he now sees DEI and the "woke mind virus" as obstacles.
  9. Ezra Klein sees the real meaning of the Twitter layoffs as CEOs taking control back from employees who had learned to talk back.
  10. Optimistic concurrency control mirrors Ezra Klein's legal time lag: act first, check later, except that a dismantled agency can't be rolled back.
