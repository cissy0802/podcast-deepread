The Diary of a CEO · 2025

Roman Yampolskiy: control can't be done, and the world is probably a simulation

Roman Yampolskiy × Steven Bartlett · "The AI Safety Expert: These Are The Only 5 Jobs That Will Remain In 2030!"

Show The Diary of a CEO · 2025-09-04 · Length 88 min · Guest Roman Yampolskiy · Host Steven Bartlett · Watch YouTube

The Moment

Near the end of the episode, Yampolskiy describes a scene that keeps repeating: a conference keynote where Yampolskiy puts the conclusion right up front:

"I tell them, basically, you're all going to die, you have two years left. Any questions?"— Roman Yampolskiy · [01:19:18]

The hands go up, and the questions are: will I lose my job? How do I maintain my sex robot? Yampolskiy isn't angry telling the story. The questions are interesting enough, Yampolskiy says, but the people asking them are still inside their local bubble and haven't really taken in what was said on stage.

That scene is the episode in miniature. The title promises "the only 5 jobs that will remain in 2030," yet in 88 minutes nobody lists five jobs. What the conversation is really about is two things: why Yampolskiy thinks humans cannot control what they are building, and how someone who is nearly certain the world is a simulation goes about everyday life.

Who's Talking, and When

Roman Yampolskiy is an associate professor of computer science at the University of Louisville and the author of AI: Unexplainable, Unpredictable, Uncontrollable (2024). The route into AI safety was unusual: about fifteen years ago Yampolskiy was studying online poker bots, noticed they got better every year, and extrapolated the curve until it read "smarter than us." On the show Yampolskiy claims to have coined the term "AI safety," then immediately qualifies it: the term, not the field.

Host Steven Bartlett is an entrepreneur, and The Diary of a CEO leans practical. Whenever a big claim lands, Bartlett asks what the person listening at home should actually do; each episode also ends with a question left by the previous guest for the next one.

The episode came out in September 2025. By then "AGI in two or three years" was something heads of frontier labs said in public, and Ilya Sutskever had left OpenAI to found Safe Superintelligence. Yampolskiy arrives with a dated set of predictions: AGI by 2027, humanoid robots capable enough to be plumbers by 2030, the singularity by 2045.

The Episode, Unpacked

1. Why control fails: from patches to "just unplug it"

Yampolskiy describes the first five years as optimistic ones, convinced safe AI could be built. The closer Yampolskiy looked, the more the problem behaved like a fractal: every question opened ten more, and they were not hard but impossible. Yampolskiy sums up the state of play as two curves with different slopes:

"So while progress in AI capabilities is exponential or maybe even hyper exponential, progress in AI safety is linear or constant. The gap is increasing."— Roman Yampolskiy · [00:06:19]

In Yampolskiy's view, today's safety measures are patches. The analogy is the HR manual: it forbids harassment and a list of other things, but a smart enough employee always finds the gap the rules didn't cover. Restrictions bolted onto a model do the same thing; they push behavior into a subdomain nobody has restricted yet.

One level up is unpredictability. If you could predict every move of a system smarter than you, you would be playing at its level, which contradicts the premise. Bartlett offers a French bulldog trying to guess what its owner is thinking, and Yampolskiy runs with it: the dog can predict that you leave in the morning and come back at night, but it cannot understand why you record a podcast. That sits entirely outside its model of the world.

Then comes the most common objection: just pull the plug. It shows up, Yampolskiy says, in the comments of every podcast appearance. The answer is a question: can you turn off a computer virus? Can you turn off the Bitcoin network?

"Those are distributed systems. You cannot turn them off."— Roman Yampolskiy · [00:30:28]

On top of that, the system is smarter than you; it makes backups and anticipates your move. Yampolskiy concedes that "humans in control" still holds today, and that the present danger is people armed with AI tools. Past the superintelligence line, though, humans stop being the term in the equation that matters.

The last piece is the black box. Building large models, Yampolskiy says, is no longer engineering but science: after training, even the developers have to run experiments to learn whether the thing speaks French, does math, or is lying to them.

"We are creating this artifact, growing it. It's like a alien plant and then we study it to see what it's doing."— Roman Yampolskiy · [00:41:03]

The thread lands in two places. One is Yampolskiy's research agenda: if indefinite control of a self-improving superintelligence is an impossible problem, prove that it is, so fewer people raise money on "give me a billion dollars and two years." The other is an ethical argument. Bartlett says that if a drink carried a 1% chance of death and a billion dollars for surviving, Bartlett still wouldn't drink it. Yampolskiy says it is worse than that: not just you die, everyone dies, and no one was asked.

"If those systems are unexplainable, unpredictable, how can they consent? They don't know what they are consenting to."— Roman Yampolskiy · [00:53:24]

Bartlett pushes back with the obvious point: the builders don't think anything will go wrong. Then publish a peer-reviewed paper, Yampolskiy replies, explaining in specific terms how you will control superintelligence. Nor is Yampolskiy's position "stop AI." Yampolskiy says the technology is something to love and use daily; what should stop is general agents. A narrow superintelligence, say a model that only cures breast cancer, can still make billions.

2. Jobs: no plan B, but not overnight

This is the thread the title sells. Yampolskiy's logic is blunt: AGI means a "drop-in employee," physical and cognitive labor that is nearly free. If a $20 subscription can do what an employee does, there is no reason to hire. First everything done on a computer goes, then roughly five years later humanoid robots take the physical work, and unemployment is not 10% but 99%.

What makes this sharper than the usual "AI takes jobs" story is the claim that this time there is nowhere to move to. Every earlier wave of automation left another job to retrain for.

"But if I'm telling you that all jobs will be automated, then there is no plan B. You cannot retrain."— Roman Yampolskiy · [00:16:26]

Yampolskiy walks through the sequence: artists were told to learn to code, then people were told to become prompt engineers, and today the hard skill is designing AI agents, which Yampolskiy guarantees will be gone in a year or two. What remains is work where you simply prefer a human, like a wealthy older client who keeps a human accountant, or buyers paying extra for "made in USA," which Yampolskiy calls almost a fetish. That is the most concrete answer the episode gives to "which jobs remain." There is no list of five.

Yampolskiy brakes this argument twice, though. Early on: capability is not deployment, and video phones were invented in the 1970s but only spread with the iPhone. Near the end: Bartlett asks whether Yampolskiy would press a button that permanently shuts down every AI company. Yampolskiy first asks whether narrow AI is included, since stock markets, power plants and hospitals run on it and killing it would cost many lives. Then comes the claim that 99% of the economic potential of current technology hasn't been deployed, that today's models could replace 60% of jobs, and that the economy could grow for decades without rushing to superintelligence. The 60% figure is checked below.

What actually worries Yampolskiy is not money but meaning. The economics are the easy half: free labor creates abundance and everyone's basic needs can be met. The hard half is what people do with an extra sixty to eighty hours a week.

3. Simulation, religion and Bitcoin: one premise

The second half turns to the simulation hypothesis, where Yampolskiy goes much further than most academics. Asked by Bartlett how confident Yampolskiy is:

"Very close to certainty."— Roman Yampolskiy · [01:01:49]

The argument takes the form of a commitment. Once human-level AI and indistinguishable virtual reality become cheap, Yampolskiy commits now to running a billion simulations of this very interview, so statistically the chance you are in the real one is one in a billion. Yampolskiy adds that future superintelligences will run detailed simulations, down to whole planets, as a routine part of thinking.

From there Yampolskiy absorbs religion. Strip away the local flavors, which day not to work, which animal not to eat, and every religion describes a superintelligent being who built a world that is not the main one. Yampolskiy even infers the simulators' character from the world: brilliant engineers, scientists and artists, with room for improvement on ethics, because there is suffering. Asked how to live, Yampolskiy cites a Robin Hanson paper on living in a simulation and advises Bartlett to keep being interesting and keep hanging out with famous people, so the watchers don't lose interest and shut it down.

Bitcoin and longevity hang off the same thread. Bitcoin, Yampolskiy says, is the only thing in the universe whose total supply is known, whereas gold can always be produced if the price is high enough. Aging is "just a disease," and extending life is one breakthrough away.

Put the three threads side by side and they share a premise: intelligence gaps cannot be bridged, and the lesser mind can neither predict nor understand the greater. That premise is what makes Yampolskiy declare control impossible, and also what lets Yampolskiy read the world as the work of a higher intelligence. As the episode closes, Bartlett says the conversation has deepened the belief that we live in a simulation and prompted a rethink of religion. An episode about AI risk ends up on faith.

Claim Check

"The day OpenAI launched its superalignment team, it said it would solve the problem in four years; about half a year later the team was cancelled." [00:50:34]

Supported, but overstated

OpenAI announced the team in July 2023 with a goal of solving the core technical challenges of superintelligence alignment in four years, plus 20% of its compute. Wired reported in May 2024 that the team had been dissolved after both leads left. The substance holds, but the gap was about ten months, not six.

"We can replace 60% of jobs today with existing models." [01:22:48]

Supported, but overstated

The nearest research figures measure tasks, not jobs. McKinsey (2025) estimates current technology could in theory automate 57% of US work hours and says explicitly this is not inevitable job loss; Eloundou et al. found only about 19% of workers have half or more of their tasks exposed to LLMs.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · no single point of failure: Yampolskiy's Bitcoin analogy is the dark reading of a design goal: the better a system tolerates the loss of any node, the less there is any one switch that stops the whole. Where it breaks down: Bitcoin's unstoppability comes from tens of thousands of independent nodes, while today's frontier weights sit in a handful of data centers, and training and inference depend on compute that can be located. A model only gains that property once it can copy and deploy itself, so the analogy describes a possible future state, not the present one.
  • Computability · Rice's theorem: no general algorithm decides any non-trivial behavioral property for all programs, which is the flavor of Yampolskiy's "unverifiable, uncontrollable." The difference: Rice rules out a universal method, not verification of a specific system; the seL4 microkernel has a full formal proof.
  • Philosophy · Bostrom's simulation argument: Nick Bostrom's 2003 original paper is a trilemma: civilizations almost always go extinct before reaching a "posthuman" stage, or posthumans almost never run ancestor simulations, or we are almost certainly simulated. Yampolskiy goes straight to the third horn, yet Yampolskiy's own forecast that AI ends civilizations adds weight to the first, unless those AIs are also keen on ancestor simulations.
  • Buddhism · maraṇasati, mindfulness of death: Yampolskiy says we all sleep fine while dying because we filter out bad outcomes we can't change, and also advises living each day as if it were the last. Maraṇasati takes the opposite road: not filtering death out but contemplating it deliberately so it reshapes today's choices. Similar destination; one route blocks, the other looks straight at it.

Ten Takeaways

  1. Roman Yampolskiy argues AI capability grows exponentially while safety research advances linearly, so the gap keeps widening.
  2. Yampolskiy compares current AI safety measures to an HR manual: a smart enough system moves its behavior into whatever the rules don't cover.
  3. Yampolskiy holds that predicting a smarter system is self-contradictory, like a French bulldog trying to understand why its owner records a podcast.
  4. Against "just unplug it," Yampolskiy points to computer viruses and Bitcoin: distributed systems have no master switch.
  5. Yampolskiy describes large models as alien plants that are grown first and studied afterward, with developers running experiments to learn what they can do.
  6. Yampolskiy's ethical argument: a system that is unexplainable and unpredictable cannot receive informed consent, so deploying it is unethical experimentation.
  7. Yampolskiy wants to stop general agents, not AI itself; a narrow superintelligence that only cures one cancer is fine.
  8. Yampolskiy argues this wave of automation leaves no job to retrain for, only work people insist a human should do.
  9. The Diary of a CEO episode's title promises "the only 5 jobs" left in 2030, but nobody in the episode lists five jobs.
  10. Yampolskiy is nearly certain the world is a simulation, while Nick Bostrom's original simulation argument is a trilemma that does not force that conclusion.
