Why is there something rather than nothing? Carroll's answer: maybe the question has no answer

Sean Carroll, solo · "Episode 9: Solo -- Why Is There Something Rather than Nothing?"

Show Mindscape · Episode 9 (Solo) · 2018-08-13 · Length 82 min · Speaker Sean Carroll (solo, no guest) · Original YouTube

The moment

Carroll says he fell for physics at ten, spending hours in the local public library reading about quarks, the Big Bang and relativity. Most of it he could think his way through. One question he could not. Lying in bed at night, mulling over some cosmology book, his mind would drift to the same place:

What if I weren't even here, what if the universe was not here, what then? And then that was it, I would no more sleep for me that night.— Sean Carroll · [00:07:03]

This episode is the homework he handed in on that childhood question four decades later. He doesn't build suspense; within ten minutes the conclusion is on the table:

I think if there is any answer to the question of why is there something rather than nothing, the answer is there isn't an answer.— Sean Carroll · [00:09:54]

The rest of the hour is his case that this is not a dodge.

Who's talking, and when

Sean Carroll is a theoretical physicist working on cosmology and the foundations of quantum mechanics; at the time he was at Caltech. This site has covered two of his later Biggest Ideas lectures, Gravity and Quantum Mechanics, recorded on a tablet in 2020. This one is much earlier: August 2018, a few months into Mindscape, and his first episode with no guest. The first few minutes are about his new Patreon, because he would rather not read ads for smoothies and penknives.

The topic came from a paper he had just finished. Two philosophers of science, Eleanor Knox and Alastair Wilson, invited him to write a review for the Routledge Companion to Philosophy of Physics, titled "Why is there something rather than nothing?" Writing it was a tremendous hassle, he says; in memory it was great fun. The paper went up on arXiv in February 2018, and this episode is essentially its spoken version. He mentions that he went to a Catholic university and was taught Aquinas's arguments for God. He is a public advocate of the many-worlds interpretation and uses it implicitly in the quantum part, though he says the conclusions don't hinge on it.

Glossary

  • Principle of Sufficient Reason: Leibniz's principle that everything that happens happens for a reason.
  • Brute fact: a fact with no deeper reason behind it; you just accept it.
  • Wheeler–DeWitt equation: what you get applying quantum mechanics to the whole universe; it says, in effect, that the universe's total energy is zero, and it contains no time.

The episode, worked through

1. Two kinds of "why," and physics only answers one

The doorbell rings and someone is standing there with a pizza. Ask your partner why, and the answers pile up: because I ordered one; because I was hungry; because we live in a capitalist system where money buys goods; because gravity is attractive, or there would be no planets and no pizza. The annoying stickler who insists on listing them all, Carroll jokes, is professionally called a philosopher.

He sorts the answers into two kinds. One is a mechanism: how the thing came about. The other is a reason: why it is this way and not another. They roughly match Aristotle's efficient and final causes. Mechanisms are physics' business; reasons are not. Modern physical laws are patterns: given the state now, an equation tells you the state next. Newton's, Maxwell's, Einstein's and Schrödinger's equations all have that shape, and none of them says "in order to."

The actual reasons that we know and love, that we would say in a more intuitive everyday situation, are emergent; they are not fundamental.— Sean Carroll · [00:26:02]

So we learned to demand reasons at the level of pizza, and, he argues, we have no right to demand the same kind of answer from the universe as a whole. There is also an older trouble he calls the "little kid problem": why is the sky blue? Scattering. Why scattering? Molecular structure. Every answer invites another why. Leibniz stopped the regress at God: the universe needs a reason, and God is his own reason, a necessary being. Hume's reply was that this is circular. You only reach a necessary being by first insisting everything needs a reason; drop that, and the universe can simply be there. Bertrand Russell put it most plainly: the universe is just there, and that's all. That, Carroll says, is the position he will defend.

2. What physics answers is often a different question

"Nothing," Carroll points out, has two meanings. One is empty space, a stage with no actors, and the question is why there is stuff in it. The other is no space or time at all, and the question is why there is a stage. Many claims that "physics has answered this" answer the first and take credit for the second. The popular line that there is something rather than nothing because "nothing is unstable" comes from Nobel laureate Frank Wilczek, but Wilczek was talking about why there is more matter than antimatter. In quantum field theory the "vacuum" is just the lowest-energy state; a "false vacuum" looks lowest but can decay, and when it releases its energy, particles appear where there were none. Physics may well explain why there is stuff. Why there are quantum fields and spacetime at all, the slogan doesn't touch.

For the harder question he turns to quantum mechanics, and the key is an apparently off-topic one: what is the total energy of the universe? Count the curvature of spacetime too, and a closed universe, finite like a sphere, can have exactly zero total energy. That splits things in two. If the energy is not zero, Schrödinger's equation implies the universe runs from the infinite past to the infinite future. He calls this the "quantum eternity theorem," admitting he named it himself. If the energy is zero, the equation becomes the Wheeler–DeWitt equation, which has no time in it at all. The passage of time we experience can then be read as correlations between configurations of the universe and readings on clocks. That time is emergent, can be finite, and can have a first moment.

Does a beginning require a beginner? The trouble, he says, is the wording:

The answer is you shouldn't say the words "popped into existence"; that's not the right way of talking about it.— Sean Carroll · [01:01:01]

"Popping into existence" already assumes a stretch of time when there was no universe. If the universe has a beginning, there is no "before the Big Bang"; the Big Bang is simply the first moment. His point: whether the universe is eternal or has a beginning, the physical description is self-contained and needs no one outside to make it. Which step of this argument is weakest is taken up in the fact-check below.

3. Five candidate answers, and he picks the least satisfying

To close, he lists five candidates: God; a "metaverse" even bigger than a multiverse, giving our universe an outside context the way hunger and phones are the context for a pizza delivery; some principle the universe satisfies, such as being the simplest or most elegant; the idea that non-existence was never a coherent option; and the universe just is.

He answers the arguments for God briefly. The cosmological argument assumes everything needs a reason, which is the very thing in question. The ontological argument says we can conceive of a most perfect being, existing is more perfect than not, so it exists. His verdict:

It's a hilarious argument. No one has ever been convinced by this argument.— Sean Carroll · [01:05:27]

His favorite way out: you think you can conceive of a most perfect being the way you think you can conceive of the largest prime. Is it male or female, blue or colorless, and which is more perfect? The concept was never defined.

The fourth option he isn't sure about, but wants philosophers to think harder about. When Derek Parfit wrote of "the case where nothing would have been," Carroll says, we're attaching being to nothingness ([01:18:49]). In the end he sides with Russell:

I think it's the one that is most courageous, most brave; it faces up to the reality of it all.— Sean Carroll · [01:18:49]

He grants that it doesn't scratch the itch and that people don't like it, but whether we like an answer is no way to judge a theory.

Fact-check

Carroll says the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem doesn't prove the universe had a beginning, only that inflation can't be eternal into the past, and that most online references to it are theological. [00:41:23]

Supported

The 2003 paper's chief result is exactly that inflationary models need physics other than inflation to describe their past boundary. It lists several possibilities for what lies beyond, a quantum nucleation event being only one. Its sole assumption is a positive average expansion rate along past paths in classical spacetime, and it notes this isn't limited to inflation.

A closed universe has exactly zero total energy; in such a universe time is emergent and can have a first moment, with no outside cause needed. [00:54:53]

Supported, but overstated

The first half is the standard result of canonically quantizing general relativity; that is what the Wheeler–DeWitt equation says. The second half, time emerging from clock correlations, faces an open "clock ambiguity" that Carroll's own paper concedes: the universe can be split into "clock" and "rest" in many ways, giving different time evolutions, and it is unclear this yields a well-formed theory. The episode skips that.

Crossovers

  • Epistemology · the Münchhausen trilemma: Hans Albert's point that every chain of justification ends in infinite regress, circularity, or an arbitrary stop. This episode has all three: the little kid problem is the regress, Hume calls Leibniz circular, and Russell and Carroll openly stop. The difference: Albert takes all three as failures of final justification; Carroll treats stopping at a brute fact as honesty, not failure.
  • Philosophy of language · Carnap on Heidegger: Carroll's complaint that Parfit attaches being to nothingness is the same move Carnap made in 1932 against Heidegger's "the Nothing itself nothings": use "nothing" as a noun and a sentence only seems meaningful. Carnap concluded metaphysics was meaningless; Carroll only says this deserves more thought.
  • Buddhism · the unanswered questions: The wanderer Vacchagotta presses the Buddha on whether the cosmos is eternal, and the Buddha takes none of the options. In Middle Discourses 72 he asks back: when a fire goes out, which direction did it go, east, south, west or north? Vacchagotta himself answers that the question doesn't apply. Carroll's suspicion that "why" doesn't apply to the universe as a whole is the same refusal: not a missing answer but a question resting on a frame that isn't there. The motives differ. The Buddha set such questions aside as useless for liberation (the poisoned arrow of MN 63); Carroll spends an hour taking one apart.
  • AI · mechanistic interpretability: At the bottom a neural network is matrix multiplies and nonlinearities; "because it recognized a cat" exists only at a higher level of description, and interpretability research tries to recover such reasons. That is Carroll's emergent reasons. The difference: the network's low-level rules have an author; on Carroll's view, the universe's don't.

Ten takeaways

  1. Sean Carroll argues that if "why is there something rather than nothing?" has an answer, it is probably that the question has none.
  2. Answers to "why" come in two kinds: mechanisms say how something happened, reasons say why it is so, roughly Aristotle's efficient and final causes.
  3. Sean Carroll holds that physical laws only describe patterns from one moment to the next, and everyday reasons are emergent rather than fundamental.
  4. Leibniz used the Principle of Sufficient Reason to make God a necessary being who is his own reason; Hume called the argument circular.
  5. "Nothing" can mean empty space or no spacetime at all; physics may explain the first and is often credited with the second.
  6. Frank Wilczek's "nothing is unstable" was about why there is more matter than antimatter, not why the universe exists.
  7. Carroll's argument splits in two: a universe with nonzero energy is eternal under Schrödinger's equation; a zero-energy one can have emergent time and a first moment.
  8. Sean Carroll says the universe "popping into existence" is the wrong language, because it already assumes there was time before the universe.
  9. Sean Carroll's five candidates are God, a metaverse, a principle of optimality, the incoherence of non-existence, and the universe just is; like Russell, he picks the last.
  10. The Buddha's refusal to say whether the cosmos is eternal, like Carroll's doubt that "why" applies to the whole universe, rejects the question's own premise.
