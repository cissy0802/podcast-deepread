Dwarkesh Podcast · 2024

Human history was not a triumphal march. It was the last group standing after extinction upon extinction

David Reich × Dwarkesh Patel · "How one small tribe conquered the world 70,000 years ago"

Show Dwarkesh Podcast · 2024-08-29 · Length 117 min · Guest David Reich · Host Dwarkesh Patel · Video YouTube

The moment

Twenty-odd minutes in, Dwarkesh asks the question everyone wants to ask. Brains were already modern-sized hundreds of thousands of years ago, so why was it only about sixty thousand years ago that one group out of Africa suddenly spread across the whole planet? Reich warns that this is outside his expertise, then describes a study so strange that some people would not put their names on it.

Besides its four letters, a genome carries a layer of on/off marks called methylation, which turns certain genes down. Remarkably, that layer survives in Neanderthal and Denisovan genomes. David Gokhman and Liran Carmel compared the methylation maps of all three groups, found roughly a thousand regions that changed only on the modern human lineage, and asked what those regions had in common. The answer was the larynx and pharynx: the soft tissue of the vocal tract, which never fossilizes.

A lot of people I know dropped off the paper. They just didn't want to be associated with it because it was so weird.— David Reich · [00:23:44]

And yet, he adds, it has held up. That sets the tone for the whole conversation: ancient DNA keeps delivering results nobody expected, and Reich's reaction is humility rather than triumph. (The paper appeared in Nature Communications in 2020.)

Who's talking, and when

David Reich runs one of the most prolific ancient-genome labs in the world, at Harvard. The introduction to his 2018 book Who We Are and How We Got Here recalls a conversation with his PhD supervisor: what if you could open a cave still echoing with languages no one speaks anymore, and record the words? Ancient DNA, he says at the end of the episode, is that kind of unexpected gift from the past.

Dwarkesh Patel is best known for his AI interviews. He reads the guest's book before recording, and here he also brings in James Scott's Against the Grain to ask whether agriculture was a catastrophe. He bends nearly every topic back toward AI: if humanity's success came from bigger brains alone, what does that say about "just make the model bigger"?

The episode went up at the end of August 2024. The Scandinavian plague paper Reich mentions had come out weeks earlier, and his lab's study of the last ten thousand years of natural selection, led by Ali Akbari, was still unpublished. It appeared in Nature in April 2026, with 15,836 West Eurasian individuals. On the show Reich said that on average selection acted on immune and metabolic traits and "hardly" touched cognition; the paper's abstract also reports rising polygenic scores for measures of cognitive performance and falling scores for schizophrenia, while noting that those effects were measured in industrialized societies.

Glossary

  • Admixture / gene flow: two populations interbreed, and DNA flows from one into the other.
  • Mitochondrial DNA and the Y chromosome: the first passes only down the maternal line, the second only down the paternal line, so they track female and male movement separately.
  • Yamnaya, Corded Ware, Bell Beaker: successive groups, and their archaeological cultures, that expanded west from the Eastern European steppe around five thousand years ago.

The episode, unpacked

1. A textbook model held together by patches

Reich opens with Ptolemy's epicycles. The "standard model" of how modern humans, Neanderthals and Denisovans relate was built up paper by paper over a dozen years. Each unexpected admixture event got patched on, and nobody went back to rethink the whole thing.

The most glaring patch goes like this. The whole genome says modern humans split from the Neanderthal–Denisovan branch 500,000 to 750,000 years ago. But both the mitochondrial DNA and the Y chromosome say Neanderthals and modern humans shared an ancestor only 300,000 to 400,000 years ago, after the split. The standard fix is a later, small pulse of gene flow from the modern human side into Neanderthals. For both the maternal and the paternal line to come from that one small event by chance, though, the odds are roughly 5% squared, so you add natural selection favoring those two pieces of DNA: one more epicycle.

The alternative he is testing is radical. Perhaps the share of Neanderthal DNA from the modern human lineage is not a few percent but 30 to 70%, in which case it is no longer clear who is "modern" and who is "archaic." Pressed on which model he favors, he says he is "very agnostic"; he is only sure the standard one is now low probability.

Later he turns the same logic on living people. Neanderthals lived in small populations for a long time and accumulated many slightly harmful mutations, so after admixture, natural selection quickly purged their DNA from mixed populations. Non-Africans today carry about 2% Neanderthal DNA, but counted by ancestors, the Neanderthal share was much higher. His analogy is African elephants: savanna elephants carry forest-elephant mitochondria, the trace of wave after wave of savanna bulls moving into forest herds and replacing them from the inside.

It's not even obvious that non-Africans today are modern humans.— David Reich · [00:00:00]

So when Dwarkesh asks what he would put a million-dollar bounty on, his first answer is ancient DNA from all over Africa, 50,000 to 200,000 years old. The modern human lineage was very likely braided together from several African lineages, while almost all the ancient DNA we have comes from Eurasia, where preservation is better.

2. An archipelago, a spark, and the last group standing

To answer "what changed sixty thousand years ago," Reich first redraws the picture. His group sequenced hunter-gatherers from eastern and central Africa over the past fifteen thousand years. Almost every group was a few hundred people, with low diversity and little contact with others, and most left no descendants. Prehistoric Africa, and perhaps Eurasia, was an archipelago of thousands of such islands, each one losing diversity and recharging only when groups occasionally merged.

Cultural knowledge leaks away the same way. A disaster kills a few elders and a craft is gone. Reich's example is Tasmania, where he says people cut off from mainland Australia lost fire. (Whether Tasmanians really lost the ability to make fire has long been debated; see Gott's 2002 paper "Fire-making in Tasmania".) Once a group passes some critical size, though, shared knowledge starts to snowball, and with thousands of small groups running the experiment at once, one of them eventually catches fire.

Here Dwarkesh steers toward AI, and Reich pours a little cold water on the analogy.

The human brain is maybe only three times larger than that of a chimpanzee.— David Reich · [00:29:49]

Compute has grown by many orders of magnitude in forty years; humans and chimpanzees differ by a factor of three. He cites work finding that human and chimpanzee infants solve logic puzzles about equally well. What sets humans apart is social learning: watching, copying, picking up what others have already worked out.

Leaving Africa was not a steady advance either. Reich's image is a forest fire throwing sparks into Siberia, South Asia and Europe. Many of the first modern humans to reach Europe some 45,000 years ago had Neanderthal ancestors within a few generations, and nearly all of them died out. Most of the ancestry of Eurasians today comes from a later wave out of the Near East, after 39,000 years ago.

There's just extinction after extinction after extinction of the Neanderthal groups, of the Denisovan groups, and of the modern human groups, but the last one standing is one of the modern human groups.— David Reich · [00:37:11]
I think it's not a triumphal march of superiority and inferiority.— David Reich · [00:38:23]

3. Pots, plague, and the asymmetry of fathers and mothers

The third thread is five thousand years ago. In the early twentieth century, a new style of pot in a layer meant, to archaeologists, a new people invading, and the Nazis later used that reasoning to tell a story of Aryan expansion. After the war the field swung to "pots, not people": how could densely settled farming Europe be displaced by thinly spread steppe herders?

Ancient DNA's answer is that it was. In Britain, the farmers who built Stonehenge largely vanished about 4,500 years ago, replaced by Beaker people with steppe ancestry, and the same happened in Spain, Germany and Italy. How did the less numerous side win? One factor Reich bets on is plague: steppe people may have lived with it longer, and its arrival in farming Europe lines up with the turnover.

I think people are embarrassed by the implication. The implication is that a third, a quarter, half of deaths in this entire period are from this.— David Reich · [00:45:53]

More revealing than the numbers is the asymmetry between the sexes. After the turnover in Iberia, only about 40% of ancestry was new, yet the Y chromosomes were almost entirely replaced: local men's paternal lines ended. Some archaeologists suggest female mate choice rather than violence. Reich points to what descendants of those Iberians did four thousand years later in the Americas: in Colombia today, 95% of Y chromosomes are European and 95% of mitochondrial lineages are Native American.

We know what happened there. It wasn't friendly, it wasn't peaceful, it wasn't nice.— David Reich · [01:06:15]

But it is not always outside men overrunning local women. Vanuatu runs the other way: the first settlers were almost purely East Asian seafarers, and five hundred years later Papuan men who had adopted long-distance sailing arrived in large numbers. East Asian ancestry there today is only about 10%.

The thread ends with an archaeologist. Colin Renfrew argued that Indo-European languages spread with farming from Anatolia, partly because steppe herders were too few to cause a demographic upheaval. When the ancient DNA came in, he said publicly that he had been wrong. Reich calls him one of his idols in the field.

Fact check

On the show, Reich says 5 to 10% of randomly sequenced Bronze Age people carry plague, and allowing for detection sensitivity, a quarter to half of all deaths in this period were from plague. [00:44:08]

Supported, but overstated

The 2015 Cell paper found it in 7 of 101 people; the 2024 study of Swedish Neolithic tombs found at least 17% and says the true rate may be higher. But the same paper cautions that tomb burials are not the whole population and that the lethality of these early strains is still debated. "Half of all deaths" is Reich's own extrapolation; neither paper says it.

On the show, Reich says after the last big stones of Stonehenge went up, within a hundred years 90% of the people were gone, replaced by migrants from the continent. [00:48:27]

Supported, but overstated

The 90% comes from Reich's lab's 2018 Nature paper, but its abstract says about 90% of Britain's gene pool was replaced "within a few hundred years." On the show the timescale shrinks to a century, a sharper story than the paper itself tells.

On the show, Reich says for non-Africans, Neanderthals were not 2% of their ancestors but 10 or 20%; that Neanderthal DNA was rapidly purged by selection is overwhelmingly supported. [01:19:04]

Supported, but overstated

Purging by selection is backed by several modeling studies. But Harris and Nielsen (2016) are explicit: an initial admixture near 10% follows if harmful mutations act additively; if most are recessive, an initial fraction closer to 1% is enough to explain today's level. 10 to 20% is a model-dependent estimate, not a settled figure.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary computation · Island-model genetic algorithms: split a population into isolated subpopulations that evolve separately and swap a few individuals every so many generations. Each island converges fast and loses diversity, yet the archipelago as a whole keeps it and is less likely to get stuck in a local optimum. Reich's archipelago of small African groups has nearly the same structure. Where it differs: in the algorithm, migration is scheduled and metered, while prehistoric mergers came from disasters and chance encounters, and no island in the algorithm wipes out another.
  • Philosophy · The Ship of Theseus: if a Neanderthal population is replaced from the inside by wave after wave of modern humans, keeping its maternal lines and perhaps its culture, is it a modernized Neanderthal group or a modern human group with new blood? Reich's question about non-Africans is the ship whose planks have all been swapped. The difference is that genetics answers with a proportion, not a yes or no.
  • AI · The collective brain and pretraining: Joseph Henrich calls humanity's edge a "collective brain": capability lives in a group's accumulated culture, not in individual brain size. Reich's answer to Dwarkesh lands on that side. Large language models, too, get most of their ability from absorbing accumulated human text, not from parameter count alone. Unlike Tasmania, though, a trained model does not lose its knowledge when a few elders die.

Ten takeaways

  1. David Reich argues that the standard model of how modern humans and Neanderthals relate is like Ptolemy's epicycles, assembled from paper-by-paper patches.
  2. Neanderthal mitochondrial DNA and Y chromosomes split from modern humans later than the whole genome did, the point the standard model struggles most to explain.
  3. In an alternative model Reich is testing, 30 to 70% of Neanderthal DNA may come from the modern human lineage.
  4. Neanderthal DNA carried harmful mutations from small populations and was purged after admixture, so Neanderthals made up far more than 2% of non-Africans' ancestors.
  5. The data Reich would most like to put a bounty on is ancient DNA from across Africa, 50,000 to 200,000 years old.
  6. Prehistoric Africa was like an archipelago of groups of a few hundred people, each losing diversity and recharging it through occasional mergers.
  7. The human brain is only about three times a chimpanzee's, and Reich thinks human distinctiveness lies more in social learning and accumulated culture than in raw compute.
  8. Leaving Africa was like a forest fire: the first modern humans in Europe often mixed with Neanderthals and mostly died out, and a later wave survived.
  9. Ancient DNA overturned the postwar consensus of "pots, not people": around five thousand years ago steppe groups caused large population turnovers in Britain and elsewhere.
  10. Asymmetries between paternal and maternal lines show how groups met: local Iberian men's paternal lines nearly vanished, while Vanuatu's main ancestry came from Papuan men.
