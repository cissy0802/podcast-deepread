The man who asked to go from E9 back to E7, and his ledger of long hours

Philip Su × Ryan Peterman · OpenAI & Meta Distinguished Engineer (IC9): Working With Zuck, Carmack & Career Growth | Philip Su

Show The Peterman Pod · 2025-05-23 · Length 81 min · Guest Philip Su · Host Ryan Peterman · Video YouTube

The moment

Near the end of the episode, Philip Su tells a story he says most people guess the ending of wrongly. It was his first year at Microsoft. He kept a sleeping bag in his office and regularly slept there. His fiancée flew from Maryland to visit him in Seattle, and the itinerary he had planned for her went like this: work ended at 11 p.m., then the two of them ate a late dinner at Denny's until midnight, and then he went back to coding.

I am very fortunate and lucky that she is my wife now. But anybody I tell that story to, you could bet nine out of 10 times that's the story that ends with that's how I got the engagement ring back— Philip Su · [01:16:07]

He closes with an image: some reeds bend and some reeds break, and the broken ones don't get fixed. Back then he bent something further than most things can bend, without noticing. On the surface the eighty minutes are about levels, Zuckerberg and Carmack. Underneath, they keep circling one question: what did the grind actually buy, and what did it put at stake?

Who's talking, and when

Su spent twelve years at Microsoft and reached the equivalent of Meta's E7 at around thirty. Over those twelve years, he says, Microsoft's stock went up fifteen cents. At 33 he joined Facebook as the second hire in its Seattle office, and for seven months he was the oldest person there; the company's median age was 27. He later took twelve engineers to London to start an engineering office that grew to four or five hundred people, which got him promoted to E9. Then he moved to Oculus to write code and asked to be releveled to E7. When he left in 2018 he paid for a day of coffee for the whole company, a bill the host puts at over $23,000.

The host, Ryan Peterman, was a new grad who had just joined Facebook in 2018 and drank that free coffee. His show interviews senior engineers and keeps returning to promotions, management, and what you would tell your younger self. This episode was recorded in spring 2025. After Facebook, Su ran a Gates Foundation-funded nonprofit that writes free software for malaria rapid testing, and spent a peak season working in Amazon's flagship warehouse, which became his podcast miniseries Peak Salvation. When AI took off, he applied to exactly one company and joined OpenAI when it had roughly 120 engineers.

Glossary

  • Meta engineering levels: E5 is senior, E6 staff, E7 senior staff, E9 distinguished engineer, three levels above staff. Su says that internally, E9 is roughly vice-president level in compensation.
  • One-way door: Amazon's term for a decision that is hard to walk back.

The episode, in depth

1. The ledger of hours: diminishing returns are still returns

Peterman asks what drove Su's fast rise at Microsoft. Su names three things: luck, talent and hard work. He is blunt about the first two. Not everyone can be an astronaut, and he will never be a great basketball player. Only the third is under your control. In those years he set a 3 a.m. alarm, following what he calls Da Vinci's theory: sleep four hours, wake up and code, then sleep another four.

Peterman pushes: if the goal is growth at all costs, was that actually optimal? Su does not defend the forty-hour week. He grants that each extra hour is less productive, but argues that for many people the curve runs a long way before it turns negative. His example is surgical residency. Nobody wants to be operated on by a surgeon in their 34th hour, yet everyone wants a surgeon who has seen a wide variety of cases, and the fastest way to pack six years of experience into four is more hours in the operating room.

So, there is diminishing returns, but there are still returns.— Philip Su · [00:04:25]

He saves the other half of this ledger for the end. Having reached E7 around thirty and decided it might be his terminal level, he went through a fairly serious depression: life had meaning while the goal was clear, and once he caught it he had no idea what came next. He calls himself the dog that caught the car, or a child actor wondering what to do with the next forty years. So his advice to his younger self has two parts. First, work out what you actually want; he quotes Roy Disney: decisions are easy when your values are clear. Second, reaching E7 at 30 versus 38 makes no difference over a whole career, because after 38 there are still thirty years of work left.

If stamina fades, what grows? His answer is judgment.

When I was young, I would outrun everybody, but I was running serpentine basically, right?— Philip Su · [01:03:51]

The experienced person jogs in a straight line, because they can smell early when something is heading the wrong way. Su's example is a friend on a Microsoft project whose vice president came to bug triage every week to keep the ship date.

look, if your VP is in triage meetings on a weekly basis so that you can ship on time, your project is nowhere near shipping on time.— Philip Su · [01:04:52]

The other thing that grows is a willingness to have the whole team pull in a direction that is a couple of degrees off. When he was young, two degrees off was simply wrong. Now he thinks a whole team pulling 98 percent right beats half a team pulling 100 percent right while the other half argues. He sums it up with Amazon's "disagree and commit."

2. A level is not a checklist

Su's yardstick for levels is how big and how long a thing you can single-handedly guarantee to deliver. He warns that his data is fifteen years old, then gives rough marks. An E5 or E6 regularly influences the work of ten to fifteen people. An E7 influences around fifty; hand them a goal, leave them alone for six months, and it should be done when they come back.

Above E8, quantity gives way to quality. He remembers the first discussions of E9 at Facebook, when the company had several thousand people and three engineers were candidates, each excellent in a completely different way. No one could have written a list saying "do these four things and you get E9." He compares it to academic tenure: in the end, you are a professor when the other professors believe you are one.

a lot of it is would other E8 think you are an E8?— Philip Su · [00:25:37]

The same yardstick explains why he asked to be demoted. What earned him E9 in London were invisible judgment calls. For instance, he modeled next year's expected H-1B volume and used it to argue with headquarters about London's intern headcount, eighteen months before the consequences would land. Back as a coder, none of that applied. When he was promoted he had raised exactly this worry with the then-CTO, Schrep, who told him to deal with it when it happened. When it happened, he found that performing well at a level feels far better than treading water one level up. He adds that after stepping down you have to bite your tongue about how the site is run and back its new leaders.

He describes the shape of these moves as a diamond. One or two years in, switching from engineer to product manager is easy. Around E5 it is hardest, because the skills have diverged. Higher up, a great E8 and a great director converge again: both need vision, business sense and the ability to lead people. So he has switched between managing and individual work at least six times, and on four occasions a former manager of his asked to report to him. For anyone weighing the management track, his test is a single question:

Would you want to work for you? That's the key question.— Philip Su · [00:11:27]

He applies it to himself. He would not have worked for his 24-year-old self, the year he had to fire a 40-year-old report two levels above him. Even in his last year of managing, he says, he was bad at giving hard feedback: he softened it so much that people missed how serious it was.

3. A generalist's value moves with company size

Su calls himself a generalist. When he joined Facebook it had about 500 engineers, and he and two others took video calling from nothing to shipped in about six months, while he also helped negotiate the Skype contract and hired and managed the testing contractors. Eight years later, with thousands of engineers, he felt like "a very generic coder." That was one reason he left in 2018.

His explanation is what he calls quantization. A small company cannot hire a three-hours-a-week comms person, so it asks a product manager who writes well to cover it; a generalist can be sliced into several fractional roles. Once every slice of work justifies a full-time hire, most of a generalist's range goes unused. That is why he tells new graduates not to bind early to one specialty: if the technology dies, you become the COBOL programmer hoping for another Y2K.

He picked OpenAI with the same kind of reasoning.

What I learned from working at Facebook is I would much rather join the market leader or nobody at all.— Philip Su · [00:51:06]

The leader, he argues, can afford a few colossal failures in exchange for brave bets. The number two has to match every one of the leader's checkboxes and then add one differentiator, like Google+'s circles, so its whole timeline is dictated by the leader.

Claim check

Su says that working 50 to 60 hours a week probably gets more done in total, and that for many people the productivity curve runs a long way before turning negative. [00:04:25]

Supported, but overstated

Economist John Pencavel reanalyzed records from British munitions plants in World War I, mostly women workers. Below 49 weekly hours, output was proportional to hours; above that it grew more slowly, peaked at about 63 hours, and output at 70 hours differed little from output at 56. The 50–60 range is still on the rising part, but the "long way" ends around 63, and this is factory work.

Su says surgical residents still work 36-hour shifts. [00:04:25]

Contrary to the evidence

Not in the United States. ACGME, which accredits residency programs, caps continuous scheduled clinical work at 24 hours in its current Common Program Requirements, plus up to four hours for handoffs and education with no new patients, and 80 hours a week. A 36-hour shift is no longer allowed.

Su says the number two's timeline is dictated by the leader, so you should join the market leader or nobody. [00:51:06]

Opinion, not fact

His Google+ example holds: in 2018 Google itself said consumer usage was low, with 90 percent of sessions under five seconds, and announced the shutdown. But Facebook is a counterexample. From 2005 to 2009 the largest social network in the world was Myspace; Facebook caught up from behind.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · the Paxos leader: in basic Paxos, two proposers that keep outbidding each other's proposal numbers can prevent any value from being chosen, a livelock. Lamport's fix in "Paxos Made Simple" is to select a single distinguished proposer to drive progress. Su's "pull together at 98 percent" makes the same trade: progress first, optimality second. The difference is that in Paxos any chosen value is equally correct, while a team's direction really can be better or worse, so the disagreement has to be voiced before the commit.
  • Computer science · bin packing: Su's quantization is bin packing. A small company's roles are bins full of odd-sized scraps, and only divisible items (generalists) fill them; in a large company each job already fills a bin, and divisibility stops being worth anything.
  • Ecology · generalist and specialist species: specialists tend to win in stable environments and generalists in changing ones. Su's COBOL warning is the same point: a specialty is a bet on a niche that may vanish, and the individual carries the risk.
  • Buddhism · bhava-taṇhā: Buddhism divides craving into three kinds, one of which is the craving to become something. Su's emptiness after reaching E7 has that structure: what he craved was the becoming, and once he had become it the craving lost its object while the suffering stayed. The difference is that Buddhism's remedy is to see the craving clearly, while Su's is to work out in advance what you actually want.

Ten takeaways

  1. In his first year at Microsoft, Philip Su slept in his office and could only see his visiting fiancée for a Denny's dinner at 11 p.m.
  2. Philip Su says fast career growth comes from three things, luck, talent and hard work, and only hard work is under your control.
  3. Philip Su's view of long hours is that each extra hour returns less, but until the curve turns negative it still returns something.
  4. Philip Su reached E7 around thirty, treated it as his terminal level, and fell into depression, like a dog that caught the car.
  5. Philip Su says senior people "jog in a straight line" by judgment; a VP attending weekly bug triage means the project is nowhere near on time.
  6. Philip Su measures levels by scope: an E7 influences about fifty people and can deliver a six-month project unsupervised.
  7. Philip Su compares promotion above E8 to academic tenure: what matters is whether peers at that level see you as one of them.
  8. After moving from leading the London site to coding at Oculus, Philip Su asked to go from E9 back to E7 and stopped weighing in on how the site was run.
  9. Philip Su's test for whether someone is ready to lead a team is whether they would want to work for themselves.
  10. Philip Su's "quantization" explains why generalists are worth more at small companies, and structurally it is the bin-packing problem.
