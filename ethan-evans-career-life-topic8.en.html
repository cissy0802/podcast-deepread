Ethan Evans Series · No. 8

Career choices and life: pick the person, do the math, and don't expect the company to love you

He teaches people how to climb the ladder faster, and every December he republishes the same essay reminding them the ladder will not remember them. This piece puts his advice on choosing a boss, switching tracks, making money and living a life side by side

Topic Career choices and life · Articles 18 free Level Up articles read in full, 15 cited (2023–2026) · Interviews BigDeal (hosted by Codie Sanchez, 2026) · A Life Engineered (2026)

His claims

1. Choose the boss before the company

In the summer of 2023, in "How To Find a Great Manager," Evans gave a plain piece of advice: when you look for a job, interview your future boss. Most people put real effort into researching companies and comparing roles, and almost none into vetting the person who will hand out their work and write their review. His reason fits in one sentence:

Yet your manager has more impact on your happiness and your success than the company or the specific job role.— Ethan Evans · "How To Find a Great Manager," 2023

He suggests three ways to dig: read what the manager says publicly, talk to the people who work or used to work for them, and ask them directly about a time they helped someone grow. The hard part comes next. A better boss may mean a smaller company and a lower salary, and most people would rather squeeze into Google than work for the right person.

The claim comes with a self-check built in. In the same piece he warns that a manager whose style doesn't suit you may be great for others, and that feedback you dislike doesn't make someone a bad boss:

If you are on your third consecutive “bad” manager, there are good odds that you have some areas to look at yourself.— Ethan Evans · "How To Find a Great Manager," 2023

So when do you leave? "When to quit your manager?" (November 2023) boils the bar down to three traits: honest, competent, supportive. A manager with all three is worth keeping, since most fall short on at least one, and we are not perfect employees either. The time to go is when an honest attempt to build the relationship has failed and they still treat you as a cog in their machine. His minimum is three months; six to nine is fairer. He uses himself as the example: two of his best managers were business leaders who inherited his engineering team in reorgs without much enthusiasm, and the pairs went on to do great things together.

2. Switching tracks means making the other side take less risk

On career pivots he always argues from the hiring manager's chair. "It's Hard to Switch Careers without Taking a Step Back" (2023) says a hiring manager has three questions: can you do the work, will you work hard and reliably, and will you fit in? A pivot makes the first one much riskier, because you have no proof you've done it. Hence:

To pivot a career, you need to either reduce the perceived risk that the hiring manager is taking or you need to find someone willing to take an increased risk, or both.— Ethan Evans · "It's Hard to Switch Careers without Taking a Step Back," 2023

His four routes all follow from that. Move internally, where the last two questions are already answered; work for someone who has worked with you; take a more modest role, which amounts to selling a proven performer from another field at a discount; or go to a smaller company more willing to gamble.

Age is the same risk model seen from another side. "Aging in tech, the truth" opens with a confession: when his hair started going white at 40, he dyed it for years, for the sole reason of not looking too old at work. His remedy is again to reduce other people's uncertainty about you. Seek opportunities through people who know your work, so they see ability rather than age, and stay current with new technology so you don't become the stereotype you fear. Working at Twitch, Amazon's live-streaming subsidiary, he could chat casually about esports, and he says that defines your apparent age more than your hair color.

In August 2026 he applied the same logic to the biggest pivot of all, leaving corporate life to work for yourself. The first of his "11 Questions You Must Answer Before Leaving Your Corporate Job" borrows Amazon's one-way and two-way doors: if it fails, can you go back? Later questions ask why anyone would hire you, whether you are a vitamin or a painkiller, and whether you can handle lumpy income. With four clients, losing one costs a quarter of the business. The most telling passage is at the top. He says he left Amazon with no business model, wrote on LinkedIn, then reviewed resumes, then coached, and found today's business along the way. Then:

However, the opportunity to “wander” my way to a successful business model came from my unique situation. Most people do not have that.— Ethan Evans · "11 Questions You Must Answer Before Leaving Your Corporate Job," 2026

3. Money is a recipe; the hard part is waiting

He treats corporate pay as a game whose rules are written in the open. "Wealth from a corporate job is a recipe" (2024) says companies pay what they feel they must, and all the big gains come from moving up levels: the worst-paid Principal Engineer or Director makes more than the best-paid entry-level engineer. So first figure out what the company values, from what it says and even more from whom it promotes; then get good at those things and make sure it shows, for example by sending frequent small progress updates instead of waiting for a big announcement. If you dislike the rules, switch companies, but don't stay and sulk:

I like ice hockey. If I stay on a volleyball team and am unhappy that I sit on the bench, exactly who is responsible for that?— Ethan Evans · "Wealth from a corporate job is a recipe," 2024

What to do with the money is textbook personal finance in "10 simple steps to build wealth and retire early" (2023): live on less than you make, pay off high-rate debt, keep six months of expenses, take the full 401k match, buy an index fund and hold it, and don't day-trade. The hard step, he says, is waiting. He wanted to be secure by 35, then 40, then 45, and made it at 50. In "So Happy" (2026) he says that as an Amazon executive he drove a dented, faded used Jeep, and the savings paid to rebuild a girls' boarding school in Hoima, Uganda, for about $60,000, the price of a nice new car.

4. The company won't love you, so draw your own line

For years he has taught people how to win at work while insisting that work isn't worth everything. Both sides first appear together in "Long hours expectations are here to stay" (May 2023). The bias toward long hours will persist, he writes, and people who can and will work longer, visible hours almost always benefit in how they are perceived; he stresses that he is describing the world, not endorsing it. If you want shorter hours, you must set boundaries, choose more balanced managers and companies, or be so good that they accept your terms, and be ready to pay for it. That September he wrote that in fifteen years at Amazon he never let vacation expire.

In December he published "In 20 years only your children will remember that you worked late." Three years after leaving Amazon, he says, almost none of his dozens of close former colleagues call each other, and the embodiment of "the company" has never reached out from any employer:

Remember that your company cannot love you and will not love you.— Ethan Evans · "In 20 years only your children will remember that you worked late," 2023

Amazon, he adds, will not send a fruit basket to his retirement home or flowers to his funeral. He has republished the piece every December since, updating "three years" to four and then five; the 2024 version adds a chart of whom we spend time with across a lifetime, the 2025 version Randy Pausch's Last Lecture. Only the 2023 version's reader discussion carries his own reckoning:

I think I would have chosen to slow down and work three years longer.— Ethan Evans · "In 20 years only your children will remember that you worked late," 2023

His arithmetic: work 10% fewer hours for one more hour a day with family, then 10% more years, and end up in the same place with better balance along the way. By 2026 the thread had become one about meaning. Asked on BigDeal how to work hard for money without burning out, he answered:

Burnout is less, it turns out, about hard work, although you can overwork to the point of physical exhaustion. It's more about losing your vision.— Ethan Evans · BigDeal [00:58:36]

Amazon's gift, he says, was autonomy: he felt like a mini-CEO who knew he was building Prime Video, so he was tired but not burned out. "So Happy" is more personal. At Amazon he would have rated his happiness 7 or 8 out of 10, partly because of the hours and partly because helping people watch movies and play games didn't feel like making the world better. Today he calls himself "11 out of 12," raising the top of the scale because he hadn't known that much joy was possible.

How the claims fit together

The four claims are four pages of one ledger. Choosing a boss and switching tracks are both about risk judgments, yours of a boss and a hiring manager's of you; the money recipe tells you how the game is scored; drawing a line answers when to stop playing. Read together, the articles suggest a sequence: learn the rules, pick the right people, save enough, then spend the money on time and meaning. It is the path he walked. He left at 50 with a nest egg, which is what let him "wander" into his current business. On A Life Engineered he says he retired with enough money and so can afford to be transparent [01:06:36]. That is the "motive" discount from No. 1, seen from the other side: the freedom to speak bluntly is itself a product of the recipe.

Across the series, this piece turns the earlier ones into a life line. When "When to quit your manager?" talks about investing in the relationship, it sends readers straight to the Magic Loop from No. 2 (his promotion method, built on asking your boss how you can help): help first, judge later. Yet No. 6 showed that reorgs can swap your boss at any time, so a carefully chosen boss has a shelf life. The "make it visible" pay recipe sits next to his year-end essay against overwork. It looks like a contradiction, and his resolution is one line: climb if you want and bank the money, but don't believe the company returns your loyalty.

Limits

In "How To Find a Great Manager" (2023) he says your career will go further with a great manager at a modest company than with the wrong boss at the best company.

Supported, but stated too broadly

Using data from one technology-based services company, Lazear, Shaw and Stanton found that replacing a bottom-10% boss with a top-10% one raises team output more than adding a worker to a nine-person team, and workers with better bosses quit less. The evidence covers output and retention; it does not compare bosses with companies on long-run pay or career height. His own reader notes add that good managers may move on after you join.

In "Long hours expectations are here to stay" (2023) he says people who can and will work longer, visible hours almost always benefit in how they are perceived.

Supported, but stated too broadly

Erin Reid's 2015 study interviewed 82 consultants at an elite firm. Some actually worked 50 to 60 hours yet appeared always available, and earned the same reviews and promotions as true 80-hour workers; those who openly asked for less were marginalized. What paid off was looking committed, not necessarily the hours. That openly drawing a line carries a cost, he got right.

Crossing over · links to other fields

  • Economics · Hirschman's Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Albert Hirschman's 1970 book argues that when an organization declines, members either exit or stay and speak up, and loyalty makes them voice first and exit later. Evans's advice to spend three to nine months helping a boss and building the relationship before deciding follows that voice-then-exit order. The differences run two layers deep. Hirschman cares how exit and voice feed signals back so the organization can repair itself; Evans cares only about the individual's choice. And Evans says outright that loyalty runs one way, while in Hirschman's model loyalty at least hopes the organization will improve.
  • Distributed systems · blast radius: four clients, and losing one costs a quarter of revenue, like a four-node cluster where any single failure hurts badly. Kevin Kelly's "1,000 True Fans" spreads the load across many small nodes, so any one failure has a small blast radius. The catch is that more nodes cost more to maintain, and Evans's own coaching business has only a few dozen high-paying clients.
  • Buddhism · dāna (giving): in Mahayana Buddhism, giving is the first of the six perfections. A discourse in the Aṅguttara Nikāya says the giver is glad before giving, clear-minded while giving, and satisfied afterward. Evans says that at about 25 he began giving a tenth of his income because his religion required it, following a rule, and only later learned to love giving, the same path from reluctant to joyful. Unlike dāna, though, his plan was to make a big pile of money first and then give time and money; Buddhist giving never asks you to get rich first.

Ten takeaways

  1. Ethan Evans argues that job seekers should interview their future boss, because the manager affects happiness and success more than the company or the role.
  2. Evans warns that someone on their third consecutive "bad" manager probably has something to examine in themselves.
  3. Evans judges managers on three traits, honest, competent and supportive, and says to invest at least three months, ideally six to nine, before leaving one.
  4. Evans says a career pivot works by lowering the hiring manager's risk about whether you can do the work: move internally, follow a former boss, step down a level, or join a smaller company.
  5. Evans dyed his graying hair for years after 40 and argues age bias is best countered by a network, a track record and staying current with technology.
  6. In his 11 questions before leaving corporate life, Evans asks first whether the door is reversible, and admits that his own slow wander into a business depended on savings.
  7. Evans says the big pay gains in corporations come from promotion levels, so the recipe is to see whom the company promotes, build those skills and make them visible.
  8. Evans's money advice is to spend less than you earn, clear high-rate debt, hold six months of cash and keep an index fund for decades; the hard part is waiting.
  9. Evans republishes "In 20 years only your children will remember that you worked late" every year, says the company cannot love you, and says he would have worked less and three years longer.
  10. Evans says burnout comes more from losing your vision than from hard work, and that finding meaning in helping others moved him from 7 of 10 to 11 of 12.
