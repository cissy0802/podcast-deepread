Anxiety as the engine of thought: Robert Greene on life's task, a stroke, and AI as a helicopter to the summit

Robert Greene × Andrew Huberman · "A Process for Finding & Achieving Your Unique Purpose | Robert Greene"

Show Huberman Lab · 2023-12-04 · Length 191 min · Guest Robert Greene · Host Andrew Huberman · Video YouTube

The moment

About two hours and forty-five minutes in, Huberman asks Greene to explain his shirt. It is a short-sleeved plaid, and on each side a jagged seam runs from the sleeve down to the buttons at the chest.

Greene says his wife gave it to him for his birthday in May 2018; he has a weakness for plaid. Three months later, on August 17, he was pulling out into traffic when she started telling him to pull over. He insisted he was fine. Then everything began to look strange, and his own voice stopped sounding like his. A clot in his neck was blocking blood flow to his brain. By the time the paramedics arrived he was unconscious, and they cut the shirt in half to get it off over his head [02:45:57]. The shirt was later sewn back together, and he wore it to the interview.

He remembers one more detail. At some point during the stroke he got out of the car and his wife pulled him back in. He thought about ten seconds had passed. She told him it was more like ten minutes. That slip in time became one of his keys to how the brain works.

Who's talking, and when

Robert Greene wrote The 48 Laws of Power, Mastery and The Laws of Human Nature. He took ancient Greek as a student at Berkeley, then worked in journalism and in Hollywood, and hated both. He says he did not really find his path until he was 38 or 39.

Andrew Huberman is a Stanford neurobiologist whose show usually ends in protocols. Here he asks for almost none, and talks a good deal about himself. In 2015 he taught a 450-student undergraduate course at UC San Diego and closed it each year by recommending three books, one of them Mastery. Without that book, he says, the podcast probably would not exist. So the episode has the feel of a student meeting a teacher: Huberman often offers a story of his own and then hands the thread back to Greene.

By the time of recording Greene was five years past the stroke. He can write for three or four hours a day, and tying his shoes takes ten minutes. He was working on a book about the sublime, with chapters on early humans and on near-death experience. The three hours also cover power, seduction, choosing a partner and reading faces. This page follows the three lines that carry the most argument.

The episode, worked through

1. Your life's task is hiding in what gripped you at four or five

Greene starts from uniqueness: your DNA has never existed before in the history of the universe, your childhood belongs to you alone, and wasting that is the worst thing you can do. What you are looking for he calls your life's task. The clues sit at age four or five, in what Maslow called "impulse voices": I love this, I hate that.

His own clue was words. At six he was obsessed with them, spelling them backwards, taking them apart, collecting palindromes. His other examples are famous people: Einstein at four, mesmerised by a compass from his father; Jobs as a boy, transfixed by the devices in a shop window. He concedes that with famous people it is easy to find the story after the fact.

The trouble is that the voice gets drowned out. After about seven, teachers tell you you're bad at math, parents have a career in mind, and as a teenager all that matters is what your peers find cool. Many people end up with a "practical" major and pick jobs by salary. Greene's method is archaeology: dig back for the bones of your childhood. His case for why it matters is his own French. He studied it for years in college and could not speak a word in Paris; then he had a French girlfriend and learned more in a month than in all those years. Once your emotions are engaged, you learn far faster.

Huberman gives his own version. As a boy, walking into a proper aquarium store or snorkeling for the first time set off an urge to do something about it, a feeling he says he locates in his left arm. He sums up the goal as getting that feeling in as many forms as possible. Greene agrees and adds that dislike is a signal too, but only if a child has first tasted loving something; otherwise hating math turns into hating learning in general. The line lands on a direction rather than a job title. It tells you which way to go, not which chair to sit in at 28.

2. Anxiety is a signal; the first answer is a trap

The weightiest stretch of the episode is Greene describing a book he wants to write. He separates live ideas from dead ones. A live idea is someone else's thought that you turn over and over, sometimes into its opposite, until it becomes yours. What stops that process is anxiety.

Anxiety is a signal to you that you don't understand something, that there's a problem out there that you can't resolve.— Robert Greene · [01:52:00]

An insecure mind grabs the first answer that makes the anxiety go away: so-and-so said it, so it must be true. A mind that can sit with the anxiety says maybe it's A, keeps going, and finds it's B. Huberman admits he had pictured Greene sitting down and pouring out books. Greene laughs:

My process is 95% pain and maybe 2 and a half percent ecstasy.— Robert Greene · [01:55:08]

Each chapter opens with a historical story. The first draft embarrasses him; the second is "kind of palatable", and that is where most writers stop. He keeps going for two more months, a stretch during which, by his wife's account, he is miserable.

When Huberman later asks about AI, Greene picks up exactly here. Human intelligence, he says, has three parts: pushing through anxiety to the next level, seeing your own biases and dark side, and suddenly seeing the whole out of scattered data points. Then he tells a story from age 19 at Berkeley: a six-week summer course packing a year of ancient Greek, with an exam every day. Near the end he spent a whole night on one paragraph of Thucydides. The teacher told him he could see his thinking, but he needed to go to another level. Greene says that line stayed with him for life. Had he simply fed the paragraph to ChatGPT, the "muscle" he has built over forty years, the one that lets him write books, would never have developed. He borrows Douglas Hofstadter's image: taking a helicopter to the top of Everest gives you the same view but not the same moment.

We think we're so modern, so sophisticated, but really we're just seduced by magic.— Robert Greene · [02:36:39]

Huberman first voices the obvious objection, that people probably said the same about walking when cars arrived, and then answers it himself:

It's fundamentally different because the journey transforms the outcome.— Andrew Huberman · [02:38:17]

Greene is not against tools; he says he uses Google all the time. What worries him is the order of operations: are we operating the tool, or is the tool operating us?

3. After the stroke: the brain builds everything

Back to the shirt. People are curious about death, Greene says, but dying is the more interesting process. In his view the brain normally builds a run of illusions for us: a continuous self, linear time, a familiar-looking world. When the oxygen supply was cut, that construction scrambled, and he glimpsed what lay underneath.

The self is literally an illusion that your brain constructs.— Robert Greene · [02:50:59]

The self he saw, he says, was like an image completely scrambled and wavy, with fifty competing selves inside. He also felt his bones softening and dissolving, a sensation he could summon for months afterwards. Notably, he adds the caveat himself: his memory may be playing tricks, and he may not remember exactly what happened, or may have translated it since.

The stroke took a great deal. He used to hike, swim and cycle; now he rides a recumbent bike while 80-year-old grandmothers zip past. He sits at his window envying people walking their dogs, then tells himself that they don't know how wonderful it is and he does. His three or four hours of writing a day have become the best part of it.

Near the end the line joins his idea of "death ground". Sun Tzu said to put an army where it must win or die, and it will fight. Greene's point is that a 23-year-old who thinks he has fifty years left is the one living an illusion; the reality is that you could have a stroke or be fired tomorrow. Huberman describes Greene's life now as running at a new frame rate. Greene's reply: before, he never once stopped to think that all of it could be taken away.

Fact-check

Greene says Howard Gardner described five forms of intelligence, and that your brain naturally veers toward one of them, which is where your power lies. [00:11:53]

Insufficient evidence

Gardner's theory now lists eight intelligences, not five. The bigger issue is independence. Visser and colleagues (2006) gave 200 people two tests in each of the eight domains: scores were broadly positively correlated and factor analysis found a large general factor, with only a few tests, such as musical and bodily-kinesthetic, as exceptions.

Huberman says a David Anderson paper found a non-sexual mounting circuit "entirely separate from all of the reflexes associated with sexual behavior", presumably present in humans too. [01:11:57]

Supported, but overstated

The paper (Karigo et al., Nature 2021) studied male mice. Mounting other males was mostly aggressive; mounting females came with ultrasonic calls and was sexual. The two were coded by distinct neuron populations within the same two hypothalamic nuclei, and most neurons were mixed-selective. "Distinct" holds up; "entirely separate" and "humans too" are not in the paper.

Greene says that if you watch people as if the TV were on mute, you can read their insincerity "like an open book". [02:19:04]

Contrary to evidence

Bond and DePaulo's 2006 meta-analysis pooled 206 documents and 24,483 judges: people tell lies from truths 54% of the time on average, and they do better with audible than with visible cues. Muting throws away the more useful channel. The judges in these studies were untrained, so this work cannot say whether practice changes the picture.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · Gazzaniga's "interpreter": In split-brain patients, Michael Gazzaniga found the left hemisphere instantly inventing a reason for actions the right hemisphere had taken, and doing so with full confidence. Greene's "fifty selves" points at the same thing: the unified self is a narrative assembled after the fact. The difference is that Gazzaniga had repeatable experiments, while Greene had one oxygen-starved experience, and he himself warns that the memory may not be reliable.
  • Buddhism · Not-self (anattā): Buddhism breaks the "I" into five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) and finds no permanent owner among them. Greene reaches a similar conclusion by the opposite road: meditation sees it slowly in a clear, steady mind; he ran into it when blood stopped reaching his brain.
  • Optimization · Simulated annealing: The simulated annealing of Kirkpatrick and colleagues (1983) deliberately accepts worse solutions early on so it doesn't get stuck in a local optimum. Greene's "sit with the anxiety, don't grab the first answer" is keeping the temperature high. The difference is that annealing has a cooling schedule and anxiety doesn't, so how long a draft takes is left to his judgment.
  • Learning science · Desirable difficulties: Psychologist Robert Bjork's "desirable difficulties" holds that conditions which make learning slower and harder, like spaced practice and self-testing, make memory stick better. Hofstadter's helicopter makes the same point, except Bjork measures retention and Greene is talking about a less measurable "thinking muscle".

Ten takeaways

  1. Robert Greene argues that each person's life's task is hidden in the preferences of age four or five, what Maslow called "impulse voices".
  2. Greene argues that after about age seven the voices of teachers, parents and peers drown out a child's own, so adults must dig back like archaeologists.
  3. Greene studied French for years without speaking it, then learned more in a month in Paris with a French girlfriend, his case that emotional engagement speeds learning.
  4. Greene treats dislike as a signal of direction too, but only if a child has first loved something; otherwise dislike spreads to all learning.
  5. Greene separates live ideas from dead ones: a live idea is someone else's thought turned over, even into its opposite, until it becomes your own.
  6. Greene sees anxiety as a sign that a problem is not yet understood, and says insecure people grab the first answer that makes it go away.
  7. Greene says his writing is 95% pain; most writers stop at a palatable second draft, while he keeps working for two more months.
  8. Greene uses Hofstadter's image of a helicopter to Everest to warn about ChatGPT, and Huberman adds that the journey changes the destination itself.
  9. After his 2018 stroke, Greene concluded that the continuous self and linear time are built by the brain, while warning that his near-death memories may be unreliable.
  10. Greene uses Sun Tzu's "death ground" to argue that believing you have plenty of time is the real illusion, and that urgency unlocks full effort.
