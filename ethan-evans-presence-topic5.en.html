Ethan Evans Series · No. 5

Executive Presence: a Grade Other People Give You, Mostly for What You've Done

What "lacks presence" actually means, why gravitas outranks speaking skill, which speaking habits quietly cost you points, and the layer he refuses to hide: height, deep voices and ape-troop tricks

Topic Executive presence and communication · Articles 17 free Level Up pieces read in full (12 by Evans or summarizing his talks; 5 guest posts or conversations with Lorraine K. Lee, Harriette Cole and Alisa Cohn), 2023–2026 · Interviews The Peterman Pod, episodes two and three (2026)

Glossary

  • Executive presence (EP): a phrase common in big-company promotion reviews, meaning someone comes across as "executive material": people listen to them and follow them. Evans himself says it has no agreed definition.
  • Gravitas: the first ingredient in author Sylvia Ann Hewlett's presence framework. Evans reads it as confidence and decisiveness, plus the reputation you have built by being right before.
  • Hedge words: "maybe," "I think," "just" and other words that leave yourself an exit.

His claims

1. Presence isn't something you have; it's a grade others give you

In May 2024, in Executive Presence: How to get "it", Evans listed three things: being seen as someone worth listening to (not necessarily the person in charge), being seen as decisive, and being seen as influential. The operative words, he stressed, are "being seen as."

On a math test, you are either right or wrong. But Executive Presence is assessed by others.— Ethan Evans · Executive Presence: How to get "it", 2024

That sentence moves presence out of the category of personality and into the category of evaluation. It is a conclusion other people reach after watching you, so you cannot prove you have it. In the same piece he admits the term is poorly defined and poorly understood yet universally wanted; in 2026 his talks still open with "there is no single definition."

If others are grading, you first need to understand what the graders mean. In January 2025 he translated a few common review phrases. "Sarah lacks executive presence" means Sarah has no influence and isn't impressing me. In other words, the phrase usually isn't pointing at a specific defect; it's a polite "not yet." On Peterman's show in 2026 he offered a test you can run on yourself: do people come to you for your opinion on things you aren't directly involved in? [02:18:27]

The grading view has a corollary, which he put most bluntly in his second Peterman appearance: presence is a neutral skill set, available to good people and bad.

Darth Vader had it in spades.— Ethan Evans · The Peterman Pod [01:12:42]

Influence and manipulation use the same skills, he went on; only the motive differs. So when he meets someone unusually good at working a room, his first job is to figure out which side they are on.

2. Gravitas first, speaking second

If it's a grade, what goes into the score? He has given two answers over the years, pointing the same way. In December 2023 a client asked him to break down influence. His list ran: preparation, facts, reputation (conduct and track record), relationships, and only then persuasive presentation. Most people, he noted, think of influence as eloquence, but eloquence comes last:

Do 1 to 4 well, and modest speaking will win the day.— Ethan Evans · Influence is critical for Executive Presence, 2023

From 2025 he adopted Hewlett's framework: 60% gravitas, 30% communication, 10% appearance. His example was a client he calls Sam: hardworking, well liked, good ratings, but silent in big meetings and rambling when put on the spot, and never promoted past Senior Manager (Amazon's L7, a manager of managers). On appearance he is practical: it isn't about being attractive but about showing you pay attention, and for many people the right clothes work like armor, mostly by making the wearer feel they fit in.

On Peterman's show he contrasted two types. Nobody has ever praised Warren Buffett's showmanship, yet his track record gives him enormous presence; Jobs and Musk also mastered the stage. [02:26:32] By June 2026 he had turned the idea into a heading: gravitas comes before great speaking, because people listen closely when they believe you have earned the right to speak. (Hewlett's 60/30/10 doesn't match her original survey; our deep read of Peterman episode three checked it, so we won't repeat that here.)

3. The trainable 30%: sound sure, be brief, then stop

Here is the interesting part: he says gravitas is 60%, but most of what he teaches is the 30%. The reason isn't hard to guess. A track record takes years; speaking habits can change over a weekend. In November 2025 he launched a live course of nine-plus hours, "Stronger Executive Presence," and the summaries of two public talks around it read like a list of point deductions.

The first kind of deduction is looking unconfident or indecisive. Most common is not speaking at all. His fix is to write your point down beforehand, and if you can't break in, ask a trusted colleague or your manager to open the door ("Let's hear what Arjun has to say"). Next come hedge words, which in his view weaken you on your behalf.

The second kind is two habits: over-contextualizing and "verbal fidgeting." The first is too much background, too many conditions, circling. The second is talking too fast and over-explaining. His prescription: slow down, pause, use short declarative sentences, make your point and stop, and leave the silence to the other person.

when you fill silence with nonsense, you negotiate against yourself— Evans talk summary · Executive Presence Killers, 2025

The drills are concrete: write your question in 20 words or fewer, then cut five; ask one question at a time; ask and stop talking, without restating it another way. For structure he recommends "What / So What / Now What": the point, why it matters and what is at risk if it fails, and what action you need from whom. In June 2026 he added that specificity creates credibility. Coaching three volunteers live, he watched answers improve the moment "this is important" became numbers, timelines and tradeoffs.

Over these years his tone softened in one visible place. In 2025 the message was how to sound certain; in a January 2026 talk he added that you don't have to steamroll anyone. Make your point, then close with a question that invites others in. When you don't know, say "I'm not the expert here" and call on someone who is; you are still the one directing traffic. If you misspeak, admit it, correct it and move on, without reminding everyone.

4. The layer he won't hide: height, deep voices and the ape troop

What sets Evans apart from the typical presence trainer is that he puts the unflattering part on the table. When Peterman asked what presence is, he started with himself. He speaks as if declaring truth from the mountain, and his wife once told him:

You know, Ethan, I've learned that you sound like you know.— Evans quoting his wife · The Peterman Pod [02:21:11]

She had known they should have turned right at the last corner but said nothing because he sounded so sure. He called that his presence "going too far." Then he listed traits nobody can change, being tall, being white, a deep voice, adding that you don't have to like it, but it's true. Emotion kept under control was another. His summary:

It's all these ape dominance tricks.— Ethan Evans · The Peterman Pod [02:24:26]

He cited Margaret Thatcher, accused early on of screeching, who systematically trained herself to speak low and slow. She shouldn't have had to, he said, but she did, and it worked. [02:25:26]

The thread goes back to 2023. That August a reader challenged him: women are often kept from promotion under the label "too quiet." He acknowledged he was a man whose advice could only partly help, then wrote:

The Straight Truth is that I know of no general case situation where a woman who doesn’t “play the man’s game” will move up at the same pace.— Ethan Evans · Women Get Passed Over For Leadership Roles, 2023

He offered two paths: conform, or be your authentic self and push for change at the cost of slower progress. Three years later he teaches people to drop hedges and slow their speech, which turns the first path into a course. He doesn't pretend the rules are fair, but he is selling the walkthrough.

How the claims fit together

Together the four form a chain from definition to prescription. The first says presence is others' grade, so you can only read it in their reactions. The second says the heaviest component is what you have already accomplished, and there is no shortcut there. The third handles the part that can be trained fast: in the few minutes you are visible, don't let speaking habits discount your gravitas. The fourth admits the grading itself is biased; his stance is to acknowledge that first and then teach you to cope.

Within the series, this piece is a magnifying glass on No. 3, stuck at L7. He wrote in 2024 that lack of presence is a key reason cited for delaying promotions from Senior Manager into executive roles, and his own translation tells you that usually means the reviewers aren't convinced yet. It also continues No. 4 on managing up: "Be bright, be quick, be gone" was etiquette there and becomes the core drill here. The motive discount from No. 1 is clearest in this topic: 60% gravitas is the framework he cites, while the course mostly sells the 30%.

Boundaries

"Confidence and Decisiveness are the top two traits that convey Executive Presence … hedge words … undermine your credibility." — Executive Presence Killers talk summary, 2025

Supported, but overstated

In two mock-jury experiments (48 and 103 participants), Tenney et al. (2007) found that confident witnesses were more credible when they made no error, but once caught in a mistake, confident witnesses lost more credibility than hesitant ones. People reward calibration, confidence that matches accuracy. Evans's wife and the missed turn are this exact effect.

"Avoid qualifiers: 'I think… I'm not sure…', 'Maybe', 'Just'." — Stop Holding Yourself Back talk summary, 2026

Supported, but overstated

Carli (1990) found that women who spoke tentatively were rated less competent, which supports him. But with the same message, tentative women were more persuasive to male listeners and less to female ones, while men's speech style made no difference to their influence. Whether the advice works depends on who is speaking and who is listening.

Cross-disciplinary connections

  • Evolutionary biology · Zahavi's handicap principle: Amotz Zahavi proposed in 1975 that animal signals are believable because they are costly and hard to fake, as with the peacock's tail. Evans's 60/30/10 reads well this way: gravitas is a costly signal paid for with years of results; a confident tone is a cheap signal anyone can learn. Cheap signals can fool people for a while, like the deepened voice of Elizabeth Holmes that Peterman brought up, or the "62% more believable" statistic Evans invented on air and then exposed. The difference is that animal signals were calibrated by selection over generations, while a meeting-room judgment often takes minutes.
  • Sociology · Goffman's The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life: Erving Goffman in 1959 described social life as a stage, with a front region where we manage impressions and a back region where we drop the act. Evans's "being seen as" is front-stage management; but where Goffman thought everyone performs all the time, Evans confines the performance to the meeting room.
  • Machine learning · Model calibration: Guo et al. (2017) found modern deep networks are systematically overconfident, with predicted confidence above actual accuracy, and need post-hoc calibration. Evans's coaching turns up a person's confidence output; Tenney's experiments show audiences recalibrate after the fact, so an overconfident manager, like an overconfident model, gets discounted after one visible error.
  • Buddhism · Dependent origination and emptiness: Madhyamaka philosophy holds that things have no independent, self-existing nature and arise from conditions. Presence as Evans defines it is just that: without the people watching, it doesn't exist, and at another company the same manner may read as arrogance. Both deny an essence; but Buddhism uses that to loosen attachment, while Evans uses it to tell you to manage the conditions.

Ten takeaways

  1. Ethan Evans sees executive presence as a judgment others make about you, not a trait you own, so you cannot prove you have it.
  2. Evans translates the review phrase "lacks executive presence" as: you have no influence yet and haven't impressed the reviewer.
  3. One self-test Evans offers for executive presence: do people seek your opinion on matters you aren't directly involved in?
  4. Evans says influence and manipulation use the same skills and differ only in motive; even Darth Vader had plenty of presence.
  5. Citing Sylvia Ann Hewlett, Evans ranks gravitas above communication and appearance, putting speaking skill behind track record.
  6. Evans's executive presence "killers" include not speaking, hedge words, over-contextualizing and talking too fast.
  7. Evans recommends "What / So What / Now What": state the point, explain why it matters, then name the action needed.
  8. In 2026 Evans added that presence doesn't require steamrolling: after making a point, invite others in with a question.
  9. Evans admits height, race and a deep voice shape judgments of presence, citing Margaret Thatcher deliberately lowering her voice.
  10. Through Zahavi's handicap principle, a track record is a costly, hard-to-fake signal, while a confident tone is a cheap one anyone can learn.
