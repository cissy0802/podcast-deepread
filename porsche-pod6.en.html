Acquired · 2023

A line drawn onto the wall, and a company that could not stop spending

Doug DeMuro × Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal · “Porsche (with Doug DeMuro)”

Show Acquired · Season 12, Episode 6 · 2023-06-27 · Length 203 min · Guest Doug DeMuro · Hosts Ben Gilbert, David Rosenthal · Original YouTube

The moment

By the early 1980s Porsche had already decided to retire the 911. Its successor was the 928, a front-engined V8 grand tourer. That was not a crazy call at the time. The 356 had once been “the Porsche” too, and it gave way to the 911; the oil crisis and emissions rules were making it hard to get power out of small engines, and rival sports cars were going bigger and softer. But the people in the factory loved the 911, and morale collapsed.

Peter Schutz, the new American CEO, walks into the office of engineering chief Helmuth Bott. On the wall hangs the product timeline, and the 911’s row simply stops. Schutz picks up a marker, extends the 911 line off the chart and onto the wall itself, then turns to Bott:

Do we understand each other?— Peter Schutz (as retold by Doug DeMuro) · [01:32:11]

The story has circulated for decades, and plenty of people assumed it was invented to lift morale. When DeMuro worked at Porsche’s North American arm, he became friends with the company’s general counsel, who happened to be neighbors with the retired Schutz. DeMuro had him ask. Schutz’s answer:

Not only did it happen but Helmuth Bott was grinning like the Cheshire Cat when I drew that line.— Peter Schutz (as retold by Doug DeMuro) · [01:32:11]

Schutz also wrote up the episode himself for a magazine. But the hosts immediately point to the other side of it: the line that saved a car also tied the company’s money to an old model it had planned to end. By the late 1980s Porsche was carrying three aging product lines and nearly went under. This episode is, at bottom, about a company repeatedly pushed along by its own commitments.

Who’s talking, and when

Acquired is a show where two hosts take turns telling a company’s history: David Rosenthal reads a stack of books and tells the story, Ben Gilbert pushes back and does the math, and at the end they grade the company. Their guest, Doug DeMuro, runs the largest independent car-review channel on YouTube and worked at Porsche’s North American arm about a decade earlier. They recorded in his garage, with his own Carrera GT parked behind them.

The division of labor is clean: the hosts handle capital and corporate structure, and DeMuro supplies the cars themselves, such as why a front-engined V8 counts as heresy among Porsche people.

The episode was recorded in the summer of 2023. Nine months earlier, Volkswagen Group had re-listed the Porsche operating company, which the hosts call the largest IPO in European history, and one man, Oliver Blume, was CEO of both Volkswagen and Porsche. The hosts questioned that arrangement on air. In October 2025 Porsche announced talks on ending Blume’s Porsche role early, with former McLaren CEO Michael Leiters lined up as his successor.

Glossary

  • Porsche AG: the operating company that builds the cars, the “Porsche” that is listed today.
  • Porsche SE: the family holding company created in 2007. It was the vehicle used to buy Volkswagen shares, and it still trades today.
  • Short squeeze: when so few shares are available that short sellers are forced to buy back at any price, pushing the stock far above its fundamentals.

The episode, worked through

1. A ledger that would not pay dividends

The episode offers an unromantic explanation for Porsche’s early success: it had more money than it could spend, and no good way to take it home.

After the war, Ferdinand Porsche and his son-in-law Anton Piëch spent two years in French prisons as war criminals. His son Ferry took twenty or so engineers to a sawmill in rural Austria and built the first 356 out of the Beetle parts lying around. Then a rebuilt Volkswagen came back with what the hosts call the deal of a lifetime: Porsche would keep doing design consulting for VW, VW would pay Porsche a royalty on every Beetle sold anywhere in the world, and the family’s Austrian company got exclusive distribution rights for Volkswagens.

Why would the West hand former Nazi industrialists such terms? Because the Cold War was next door, the hosts explain, and West German industry had to be rebuilt fast. The deal came with a string attached: tax. As the episode tells it, industrialists faced a 95% marginal rate, corporate profits likewise, so money taken out was almost entirely taxed away and was better spent on new models, R&D and racing. How accurate that is gets examined under “Fact-check” below. In the episode’s logic, this unspendable cash is what let Porsche chase races like Le Mans with no clear return, and the reputation won on track fed back into the brand.

The thread comes back late in the episode, with a darker ending. By the 2000s, under Wendelin Wiedeking, Porsche was again flush. German tax rules still discouraged paying profits out, the hosts say, but the new models and new plants were already funded, and there was nowhere left to spend inside the company. So the cash started flowing outward, and the investment it finally settled on was Volkswagen.

2. The family steps out, then comes back through the side door

In the fall of 1970, Ferry and his older sister Louise called a family summit. Two grandsons, both named Ferdinand, were fighting for control. Ferry’s son Butzi had designed the 911’s body; Louise’s son Ferdinand Piëch had run the 914 joint project with VW and developed the flat-six that went into the 911. The outcome surprised everyone: neither of them got the job, and the whole family withdrew from management to act purely as owners. The hosts relay Ferry’s later account from the company history: there may have been a better solution, but at least the family could go to each other’s birthday parties again.

The decision was a disaster for Porsche and a new beginning for Piëch. He went to Audi, then considered a second-tier brand, turned it into a real rival to Mercedes and BMW, became CEO of Volkswagen Group in 1993 and later its chairman. All the while he kept his seat on Porsche’s supervisory board as a family member.

In 2005 Wiedeking began buying Volkswagen shares with Porsche’s cash, much of it quietly through options and other derivatives. In 2007 he set up the holding company Porsche SE, loaded it with roughly ten billion dollars of cheap debt, and pushed the stake past 50%, aiming for 75%, the level needed to consolidate VW. But the free float of VW stock kept shrinking, and after Lehman collapsed in October 2008 a short squeeze briefly made Volkswagen the most valuable company in the world.

It looked like Wiedeking had won. The hosts argue it was a trap: the price existed only because of the squeeze, so the moment Porsche sold shares to pay its debt, it would crash; and with Lehman just gone, there was no new credit to keep buying. That is when Piëch, who had not moved a single piece on the board, made his move. Volkswagen announced publicly that Porsche was no longer viable and offered to “rescue” it. VW ended up buying the Porsche operating company in two stages for about 8.5 billion euros, and Wiedeking was gone by January 2009.

When the dust settled, the winner was neither Porsche the company nor Volkswagen the company, but the two families. Porsche SE already held more than half of VW, and now it also had 8.5 billion euros from the sale. According to the hosts, the families own about 32% of Volkswagen Group’s equity but more than half the votes. The family shown the door in 1970 came back through another one, into a much bigger house.

3. Borrowing against the brand with SUVs, paying it back with supercars

In the early 1990s Porsche was close to being taken over. US sales fell to about 4,100 cars a year, below 1965, and the hosts say its market cap at one point was under 400 million euros. Wiedeking, CEO from 1993, first brought in the Toyota Production System, then cut the lineup to the 911 alone. Asked about an entry-level car, he gave an answer the hosts keep savoring:

Porsche's strategy for an entry-level Porsche is a used Porsche.— Wendelin Wiedeking (as retold on the show) · [01:51:16]

He did have a plan. The new entry car, the Boxster, would not share a platform with Volkswagen as the 914 and 924 had; it shared its front end, headlights and nearly the whole interior with the next 911. DeMuro notes the sharing went so far that the 911’s coat hook ended up on the Boxster’s seatback, where the seat sits flush against the bulkhead and no coat could ever hang. For the first time the entry car did not feel second-class, and costs came down too. Wiedeking was explicit:

We don't want to be Germany's Ferrari. We don't want to be a big fish in a pond that's shrinking, but rather a growing fish with more room to move in a larger lake.— Wendelin Wiedeking (as retold on the show) · [01:55:55]

The larger lake was SUVs. The first Cayenne was co-developed with the Volkswagen Touareg, under Piëch. DeMuro says Porsche had no idea what SUV buyers wanted, to the point of seriously asking American employees whether to offer a gun rack. In the end it piled every off-road feature into the car, which came out heavy and ugly, and sold like crazy. By rights an SUV should have drained the brand, but in the same new factory Porsche also built the Carrera GT.

Porsche always made sure to be making other cool stuff and to keep coming out with other cool stuff.— Doug DeMuro · [02:01:21]

Today, according to the hosts, roughly two-thirds of Porsche’s revenue comes from SUVs. Their analogy: Porsche is Louis Vuitton, selling many tiers under one brand, and Ferrari is Hermès, living only at the top. The episode puts Porsche at 350,000 cars a year, which is high; the company reported 309,884 deliveries for 2022, though the order of magnitude holds. DeMuro gave the brand a perfect score on defensibility:

It isn't Ferrari, it doesn't have that brand equity that Ferrari does, but it's got more than you'd think, and it's especially got more than you'd think for a company that mostly makes SUVs.— Doug DeMuro · [03:10:04]

Fact-check

“Post-war West Germany kept ordinary people’s rates around 15–20% but set a 95% marginal rate for these industrialists, and the same for corporate profits, to force reinvestment.” [00:42:09]

Supported, but overstated

The 95% top rate was real, but it was the Allied occupation’s personal income tax schedule, applying to anyone earning above roughly 6,000 marks, not a rule aimed at industrialists. The 1948 reform narrowed it to incomes above 250,000 marks. The corporate rate was set at a flat 50%, not 95%.

“A 32-billion-euro market cap in 2007 wasn’t crazy, because Porsche was doing almost 2 billion euros a year in operating profit.” [02:10:00]

Supported, but overstated

In fiscal 2006/07 Porsche reported 5.86 billion euros of pre-tax profit, of which 3.59 billion came from Volkswagen stock options and about 1.2 billion more from its VW stake. Strip those out and cars and everything else earned roughly 1 billion. The market cap was priced more on the Volkswagen bet than on the cars.

Crossovers

  • Complexity science · Path dependence (W. Brian Arthur): Arthur argued that in markets with increasing returns, small historical accidents get amplified by positive feedback until a whole industry is locked into one path. Porsche’s rear-engine layout came from leftover Beetle parts in a sawmill, the 911’s engine from an aborted racing project, and fan devotion then locked those accidents in. The difference: Arthur’s lock-ins often let an inferior technology win, while here what got locked in was the company’s most valuable asset.
  • Evolutionary biology · The handicap principle (Amotz Zahavi): Zahavi proposed that a signal is credible precisely because it is costly; the peacock’s burdensome tail proves it can afford one. Racing and the Carrera GT are Porsche’s tail. The difference: the peacock pays for its own tail, whereas Porsche lets a supercar made once every decade or so vouch for hundreds of thousands of SUVs, which looks more like a shared signal, and one open to free-riding.
  • Distributed systems · Deadlock: Porsche in 2008 was a process holding one resource while waiting on another: it held VW shares it could not sell without crashing the price, and it needed credit it could not get. The system could not resolve itself; it took an outside actor with higher privileges, Volkswagen and Piëch, to kill one side and restore progress.
  • Philosophy · The Ship of Theseus: the 911’s silhouette has survived since the 1960s while nearly every part inside has been replaced; the company is the same, with the operating company owned by VW and VW controlled by the Porsche family. The episode ends up asking which one is “Porsche”, the same question as whether a ship with every plank replaced is still the same ship.

Ten takeaways

  1. In the early 1980s Porsche CEO Peter Schutz extended the 911’s product line onto his engineering chief’s office wall, saving a car slated for retirement.
  2. The cost of Schutz keeping the 911 alive was that Porsche carried three aging product lines into the late 1980s and nearly had to sell itself.
  3. After the war Volkswagen paid Porsche a royalty on every Beetle, and that steady cash let Porsche fund racing and other bets with no clear return.
  4. Acquired argues that high post-war West German taxes pushed Porsche to reinvest its profits rather than pay them out.
  5. In 1970 the Porsche and Piëch families withdrew from management entirely, and Ferdinand Piëch went on to run Audi and later Volkswagen Group.
  6. Wendelin Wiedeking rescued Porsche with the Toyota Production System and a retreat to the 911 alone, saying Porsche’s entry-level car is a used Porsche.
  7. The Boxster shared its front end and interior with the 911, so Porsche’s entry car stopped looking second-class and Porsche shared parts with itself.
  8. The Cayenne could have drained Porsche’s brand, but supercars like the Carrera GT, built in the same plant, paid that brand equity back.
  9. In 2008 Porsche SE borrowed heavily to take control of Volkswagen, got stuck after the short squeeze, was bought by VW instead, and the families came out the biggest winners.
  10. Through the lens of path dependence, Porsche’s rear-engine layout and 911 engine began as accidents that fan devotion locked into constraints even a CEO could not remove.
