Roger Penrose: spacetime ends, the universe cycles, and consciousness comes from collapse

Roger Penrose × Sean Carroll · Episode 28: Roger Penrose on Spacetime, Consciousness, and the Universe

Show Mindscape · Episode 28 · 2019-01-07 · Length 95 min · Guest Roger Penrose · Host Sean Carroll · Source YouTube

The moment

As a young pure-mathematics student at Cambridge, Penrose sat in on Dirac's quantum mechanics course. He remembers the first lecture being about the superposition principle: a thing can be in two places at once. Dirac had a piece of chalk, and Penrose thinks he broke it, as an image of "here and also there." Penrose's mind wandered, and when it came back, Dirac had moved on to something about energy.

"I think it's just as well I didn't hear his explanation, because it might have calmed me down."— Roger Penrose · [01:08:32]

He has worried about it ever since: a neutron can be in two places at once, so why not a cricket ball? Sixty-odd years later, sitting across from Sean Carroll at 87, Penrose had followed that worry to a very distant conclusion: our consciousness comes from the moment a quantum state makes up its own mind.

Who's talking, and when

Roger Penrose is an Oxford mathematical physicist. Carroll opens with a long list of things named after him, from Penrose diagrams to Penrose tilings, and he has written heavyweight popular books such as The Emperor's New Mind. The episode was recorded in Southern California because Penrose had just launched an institute bearing his name; his collaborator on consciousness, the anesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff, was sitting in the room.

Sean Carroll was then a theoretical physicist at Caltech and a champion of the Many-Worlds interpretation; this site has covered his lectures on gravity and quantum mechanics. In the intro he admits he disagrees with many of Penrose's ideas. But as a young cosmologist he also disagreed with Penrose about the entropy of the early universe, later realized Penrose was completely right, and says that shaped his career. So this is not a fan meeting a hero; it is two colleagues who overlap and diverge, comparing notes.

The episode came out in January 2019. Three months later the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black hole. In 2020 Penrose received half the Nobel Prize in Physics, "for the discovery that black hole formation is a robust prediction of the general theory of relativity"; the other half went to Genzel and Ghez, who tracked exactly the stars Penrose mentions here, circling an unseen heavy object at the center of the Milky Way.

The episode, in depth

1. Proving spacetime ends, without symmetry

In 1939 Oppenheimer and his student Snyder found an exact solution for a collapsing cloud of dust that looks like what we now call a black hole. Many people were unconvinced. The dust had no pressure and was perfectly spherical, so all the matter fell straight at the center, and infinite density there was no surprise. Real stars are irregular; perhaps the matter would swirl past the center and fly back out. Quasars made the question urgent: they outshine whole galaxies yet are not much bigger than the solar system.

Penrose changed technique. Instead of hunting for a symmetric exact solution, he drew conclusions from very general properties. He gives Carroll an optical picture: gravity acts on a parallel beam of light like a lens. In empty space it is an astigmatic lens, focusing in one direction and defocusing in the other, which is why someone falling into a black hole gets spaghettified. But put two such lenses at right angles and pull them apart, and the net effect is focusing. Gravity only ever draws light rays together. Combine that with topology and you can show that once a point of no return is passed, the focusing is irreversible and spacetime must come to an end somewhere.

He is careful to say the proof shows only that spacetime cannot be continued, not that curvature necessarily becomes infinite. To call the result a black hole you need an extra assumption of his own, cosmic censorship: generic singularities are hidden behind horizons. That conjecture is still not fully proven.

2. The mammoth in the microwave background

The second thread is the argument that redirected Carroll's career. The second law says entropy increases with time, so going back in time it should get lower. Yet the earliest universe we can see directly, the cosmic microwave background (CMB), light released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, has a spectrum that fits Planck's blackbody curve almost perfectly, and blackbody radiation is what maximum entropy looks like.

"So here, this is what I call the mammoth in the room."— Roger Penrose · [00:33:11]

The answer lies in gravity. For a box of ordinary gas, uniform is the highest-entropy state; for gravity it is the reverse. Uniformly spread matter is low entropy: it clumps, forms stars, and finally collapses into black holes, and entropy shoots up along the way. In the early universe matter and radiation were as disordered as they could be, but the gravitational degrees of freedom were almost untouched. The Sun is a hot spot in a cold sky; plants photosynthesize from it and we live off plants, all spending a low-entropy deposit left at the beginning of the universe.

So the question becomes: why did the universe begin that way? Many cosmologists look to inflation. Penrose's first reaction:

"I thought this is a crazy idea, it won't last a week. How wrong I was."— Roger Penrose · [00:37:58]

What he got wrong was only how long it would stay popular; in the next breath he says it is completely wrong. His reason: for inflation to work the universe already has to be fairly uniform, so it cannot explain the low-entropy start. Carroll sides with him on that, but still gives inflation a 50% chance of being part of the final answer. Penrose says that is a lot more than he gives it; Carroll adds that it is a lot lower than his colleagues give it.

3. A cyclic universe grown out of boredom

Penrose's own answer is conformal cyclic cosmology. He admits the starting point is almost an emotional argument. The universe expands ever faster and thinner; even black holes eventually evaporate by Hawking radiation, the big ones after roughly a googol years, and then comes endless tedium. But who would be bored? What remains is mostly photons, and a photon experiences zero time between its creation and infinity. For anything massless, the infinite future is just a boundary you can pass through.

He compares it to Escher's Circle Limit: the angels and devils shrink toward the rim, yet to themselves they are all the same size. A picture that preserves shape but not scale is called conformal, and massless light cannot tell the difference. Penrose squashes the infinite future down to such a boundary and stretches the Big Bang out. Near the Big Bang temperatures were so high that particles were effectively massless and equally blind to scale. Glue the two ends together, and the infinite future of a previous "aeon" becomes our Big Bang.

"I can go on talking about this and nobody will ever prove me wrong."— Roger Penrose · [00:53:56]

That was his joke in early lectures; later he found something testable. When supermassive black holes at the centers of galaxy clusters evaporate in the previous aeon, their entire mass would be concentrated at one point on that boundary, come through, and leave a hot spot a few degrees across in the CMB. He calls these Hawking points, and says he and his Polish collaborators have seen them in Planck satellite data; the claim check below looks at that number. He also argues this is a problem for inflation: anything from early in inflation would be stretched enormously, so only something happening just as inflation ended could leave such a small spot.

4. From Dirac's chalk to microtubules

The last half hour returns to that piece of chalk. Penrose argues there is a basic conflict between general relativity's equivalence principle and quantum mechanics' superposition principle. A lump in two places at once has two different gravitational fields; treat them Einstein's way and each position has a different vacuum, as if you needed two quantum field theories at once. He estimates this mismatch as an energy uncertainty and, borrowing from unstable nuclei, where greater energy uncertainty means a shorter lifetime, concludes that a superposition will pick one location by itself within some time, whether or not anyone is looking. He is explicit that this is a modification of quantum mechanics, not a completion.

The other road runs through a logic course he took in the same years. Gödel showed that for any mechanically checkable proof system you trust, you can construct a statement you must also believe true, yet the system cannot prove.

"I thought, gosh, this means as far as I can make out that our abilities to understand things are not a computation."— Roger Penrose · [01:22:38]

Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell's equations and general relativity can all be put on a computer; the only gap he could find was the seemingly random step in quantum measurement. The two roads meet there:

"It's not that our consciousness creates the collapse, it's the collapse that creates our consciousness."— Roger Penrose · [01:25:44]

He admits he knew little neurobiology when writing The Emperor's New Mind and made up the ending without really believing it. Then Hameroff wrote to him about nanoscale tubes inside cells, microtubules. Their view now is that every collapse is accompanied by a moment of proto-consciousness, the building blocks from which consciousness is made. Carroll asks whether this addresses Chalmers' hard problem; Penrose says Chalmers wasn't persuaded, and that whether it does is up to you.

Claim check

Penrose says the Hawking points he and his Polish collaborators found in Planck data reach a 99.98% confidence level. [01:02:23]

Insufficient evidence

That figure holds only for one fixed choice of ring size. When Jow and Scott re-ran the analysis in 2020 treating ring size as a parameter to search over, paying for the look-elsewhere effect, the significance fell to 87%, barely more than 1σ.

From Gödel's theorem, Penrose concludes that human understanding is "not a computation." [01:22:38]

Opinion, not fact

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's Gödel entry says there is wide consensus that such arguments fail. The Gödel sentence is true only if the system is consistent, and humans cannot always see whether a system is consistent.

Penrose says gravity makes superpositions collapse on their own, and experiments to test it are close. [01:17:29]

Insufficient evidence

No experiment has seen such a collapse. In 2020 a germanium detector at Italy's Gran Sasso underground lab looked for the faint radiation the mechanism should produce, found none, and ruled out the parameter-free version based on Penrose's own estimate. Versions with a wider parameter survive.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · FLP impossibility: Penrose's singularity theorem plays the same game as Fischer, Lynch and Paterson's 1985 FLP result: don't simulate a particular system, assume as little as possible, and prove some failure is unavoidable. FLP says that in an asynchronous network, if even one node may crash, no deterministic consensus algorithm can guarantee termination. Both also say only that something goes wrong, not what. The difference: engineers sidestepped FLP with randomization and timeouts; spacetime has no timeout to set, and we have to trust cosmic censorship to keep the singularities hidden.
  • Theory of computation · Turing's "mathematical objection": In "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), Turing already listed the Gödel-style rebuttal as "the mathematical objection" and answered that machines do have such limits, but no one has proved humans don't. Penrose is trying to supply exactly that missing step, and he places it in the one part of physics that still lacks a theory.
  • Buddhism · momentariness: The Abhidharma tradition treats mind as a stream of discrete moments, each arising and ceasing. Penrose's proto-conscious moments likewise break consciousness into discrete events. The difference is that the momentariness doctrine comes from introspection and Penrose's from physics, and he stresses that a single moment has no purpose of its own.

Ten takeaways

  1. Roger Penrose's singularity theorem needs no symmetric exact solution; light-ray focusing plus topology show that collapse past a point forces spacetime to end.
  2. The singularity theorem shows only that spacetime cannot continue; calling it a black hole requires Penrose's still-unproven cosmic censorship conjecture.
  3. The CMB spectrum looks like maximum entropy, but for gravity uniform matter is low entropy, which Penrose calls the mammoth in the room.
  4. The low entropy life depends on traces back to untouched gravitational degrees of freedom in the early universe; the Sun is just a hot spot in a cold sky.
  5. Penrose argues inflation cannot explain the early universe's low entropy, because inflation only works if the universe is already fairly uniform.
  6. Conformal cyclic cosmology holds that, under a scale-blind conformal rescaling, the infinite future of a previous aeon joins smoothly onto our Big Bang.
  7. Penrose predicts that supermassive black holes evaporating in a previous aeon leave Hawking points, hot spots a few degrees across, in the CMB.
  8. Penrose sees a conflict between the equivalence principle and superposition, so a massive superposition collapses on its own in finite time, observer or not.
  9. Penrose infers from Gödel's incompleteness theorem that understanding is not computation and ties consciousness to collapse; Hameroff points to microtubules.
  10. Penrose's singularity theorem and the FLP impossibility result in distributed systems both use minimal assumptions to prove a failure is unavoidable.
