Betting the company isn't a gamble: Jensen Huang pulls tomorrow's risk into today

Jensen Huang × Ben Gilbert, David Rosenthal · "NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang"

Show Acquired · 2023-10-16 · Length 90 min · Guest Jensen Huang · Hosts Ben Gilbert, David Rosenthal · Video YouTube

The moment

Ten minutes in, the hosts have already told half of Jensen Huang's 1997 story for him. The company has a few months of cash left. RIVA 128 is tested entirely in simulation, no physical prototype ever arrives, and the production run is ordered sight unseen with the rest of the money. Then they offer the obvious moral: so the lesson for founders is, when you have conviction, push all your chips in? It has worked for you every time.

Huang says no. When he pushes his chips in, he says, he already knows it will work. They could assume the chip was perfect because the whole chip had been emulated first, and the entire software stack, every driver and every game they owned had already run on it. The hosts heard a gamble. He describes homework that was finished before the bet was placed.

Who's talking, and when

Acquired is usually Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal taking turns narrating a company's history, with the protagonist nowhere in the room. This time it's reversed. They say they spent about 500 hours researching NVIDIA over two years and three episodes, then flew to NVIDIA headquarters and sat down across from the man himself. NVIDIA was worth $1.1 trillion at the time of recording, and the hosts put the question up front: how long can this last?

October 2023 was the first year after ChatGPT set off the scramble for compute. H100s were scarce, and one of the episode's own sponsor reads is about the GPU shortage. NVIDIA had just turned thirty.

The episode also covers his forty-plus direct reports and the idea that "mission is the boss"; this site's Jensen Huang series · flat organization has already written that up, so this page sticks to the three lines in the episode that carry a full argument.

The episode, unpacked

One tape-out: do the future's risky work now

RIVA 128 was a reset. NVIDIA's earliest chips used forward texture mapping and rendered curved surfaces rather than triangles, which let them skip the Z-buffer. Huang admits that turned out to be completely the wrong answer. By the time they realized it, Microsoft had shipped DirectX, which was fundamentally incompatible with NVIDIA's architecture, and thirty competitors had shown up.

He calls it probably NVIDIA's best moment, because with its back against the wall the company made a run of good decisions. First, don't fight DirectX; build the best chip in the world for it. The whole 3D pipeline was hardware-accelerated, the die was as big as anyone had imagined, the memory was the fastest available, and the price sat above anything a competitor would dare charge. The bet was on enthusiasts who always want the fastest thing, and on the fact that 3D graphics is never good enough, so the technology always has room to improve.

Second, buy an emulator. When he called a company that made hardware emulators, it was shutting down for lack of customers, and he bought its inventory. The arithmetic explains why. The normal cycle is tape out (send the design to the fab), get silicon back, write the software, find the bugs, tape out again. Run that loop and the money is gone. With six months and one tape-out, the chip has to be perfect. When his leaders asked how he knew it would be, he said:

I know it's going to be perfect because if it's not, we'll be out of business. And so let's make it perfect.— Jensen Huang · [00:08:07]

The software team wrote the whole stack, ran it on the emulator, and sat in the lab watching Windows paint, at something like an hour a frame by his recollection. Because they assumed the chip was perfect, on tape-out day they went straight to production and a marketing blitz, with no path back. That is the reasoning behind his "no":

When you bet the farm, you're saying I'm going to take everything in the future, all the risky things, and going to pull it in advance.— Jensen Huang · [00:10:55]

He says NVIDIA still designs chips this way, prefetching anything that can be simulated today. The logic is plain: why tape out seven times if you can do it once? Time to market is performance.

Reasoning back from AlexNet: ask why it works

The hosts ask whether the years poured into CUDA were spent ahead of demand. Yes and no, he says. Around 2012 NVIDIA was trying to make CUDA a good computer vision platform, and most vision algorithms were a poor fit for it. Then AlexNet appeared. When a new algorithm leapfrogs thirty years of computer vision work, his reaction is to step back and ask: why? Does it scale? And if it scales, what else can it solve?

His reasoning goes in steps. With enough examples you can teach this function to predict. The dimensionality can be as high as you like, and the network can be stacked very deep. That amounts to a universal function approximator. Then come the homely examples: he doesn't care why you prefer this toothpaste, or why someone who buys a hot dog also buys ketchup and mustard; he only cares that he can predict it. Weather too: what people really want to know is whether to wear a sweater. A huge share of the world's problems need prediction rather than causality, and that market is enormous. So, he concluded, almost all software would eventually be written this way, and how you build a computer and a chip could change completely.

What followed was execution. CUDA's earliest users were researchers, so NVIDIA went looking for every AI researcher on the planet and asked how it could help. That is how he met Yann LeCun, Andrew Ng and Geoffrey Hinton, and first met Ilya Sutskever at a conference. He remembers that when he first met Ian Goodfellow, a GAN produced a blurry 32-by-32 cat. When NVIDIA built the first DGX, he delivered it to OpenAI himself.

Standing under the tree: data centers, Mellanox, and a network that isn't a moat

NVIDIA's road to the data center started, he says, about seventeen years earlier. He worried that a GPU plugged into a PC, which has to sit next to a monitor, would cap the company someday. Then an engineer showed him a demo: capture the frame buffer, encode it as video, stream it to another device. That became GeForce Now, NVIDIA's first data center product. The second was remote graphics in enterprise data centers; the third was CUDA plus GPUs as a supercomputer. By the time AI arrived, NVIDIA already knew how to build data center computers. He calls this standing under the tree:

how do I position the company to be near it, to be just standing kind of near under the tree, and we can do a diving catch when the apple falls— Jensen Huang · [00:38:01]

Buying Mellanox was the next step on the same path, for two reasons. First, desktops and data centers use nearly the same CPUs and GPUs; what defines a data center is not the processor but the networking, the infrastructure, how computing is distributed and how security is provided. Second, cloud computing grew up as hyperscale, virtualizing many users onto commodity machines, and off-the-shelf Ethernet is fine for Hadoop and search. AI is the opposite:

AI is really about distributed computing where one job, one training job, is orchestrated across millions of processors. And so it's the inverse of hyperscale almost.— Jensen Huang · [00:41:02]

The hosts turn to moats. If you create a market and take care to help the developers and customers around you succeed, he says, you end up with a network of networks. But he dislikes the word:

I don't love thinking about it in the context of a moat, and the reason for that is because you're now focused on building stuff around your castle.— Jensen Huang · [00:49:20]

Holding that network together is one hard rule. He says NVIDIA has had a unified architecture called UDA since day one; it is the UDA in CUDA. The insight was that chips people paid for to run DirectX and OpenGL could build an installed base for CUDA on the side, as long as every chip was compatible:

That's the only unnegotiable rule in our company. Everything else is negotiable.— Jensen Huang · [00:54:05]

The final question: if you were thirty today, sitting in a Denny's with your two best friends, what company would you start? He wouldn't start one. Building NVIDIA turned out a million times harder than anyone expected, and if they had known the pain, the embarrassment and the vulnerability, nobody in their right mind would have done it. An entrepreneur's superpower is not knowing how hard it is, and asking only how hard it can be.

And to this day, I trick my brain into thinking, how hard can it be?— Jensen Huang · [01:20:42]

Read it next to the first thread and it is a curious pairing: with chips, he insists on rehearsing every future risk in advance; with himself, he deliberately refuses to rehearse how hard the future will be.

Fact check

"We are the only accelerator on the planet where every single accelerator is architecturally compatible with the others … at 30 years it's all completely compatible." [00:52:34]

Supported, but overstated

NVIDIA's own CUDA programming guide says PTX can always be compiled for equal or newer architectures, while binaries are only forward-compatible within a major version. The CUDA 12.0 release notes remove Kepler support outright. Compatibility runs one way: old programs run on new cards, new toolchains don't keep old cards. And CUDA itself launched in 2006, not thirty years ago.

The hosts say many people call Mellanox one of the best tech acquisitions ever; Huang says "I think so too," calling it one of the best strategic decisions he ever made. [00:41:02]

Supported

Per NVIDIA's announcement, the deal closed in April 2020 at a $7 billion transaction value. NVIDIA's SEC-filed results put networking revenue at $8.575 billion in fiscal 2024 and $12.99 billion in fiscal 2025. Those two years of networking revenue alone come to roughly three times the purchase price, though that is revenue, not profit.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · FoundationDB's deterministic simulation: FoundationDB runs the same code in production and in deterministic simulation, tens of thousands of simulations a night, with the goal of finding problems in simulation rather than in the real world. Structurally it is the RIVA 128 move: drag failures that would only surface in the future into a world you can replay. The difference is that a database bug can be fixed before release, while a taped-out chip can't be changed, so Huang's emulation wasn't an extra safety net; there was no second chance.
  • Statistics · Breiman's "two cultures": In 2001 Leo Breiman published "Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures" in Statistical Science: one culture assumes the data come from a stochastic model, the other treats the mechanism as unknown and judges only predictive accuracy. Huang's toothpaste and ketchup stand squarely in the second. Where it breaks: a model that only predicts has an old weakness, since once the world is intervened on, with a price change or a rule change, it may stop being right, and none of Huang's examples involve intervention.
  • Mathematics · the universal approximation theorem: In 1989 Cybenko proved that single-hidden-layer networks with sigmoid activations can approximate any continuous function arbitrarily well, which is where Huang's "universal function approximator" comes from. But these theorems only prove existence; they don't say how large the network must be or how to find the weights. The real news from AlexNet was not "it can approximate" but "with GPUs and lots of data, you can actually find it."

Ten takeaways

  1. NVIDIA's earliest chips used forward texture mapping to render curved surfaces; Jensen Huang admits that was the wrong road, and RIVA 128 was a reset of the company.
  2. To tape out only once, Jensen Huang bought the inventory of a failing emulator company and ran the entire software stack in emulation before tape-out.
  3. Jensen Huang denies RIVA 128 was a gamble: to him, betting the farm means pulling every risky future task into the present and finishing it.
  4. After AlexNet, Jensen Huang asked why it worked and whether it scaled, and concluded that almost all software would come to be written by learning from examples.
  5. Jensen Huang argues that many problems need prediction rather than causality, citing toothpaste preference, ketchup with hot dogs and the weather.
  6. NVIDIA's data center path began with GeForce Now cloud gaming, then remote graphics, then CUDA supercomputers, so it knew data centers before AI arrived.
  7. Jensen Huang bought Mellanox because networking defines a data center, and AI training spreads one job across huge numbers of processors, the inverse of hyperscale.
  8. Jensen Huang dislikes the word "moat" and prefers to see NVIDIA as a network in which developers and customers succeed together.
  9. Jensen Huang says CUDA compatibility across every NVIDIA chip is the company's only non-negotiable rule.
  10. FoundationDB's deterministic simulation shares a structure with RIVA 128's single tape-out: both drag future failures into a world that can be replayed.
