A Life Engineered · 2025

Management is a door you can walk back through. The door for new grads is narrowing.

Gergely Orosz × Steve Huynh · "Gergely Orosz on Tech's Entry-Level Crisis and What Comes Next"

Show A Life Engineered · 2025-08-01 · Length 44 min · Guest Gergely Orosz · Host Steve Huynh · Video YouTube

The moment

When Gergely Orosz interviewed at Uber, he thought he was interviewing for mobile; he had been writing iOS at Skyscanner. Someone mixed things up and put him on the backend loop instead. He shrugged, since he had done backend before, did well, and got a backend offer.

Two weeks before his start date, his manager asked whether he could do Android instead. He couldn't be told why, only that it was urgent. He had done Windows Phone and iOS, never Android, and said yes anyway. [00:07:05] So on day one, Uber's new senior Android engineer walked in carrying an Android book. The mentor he was assigned, one level below him, took one look and asked what on earth that book was. His answer: hear me out, I promise I'll learn very quickly.

It's a funny scene, but it touches the thread that runs through the whole episode: title, skill and what the company needs right now rarely line up, and most career decisions get made inside that mismatch.

Who's talking, and when

Gergely's own résumé is a string of such mismatches. He built trading software at JP Morgan and worked on Skype at Microsoft; at Skyscanner he became one of six or seven principal engineers in the company, which he felt was over-leveling; then he joined Uber as a senior engineer, a step down in title, and his pay doubled. He spent four years at Uber in Amsterdam, went from engineer to managing teams, then left to write The Pragmatic Engineer, now the top technology newsletter on Substack, and started his own podcast.

The host, Steve Huynh, is a former Amazon principal engineer with nearly twenty years in the industry. This episode turns the tables: Steve had recently been a guest on Gergely's podcast, and now Gergely sits on the answering side.

It came out in early August 2025. AI coding tools were at peak hype, "AI is like a junior engineer" had become a line many executives repeated, and new grads struggling to find work was an open anxiety across the industry. Shortly before, Gergely had interviewed GitHub CEO Thomas Dohmke on his own show, a conversation he comes back to when the talk turns to new grads.

The episode, unpacked

Management as a door you can walk back through

By the time he became a tech lead at Uber, Gergely had ten years of coding behind him across several stacks. He wasn't short of a path upward; the next level above senior would have come eventually. What made him hesitate was something else:

"I felt I was flatlining, but for management I knew I'm a baby. I know nothing."— Gergely Orosz · [00:07:38]

A flattening learning curve was the first reason. The second was that Uber made the experiment cheap: new managers spent six to twelve months as "apprentice managers" and could switch back to engineering at any point, and the company meant it. His advice follows from that: if you are five to ten years in and someone asks whether you want to manage, try it for a year or two. Going back to engineering after managing is easy; wanting to manage twenty years in, having never done it, is hard, and he has met plenty of senior engineers stuck and bitter there. He adds that such offers only appear at high-growth companies, and high growth is not the normal state of things.

Some of the best staff engineers he knew were, in fact, "failed managers":

"They went into management, they did it for a year, they give it their best shot, they hated it and they were not that good at it, but they came back to being an engineer."— Gergely Orosz · [00:10:05]

Back on the IC side they made the best partners a manager could have: they knew what the manager was dealing with, wanted to help, and had no interest whatsoever in the job.

The third reason was an old grievance. At Microsoft he had two managers at opposite extremes. The first said "good job" in every one-on-one, then gave him a middling review and couldn't name a single thing to improve; that, he realized, was a manager who didn't care. The second cared too much: when Gergely flagged a small chance his project might slip, along with a recovery plan, the manager took the project over on the spot. Gergely kept a list of the manager he never wanted to be. So when his turn came:

"If you have an opportunity like this and you say no, you have no right to complain about managers ever again, because doing the job is a lot harder."— Gergely Orosz · [00:14:09]

Once in the role, he found that most engineering managers have had almost no training; Uber's two-day internal course was more than most get.

How technical should a manager be: who wins the meeting

The question is an old one. Gergely thinks people who never coded can be good managers, but it is much harder. His evidence is his own team at Uber, which mixed backend and mobile engineers. He knew mobile well, but not distributed systems, and he had never written Python. So he sat in every architecture review and wrote down each term he didn't understand: strong versus weak consistency, why durability matters. A few months later he could argue in the room that a project should use AWS DynamoDB rather than the company's on-prem Cassandra, because Cassandra didn't give them strong consistency and changing that would be a long project. Other managers nodded and said they'd need to check with their tech lead.

"Guess what, guess who won the arguments?"— Gergely Orosz · [00:21:07]

Five years for new grads: three waves stacked

Steve then raises what he calls a demographic bomb: today's senior engineers were yesterday's juniors, so if the entry pipeline breaks, the industry will be short of seniors in five years.

Gergely answers with a timeline. When the pandemic hit and everyone went remote, companies had no idea how to onboard juniors, who had always learned by sitting next to experienced people, so they stopped hiring them. Just as lockdowns ended, the 2022–23 layoffs arrived as big companies realized they had over-hired. And once the cuts were done and business recovered, AI arrived, and "AI is like a junior engineer" gave leaders another reason to hold off. His conclusion: entry-level hiring has been down for five straight years. The first half of that timeline is checked against data below.

He adds an angle that is often missed. Big tech and startups can afford seniors; juniors historically came in through consultancies and agencies such as Accenture or Cognizant. An engineer at one such firm, about 50,000 people, told him the CEO said at an all-hands that their big clients had come to them almost in unison:

"We've heard you've said that, so we want to pay you 30% less for the same work."— Gergely Orosz, relaying the firm's clients · [00:27:17]

The biggest entry point for juniors is being bitten by its own efficiency story.

So what should a new grad do? Gergely gives one answer: build something and ship it. Use AI to help you make a web page or a React Native app and put it in the store. The two of them note that the bar is moving: an app alone is no longer remarkable, but earning one dollar from a stranger shows someone actually wants what you made. He also mentions GitHub: Dohmke told him GitHub is hiring more juniors, because young people use AI without a second thought. To Gergely, a company that builds AI tools hiring more juniors means it knows something.

Steve closes the topic with optimism: every step change in technology has increased the number of programmers. Gergely refines it: when high-level languages arrived, the number of people writing assembly didn't grow much; what grew was the number writing high-level languages. So he is "110%" sure more people will create code, though what we call programming may look different.

Which ledger the company keeps

Steve asks how a junior or mid-level engineer catches management's eye. Gergely's first step is always to crush the work you're assigned, then look at who the strongest engineers on the team are and what they actually do, and emulate it.

Then he tells on himself. At Skype he spoke at several conferences about how Skype was built, more talks than some staff engineers gave, and got "meets expectations." Only as a manager did he understand: those staff engineers spoke because the team couldn't hire enough platform engineers, so the talks were part of a recruiting plan; his talks grew him but didn't help the team. His conclusion isn't cynical: when the two diverge, it's fine to do things that only grow you, just don't expect the company to pay for them.

In the rapid-fire round, the idea he most wants to explore is energy, which he took from a conversation with Steve McConnell, author of Code Complete: a team with energy gets things done under any process, and no process saves a team without it. He asked people at OpenAI how they ship so fast, and they said there is a kind of electricity in the air. Then:

"I think we can see it with, let's say, you know, Google. I'm sure parts of it are coming back, but that light bulb went out."— Gergely Orosz · [00:42:50]

Steve gets the last word: good companies turn the work in front of them into a game everyone wants to win, the way people play Factorio for twenty-four hours straight.

Fact check

"The job market has been brutal for entry-level engineers since around 2020, right as COVID started." [00:23:58]

Supported, but overstated

Using ADP payroll data, Brynjolfsson and colleagues at Stanford find that in the occupations most exposed to AI (software development included), employment of 22–25-year-olds is 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed peers. But the same data show those young workers rising relatively in 2020, returning to pre-pandemic levels by November 2022, and declining only after that. The slump is real; it starts in late 2022, not 2020.

"Since 2013, Elon Musk has been saying that self-driving is one or two years out maximum." [00:38:49]

Supported

Wikipedia's sourced list of Musk's predictions starts in September 2013, when he said 90% of miles could be autonomous within three years; nearly every year since, he has put full autonomy "one or two years" out, and none arrived on schedule. The only gap: the 2013 forecast said three years, not one or two.

Crossovers

  • Economics · Jevons paradox: In 1865 William Stanley Jevons observed that as steam engines burned coal more efficiently, Britain's total coal use rose. Steve's "every step change brought more programmers" has the same shape. Gergely's refinement shows where it breaks: Jevons was about more of the same coal being burned, while programming history added a different kind of person. Whether today's efficiency gains reach today's new grads depends on whether they are that different kind.
  • Management · Bezos's two-way doors: In Amazon's 2015 shareholder letter, Bezos split decisions into irreversible one-way doors, to be made carefully, and reversible two-way doors, to be made fast. Uber's apprentice-manager program turned "should I manage?" from a one-way door into a two-way one. The difference is Gergely's caveat: the door swings both ways, but it is only open during high growth.
  • Fisheries ecology · recruitment failure: When one year's young fish fail to recruit into a population, that missing year class shows up again in the adult stock years later, even if every year after is normal. Steve's demographic bomb is the same mechanism: juniors not hired today become the missing seniors five to ten years out.
  • Psychology · flow: The flow state Csikszentmihalyi studied needs clear goals, immediate feedback, and challenge matched to skill. Factorio delivers all three, which may explain why repetitive work becomes addictive inside a game. Gergely's team "energy" may draw on the same source, but it also involves morale and trust; flow only explains the individual layer.

Ten takeaways

  1. Gergely Orosz moved into management because his technical learning curve had flattened while management was entirely new to him.
  2. Uber's apprentice managers could switch back to engineering within six to twelve months, so Gergely Orosz advises engineers five to ten years in to try managing for a year or two if offered.
  3. "Failed managers" who return to engineering often become a manager's best partners, because they understand the job and don't want it.
  4. The manager who doesn't care and the manager who micromanages are two common failure modes; the first leaves reports blindsided at review time.
  5. A manager who understands strong versus weak consistency can argue an architecture decision in the room instead of deferring to a tech lead.
  6. Gergely Orosz sees the entry-level hiring slump as three stacked waves: remote work, post-pandemic layoffs, and the "AI is like a junior engineer" narrative.
  7. Consultancies and agencies were the main entry point for juniors, and their clients now cite "30% AI efficiency" to demand 30% lower prices.
  8. When high-level languages arrived, assembly programmers didn't multiply; high-level programmers did, and Gergely Orosz expects AI to work the same way.
  9. A conference talk counts toward performance only when it helps the team or business; work that only grows you may go unrewarded.
  10. Bezos calls reversible decisions "two-way doors," and Uber's apprentice-manager program made moving into management one.
