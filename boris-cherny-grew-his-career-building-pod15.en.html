Build only what people already do: the career of Claude Code's creator

Boris Cherny × Ryan Peterman · Boris Cherny (Creator of Claude Code) On What Grew His Career And Building at Anthropic

Show The Peterman Pod · 2025-12-15 · Length 85 min · Guest Boris Cherny · Host Ryan Peterman · Video YouTube

The moment

Boris Cherny's first real product at Facebook put chat rooms inside Facebook Groups. The team was tiny and had no user researcher, so he improvised. At lunchtime he would carry the new feature down to the company cafeteria, hand the phone to one of the cafeteria workers, ask "can you figure out how to open a chat?", and then say nothing and watch where they got stuck. [00:02:57]

He taught the method to the rest of the team, and soon every engineer on it was going to lunch to pester the cafeteria staff. He calls it observational user research on no budget, and the engineers he most wants to hire today are still the ones who write code and also go find their users. The episode runs past eighty minutes, from four promotions at Meta to Claude Code, and one habit runs through all of it: look at what people actually do before you build.

Who's talking, and when

Cherny studied economics, dropped out to do startups, and was running his first company at eighteen. Before Facebook he wrote code at a hedge fund, and he wrote O'Reilly's Programming TypeScript. At Meta he rose from mid-level engineer to IC8 (Principal). Along the way, three months into a relationship, he followed his girlfriend to rural Japan when she found her dream job there, and moved from Facebook Groups to Instagram's Tokyo office. He says he had never even installed Instagram before he took the job.

Host Ryan Peterman is also ex-Meta. His show profiles senior engineers and likes to walk up the ladder one rung at a time asking what got you promoted, and the first half of this episode follows that pattern exactly. Judging from what comes up, it was recorded in late October 2025: Claude Code plugins had launched "a couple weeks ago" (October 9), and Karpathy's long interview had just aired. Claude Code had shipped as a research preview eight months earlier, in February 2025, and was by then something everyone at Anthropic used. The episode went up on December 15.

Glossary

  • Meta engineering levels: E4 is mid-level, E5 senior, E6 staff, E7 senior staff, E8 principal. IC means an individual contributor, someone who does not manage people.
  • Better Engineering: the axis of Meta's performance review for work that pays down tech debt or improves developer infrastructure rather than shipping product.
  • Comet: the full rewrite of Facebook's desktop site, from patched-together PHP pages to a single-page JavaScript app.

The episode, in depth

1. Latent demand: build only what people already do

Peterman asks him to explain latent demand, and Cherny calls it the single most important principle in product. His example is Facebook Marketplace. A large share of posts in Facebook Groups were people buying and selling things. Groups were not designed for commerce, but users bent them into a bazaar anyway, so first came buy-and-sell groups and then Marketplace.

I think the principle in product is you can never get people to do something they do not yet do.— Boris Cherny · [00:05:40]

What you can do is find the intent people already have and steer it toward an easier outlet. The interesting part is that he runs infrastructure work on the same logic. He couldn't wrap his head around Redux, so he wrote a simpler state-management library, Undux. At Facebook he found that the internal Redux group was full of people asking the questions he had once asked. He wrote a script to tally the people reporting problems by team, messaged each team's tech lead and manager, and over a few weeks biked around campus giving twenty to forty team-specific tech talks. In code review he kept a spreadsheet of the comments he made over and over; once the same kind of comment came up a few times, he wrote a lint rule for it, until the rules were doing most of his reviewing.

The thread lands back on Claude Code. He walked into the office one day and found the data scientist next to his team had installed Node.js by himself and was using Claude Code to write SQL and run analyses. Now half of Anthropic's sales team uses it too. This is not how we designed it, he says. By his own principle, that is exactly the signal for where the next product should grow.

2. Trust is earned, and titles don't help

The second thread is about where influence comes from. Meta hid levels, so even the most senior engineer's title was just "software engineer." Anthropic goes further: engineers, PMs and designers are all "member of technical staff." Peterman pushes back: without a title, how does someone across the company know how seriously to take you? Cherny grants the cost but thinks the upside outweighs it.

And in some ways, having a manager title makes it a little bit harder to earn this kind of trust.— Boris Cherny · [00:29:00]

The story behind his IC7 promotion shows how the trust gets earned. Public groups were changing so that people could participate without joining first, and Bob, the most senior engineer in the org, insisted that followers and members had to be stored separately in the data model, even though that meant migrating hundreds of call sites. Cherny disagreed but went along. Six months to a year later the work was done, and the audit showed that every call site had simply gained an if-else; the original field had been enough all along.

And so I pushed the same engineer that did this to then undo it.— Boris Cherny · [00:39:58]

Cherny says Bob came to trust him more as a technical leader because of it. His explanation is to disagree and commit first, build up trust, and only then will your objections carry weight. At Instagram he had to earn it all over again. To move the Python codebase onto Facebook's Hack stack, he went first to the infrastructure old-timers most likely to object, bought them beer in New York, and got to know them as people before talking about the technical problem. He also says he was lucky to be underleveled when he joined, because lower expectations gave him room to explore. Listeners of career shows might add one layer: this is the view looking back from IC8.

3. Build for the model six months from now

The third thread is Claude Code itself. Cherny says that back then, AI for coding meant tab autocomplete. It was his manager at the time, Ben, who pushed him to aim further. Ben had been at Anthropic since the beginning and had a gut feel for how fast the models were improving:

he actually pushed me really hard to be like don't build for the model of today build for the model 6 months from now.— Boris Cherny · [01:11:01]

So for a long time Claude Code was not a great product, and he used it for maybe ten percent of his own code. Then Claude 4 came out (he remembers it as March; it was May 2025), the product suddenly just worked, and half his code went to it. The price of the bet was half a year building something that didn't work yet; the wager was on the slope of model progress.

Peterman brings up Karpathy's criticism that vibe coding produces a lot of slop. Cherny doesn't argue; he describes the team's rule:

for the Claude Code team, we have the same exact bar regardless of whether the code was written by the model or by a human.— Boris Cherny · [01:12:59]

He describes three modes. Only throwaway prototypes get vibe-coded. Most of the time he aligns with the model on a plan in plan mode, then they revise the code together. And in the parts of the core query loop where he has strong opinions down to parameter names, he still writes by hand. He then adds something you might not expect from the person running the product:

the models I think are still overall not great at coding. I think there's still so much room to improve and this is the worst it's ever going to be.— Boris Cherny · [01:14:08]

He estimates that moving Facebook Groups onto Comet, which took twenty or thirty engineers about two years, might take five engineers six months today, and in another six months maybe one. His day now starts with launching a few agents from his phone in the morning and checking on them once he reaches a computer. As for Codex, he says he doesn't really use other products, and that watching competitors is a failure mode big companies fall into.

Fact check

Cherny says Anthropic tripled in size this year, yet productivity per engineer, measured by PRs, grew almost 70% because of Claude Code. [01:11:43]

Supported, but overstated

Anthropic's own report has the number: after Claude Code was adopted across engineering, merged PRs per engineer per day rose 67%. But it is a before-and-after comparison with no control group, PR count is not output, and the report itself warns its findings likely don't generalize to other companies.

Cherny says that with Opus 4 the risks went way up, to models that could be used to design things like bioviruses. [01:06:28]

Supported, but overstated

In May 2025 Anthropic did turn on ASL-3 protections for Opus 4, aimed first at biological weapons. But the announcement says it had "not yet determined" whether the model crossed the capability threshold, only that the risk could no longer be ruled out. The safeguards were a precaution, not a confirmed capability.

Cherny says Marketplace came from the observation that 40% of posts in Facebook Groups were buying and selling. [00:05:40]

Insufficient evidence

The origin story holds: Facebook's 2016 launch post says the activity "started in Facebook Groups," with more than 450 million people visiting buy-and-sell groups monthly. But no public source we found gives the 40% share of posts.

Crossovers

  • Urban planning · Desire paths: the diagonal shortcut worn into a lawn is pedestrians voting with their feet, and some planners wait for the paths to appear and then pave along them. That is Cherny's latent demand exactly: don't draw the path, watch where people walk. The difference is that a lawn can't be "abused" into something worse, while a product has to be designed to be hackable before the demand can show.
  • Machine learning · Ensemble disagreement: to merge the data models of Groups and Pages, Cherny split the org's tech leads into two teams and gave each three hours at a whiteboard. The two designs came out 80% the same, and the 20% where they differed marked exactly where the risk was. Deep ensembles estimate uncertainty the same way: where independently trained models agree you can relax, and where they disagree the model doesn't know. The difference is that models only tell you where they're unsure, while each engineering team arrives carrying a concrete solution that a technical spike can test.
  • Neuroscience · Synaptic pruning: Instagram's "unshipping" culture sets a usage bar for every feature and deletes the ones that miss it, because screen space is finite and features compete for it. The developing brain likewise prunes connections that go unused. Both filter by "is it being used" under scarce resources. The difference is that pruning happens automatically, while unshipping always leaves a small group of users angry about the feature they lost.
  • Logic · Curry–Howard correspondence: Cherny says the type signatures matter more than the code itself. Curry–Howard makes that rigorous: types are propositions and programs are proofs, so getting the signature right means stating the right theorem before proving it.
  • AI · Sutton's Bitter Lesson: Sutton argued in 2019 that general methods that ride growing compute eventually beat hand-crafted cleverness. "Build for the model six months from now" is the product version: don't patch around today's model's weaknesses.

Ten takeaways

  1. With no user researcher at Facebook, Boris Cherny took new features to the company cafeteria and watched the workers try them, as observational user research.
  2. Boris Cherny calls latent demand the first principle of product: you can't get people to do what they don't already do, only steer an intent they already have.
  3. Boris Cherny's example is Facebook Groups being bent into a place to buy and sell, which led to buy-and-sell groups and then Marketplace.
  4. To spread his state library Undux, Boris Cherny tallied by team who was complaining about Redux, then gave each team its own tech talk.
  5. Boris Cherny logged his repeated code-review comments in a spreadsheet and turned any recurring one into a lint rule, automating most of his reviews.
  6. Boris Cherny first carried out the migration senior engineer Bob wanted, then, once it proved unnecessary, had the same engineer undo it.
  7. Boris Cherny argues that at a company without titles trust must be earned through work, and a manager title can make it harder to earn.
  8. Boris Cherny's manager at the time, Ben, told him to design Claude Code for the model six months out, not the model of today.
  9. The Claude Code team applies one merge bar to model-written and human-written code; if the code is bad, the model is asked to improve it.
  10. Boris Cherny had two teams design the same system separately and used their disagreement to locate risk, the same structure ensembles use to estimate uncertainty.
