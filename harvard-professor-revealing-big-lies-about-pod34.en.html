We evolved to take the elevator, and our bodies repair themselves on the stairs

Daniel Lieberman × Steven Bartlett · "Harvard Professor: REVEALING The 7 Big LIES About Exercise, Sleep, Running, Cancer & Sugar!!!"

Show The Diary of a CEO · 2023-07-10 · Length 89 min · Guest Daniel Lieberman · Host Steven Bartlett · Episode YouTube

The moment

In the mountains of northwestern Mexico, Daniel Lieberman was interviewing a Tarahumara man in his seventies. The Tarahumara are famous for long-distance running, and this man was a famous runner among them. Lieberman asked how he trained, and the translator got stuck: the language has no word for training. After a long detour she got the question across, and even through translation Lieberman could hear the tone of the answer:

why would anybody run if you didn't have to?— Lieberman, conveying the old man's tone · [00:05:01]

That was when it clicked for him that exercise is a strange thing. If you farm all day without machines, or walk ten miles a day for food and still go hungry, why on earth would you go for a pointless five-mile run in the morning? The episode covers sleep, step counts, cancer, running shoes and weight loss, but almost every topic grows out of that question: a body built for necessary work, dropped into a world where nothing is necessary.

Who's talking, and when

Daniel Lieberman is a human evolutionary biologist at Harvard. He started out studying skulls, drifted toward the evolution of running and the link between physical activity and disease, and has done fieldwork in Kenya for about fifteen years. He counts himself part of "evolutionary medicine." The most telling detail about him: the day before the recording, flying from Denver to Boston, he watched everyone in the airport queue for the escalator next to an empty staircase, and ran up the stairs.

Steven Bartlett's Diary of a CEO tends to pull every topic back to the host's own life. Here he brings his out-of-shape father in his sixties, his own plantar fasciitis and the insoles a podiatrist gave him, and a WhatsApp group of ten friends where whoever logs the fewest workouts each month gets evicted and a new member is raffled in. They have kept it going for three and a half years.

The conversation was recorded in New York and went up in July 2023. It is about Lieberman's 2020 book Exercised, which lists ten myths about exercise. The "seven big lies" in the title are the show's framing: cancer and sugar are not debunked as lies here, they are Lieberman's examples of why activity matters. In 2021 he turned the book's core idea into a PNAS paper, the "active grandparent hypothesis."

The episode, worked through

Age is not the cause; inactivity is

The most important myth, Lieberman says, is that it is normal to become less active as you get older. He starts with a distinction: aging is just the clock ticking, and nobody can stop it; senescence is the way the body wears down over time, and that can be slowed.

His argument comes from a strange feature of human life history. We live roughly twenty years after we stop reproducing, which almost no other animal does, killer whales being the possible exception. His explanation is that humans evolved to be grandparents, and grandparents in the past did not retire to play golf. They worked in fields, foraged and helped raise children. That activity switches on a suite of repair and maintenance processes: it keeps muscles strong, limits DNA mutations, keeps mitochondria numbers up, and keeps "gunk" from accumulating in the brain. So he reverses the usual order:

But really it's the absence of physical activity, which lets aging run amok.— Daniel Lieberman · [00:16:07]

His favorite evidence is Ralph Paffenbarger's long follow-up of Harvard alumni. As Lieberman tells it, alumni in their twenties to forties who exercised four or five times a week had about 20% lower death rates; by their sixties and seventies the gap had grown to 50%. Exercise matters more with age, not less.

Bartlett brings up his father: on a 300-meter staircase in Indonesia he realized his dad could no longer manage it, and he had assumed that was genetics. Lieberman answers with a saying from his field, that genes load the gun and environment pulls the trigger. Medical students, he adds, are taught that blood pressure rises with age, which is only true in sedentary Western populations eating poor diets:

What we've done is we've confused diseases that are more common with aging with age being a cause of those diseases in the first place.— Daniel Lieberman · [00:26:50]

The line lands on one concrete change in his own life. He has always loved running and hated weights, and the more he read while writing the book, the more he kicked himself. Now he does at least two strength sessions a week, because sarcopenia, age-related muscle loss, makes people frail, which makes them move less, which feeds the loss. Asked whether we shouldn't retire, he says retirement is a modern Western invention and we do pay a price for it, but you can replace work with other engaging things. The point is not to stop moving.

People move only when it is necessary or rewarding

If the body needs activity, why do we resist it so hard? Because taking it easy is not a flaw, Lieberman says, it is an instinct. When food was scarce, every bit of saved energy was a better chance of surviving. He compresses this into one rule:

people evolved to be physically active for two reasons and two reasons only. When it's necessary or rewarding.— Daniel Lieberman · [00:24:39]

Modern life has removed almost all of the necessity, which leaves reward, and reward is exactly what beginners get least of. Lieberman says that when he runs in Central Park he gets a big dopamine hit, but someone unfit and overweight running the same loop does not; by his account it can take months or years before that reward arrives. So he keeps asking for compassion toward people who struggle, and he starts with himself. His office is on the fifth floor of an old building, and every day he wants the elevator:

It's not that I don't want to take the elevator. I do want to take the elevator, right?— Daniel Lieberman · [01:17:25]

The only reason he takes the stairs is that being caught in the elevator would make him a hypocrite. The joke is also his program: if people move only out of necessity or reward, manufacture both, and the strongest source of both is other people. He has a grant to study how people become exercisers, and his bet is that it's social. He meets a friend, Aaron, for 6 a.m. runs; the night before it seems like a great idea, the next morning he wants to stay in bed, but he agreed, so out he goes. A friend of his signed a stickK commitment contract with her husband as referee: any day she misses her walking target, $50 goes to the National Rifle Association, which she loathes. The NRA hasn't received a penny.

The hardest version of "necessary" is the Swedish sportswear company Björn Borg, whose CEO requires everyone, from the mail room to visiting board members, to join a sports hour every Friday at 11. Lieberman visited; some people had quit over it, but most who stayed said it was a good thing. Bartlett pushes back: requiring staff to read a book seems fine, requiring them to run feels like overreach.

The bill for comfort

The third line is the central concept of evolutionary medicine: mismatch disease, a condition that is more common or more severe because our bodies are poorly adapted to modern environments. The example on the table is Bartlett's own foot.

He had plantar fasciitis and was prescribed insoles. Lieberman's diagnosis: you train every muscle group except your feet. The arch is held up by four layers of foot muscles; beneath them is a sheet of connective tissue, the plantar fascia, which has almost no blood supply, so once it is overstretched and inflamed it heals poorly. Stiff-soled shoes with arch support are comfortable precisely because the foot muscles do less work, and so they weaken. Insoles relieve the pain without touching the cause. Lieberman calls this loop of treating symptoms rather than causes "dysevolution," and jokes that podiatrists are a bit like drug pushers, since you end up needing the insoles for life. His advice is not extreme, though: wear the insoles while it hurts, strengthen the foot, and move to minimal shoes slowly. Switch too fast and the fasciitis comes roaring back. Both men turned out to be wearing the same barefoot shoes.

Running follows the same logic. In a 2010 Nature paper, Lieberman's lab found that habitually barefoot runners tend to land on the forefoot and avoid the impact peak that comes with heel-striking in cushioned shoes. But nothing is free, he says: forefoot striking shifts load onto the calves and Achilles tendon, and people who switch abruptly get hurt there. What matters most is not to overstride: land with the shin vertical, at roughly 170 to 180 steps a minute.

The other face of comfort is the idea that more is always better. Asked about the influencer "Liver King" and his physique, Lieberman says hunter-gatherers look nothing like that. Muscle is about a third of the body and burns a fifth or more of our calories even at rest; for someone near the edge of food security, surplus muscle is a liability, which is why the body uses it or loses it. His summary:

We love comfort, but comfort's not necessarily good for us.— Daniel Lieberman · [00:55:55]

Claim check

Lieberman says it is "absolutely definitively not true that running increases rates of knee cartilage damage and arthritis," and that running may even be slightly protective. [01:01:38]

Supported, but overstated

Alentorn-Geli et al. (2017) pooled 17 studies of about 115,000 people: hip and knee osteoarthritis in 3.5% of recreational runners versus 10.2% of sedentary controls. But competitive and elite runners came in at 13.3%, above controls. All of it is observational, and the authors say causation can't be determined. True for recreational runners; "absolutely" goes too far.

Lieberman says women who get 150 minutes of activity a week have on average a 30 to 50% lower lifetime breast cancer risk than sedentary women. [00:30:34]

Supported, but overstated

Right direction, several times too big. Moore et al. (2016), pooling 12 cohorts and 1.44 million people, found only 10% lower breast cancer risk at the 90th versus the 10th percentile of activity. In Kyu et al.'s (2016) dose-response analysis, even activity at more than ten times the recommended minimum cut risk by just 14%.

Lieberman says people without electricity sleep six to seven hours a night, that sleep duration and mortality follow a U-shaped curve bottoming out around seven hours, and that the eight-hour rule is nonsense. [00:08:15]

Supported

Yetish et al. (2015) put monitors on people in three pre-industrial societies and measured 5.7 to 7.1 hours of actual sleep a night. Cappuccio et al. (2010), pooling 1.38 million people, found 12% higher mortality for short sleepers and 30% for long sleepers. Some of the long-sleep risk likely reflects existing illness, a caveat Lieberman raises himself.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Autoscaling: "Use it or lose it" is capacity scaled to load. On Lieberman's account muscle is expensive reserved capacity, burning a fifth or more of resting calories, so the body scales it down the moment the load disappears, which is exactly how a cloud bill works. Where it differs is scale-up speed: a cloud fleet can be brought back in minutes, but the body's scale-up gets slower with age. Sarcopenia is, at bottom, scale-down outrunning scale-up.
  • Game theory · Thomas Schelling on self-command: The stickK contract that sends money to the hated NRA is the kind of commitment device Schelling discussed in "Egonomics": tonight's self removes options from tomorrow morning's self. It works because the referee is her husband, not herself; that is what makes the commitment credible.
  • Buddhism · Māna (conceit): At the end, Lieberman says his biggest source of friction is comparing himself to others: winning breeds pride, losing breeds envy. Buddhism names three forms of conceit, "I am better," "I am equal," "I am worse," and counts even "I am the same as him" among them. Lieberman sees the emotions comparison produces; Buddhism goes one layer down and asks about the "I" being compared.

Ten takeaways

  1. Daniel Lieberman found that the Tarahumara language in Mexico has no word for training, which convinced him that exercising for its own sake is a strange modern habit.
  2. Lieberman distinguishes aging, the passage of time, from senescence, the body's wear, and argues that physical activity slows senescence.
  3. Lieberman's active grandparent hypothesis holds that humans evolved as grandparents who kept working after reproduction, and that this activity switches on repair and maintenance.
  4. Citing Paffenbarger's Harvard alumni study, Lieberman says the mortality gap from exercise grows from about 20% in younger adults to about 50% in their sixties and seventies.
  5. Lieberman argues that medicine confuses diseases that become more common with age with diseases caused by age, and that rising blood pressure in old age is not inevitable.
  6. Lieberman holds that people move only when it is necessary or rewarding, and that taking it easy is an instinct from times of scarcity, not a failing to be shamed.
  7. Lieberman thinks social ties work best for sticking with exercise, such as a friend waiting for a 6 a.m. run or a stickK commitment contract that stings when broken.
  8. Lieberman calls plantar fasciitis a mismatch disease: insoles relieve symptoms, while strengthening the foot and slowly moving to minimal shoes treats the cause; treating symptoms only is what he calls dysevolution.
  9. Lieberman says the most important running skill is not overstriding: land with the shin vertical at about 170 to 180 steps per minute.
  10. "Use it or lose it" can be read as the body autoscaling to load: muscle is expensive reserved capacity that gets scaled down when unused.
