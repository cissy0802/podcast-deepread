An ICU doctor's bet: vitamin D is just the mark that sunlight leaves behind

Roger Seheult × Steven Bartlett · "Vitamin D Expert: The Fastest Way To Dementia & The Big Lie About Sunlight!"

Show The Diary of a CEO · 2025-07-17 · Length 125 min · Guest Roger Seheult · Host Steven Bartlett · Episode YouTube

The moment

June 2024, a hospital in Minnesota. A fifteen-year-old boy on chemotherapy for leukemia develops a flesh-eating fungal infection in his lung. Surgeons remove his left lung; the infection moves into the right one. He is on the strongest antifungals there are and still getting worse. The doctors tell the family he has about two days.

Asked what he wants to do with them, he says he wants to go outside. Nurses and respiratory therapists wheel his bed, breathing machine and all, out into the open air. On day two his white count starts falling; by day five he is off the BiPAP; a repeat CT shows the disease in his right lung 60–70% gone. Seheult heard the story from the boy's mother. Having told it, he adds a caveat of his own: once outside, the boy was also getting more regular sessions on a multi-wavelength light device called a Firefly, and

I don't know which did it.— Roger Seheult · [00:12:10]

That line is the key to the whole episode. Seheult's evidence runs from single cases all the way to cohorts of hundreds of thousands, and he knows exactly how hard each rung is. Yet when it comes time to conclude, he keeps taking one step further in the same direction.

Who's talking, and when

Roger Seheult is a pulmonary and critical-care physician in Southern California and a co-founder of the medical-education channel MedCram, where he ran a long series of COVID explainers during the pandemic. He says he works at three hospitals, and the change he most wants to push in all of them is getting inpatients outdoors. The biggest barrier isn't medical; it's staff to push the beds. On his 7-to-7 shifts he tries to get out into the sun for fifteen minutes at lunch.

Steven Bartlett's show is good at turning health talk into "what do I do tomorrow." This time he lines up a red-light mask, a SAD lamp and blue-blocking glasses on the table and asks about each in turn. Midway through there's an ad read for the red-light mask brand, which Bartlett himself uses.

"Dementia" in the title comes up only a handful of times, as one of the downstream effects of failing mitochondria. The real subject is sunlight. Seheult strings eight "pillars of health" onto the acronym NEWSTART, a lifestyle framework promoted by Weimar University in California. He reads its final T, trust, as religious faith, and for the show's closing question he chose to talk about the Bible. This page covers the part he spent the most time on and that evidence can actually test: sunlight.

Glossary

  • UVB / UVA / near-infrared: three bands of sunlight, from shorter to longer wavelength. UVB lets skin make vitamin D and barely reaches high latitudes in winter; UVA is slightly longer; near-infrared is invisible but passes through clothing, and it is the warmth you feel on your skin.
  • Photobiomodulation: therapies that shine specific red or near-infrared wavelengths on tissue to shift cell metabolism.
  • Mitochondria: the organelles that produce a cell's energy.

The episode, worked through

Vitamin D is a marker, not a switch

Seheult's argument starts from something that didn't fit in his ICU. Early in the pandemic, doctors were told COVID would hit patients with lung disease first. The people dying around him were instead the obese, and patients with heart disease, kidney disease and dementia. His explanation is that these conditions share a root: mitochondrial dysfunction. His metaphor is a car. The mitochondrion is the engine, oxidative stress is its heat, chronically ill patients are already running hot, and COVID is a steep hill. The cars that stall at the top are the ones whose gauges were already in the red.

What cools the engine? He leans on a 2019 review by melatonin researcher Russel Reiter and optical engineer Scott Zimmerman. It proposes that mitochondria make their own melatonin inside the cell as an antioxidant, and that the near-infrared part of sunlight, which reaches deep into the body, prompts them to make more. Seheult's own word for this is "proposed": it is a hypothesis, not an experimental result.

Then comes a pandemic pattern. Patients admitted with higher vitamin D levels died less often, so doctors gave vitamin D, and it did almost nothing. Seheult's reading:

I believe that that was a marker of something else.— Roger Seheult · [00:30:58]

High vitamin D means someone spends time outdoors; outdoors means the full spectrum; and the thing doing the work may be the near-infrared arriving alongside the UVB. As epidemiology, that reasoning is sound: if moving a marker doesn't move the outcome, the marker isn't the cause. But it only rules vitamin D out. It doesn't rule infrared in. Nor is Seheult against supplements. He takes vitamin D daily and cites a large randomized trial in which 2,000 IU a day lowered the risk of autoimmune disease. His claim is that pills can't replace the sun, not that pills don't work.

From one patient to a dose-response curve

The most instructive part of the episode is how Seheult stacks his evidence. The bottom rung is cases. Besides the boy, there's his own COVID patient on 35 liters a minute of high-flow oxygen in a pitch-dark room with the blinds shut, who asked him, before anything else, how much time he had left Seheult rallied a respiratory therapist, rigged two oxygen tanks together, and wheeled him into the sun. The next day he was down to 15 liters; five days later he went home without oxygen. Then, immediately:

Now, obviously that's an anecdote, right? That's not a study.— Roger Seheult · [00:44:38]

One rung up is a 30-patient Brazilian trial: patients wore vests lined with 940-nanometer LEDs, half switched on, half off. Above that are observational studies: a twenty-year cohort of Swedish women, Richard Weller's work on UK Biobank data, an Edinburgh team's analysis of COVID deaths in the US, England and Italy, and the way death peaks fall in each hemisphere's own winter, in Australia as much as in the north.

When he gets to the Swedish study, Bartlett asks the question the whole episode turns on:

How do they know it wasn't linked to exercise?— Steven Bartlett · [00:48:36]

Seheult first concedes that association alone can't give you causation. Then he reaches for dose-response from the Bradford Hill criteria: more sun, less death, in a graded line, the same kind of evidence that convicted smoking of causing lung cancer. That answer is half right. A gradient makes pure chance less likely, but it doesn't answer Bartlett's question, because people who spend more time outdoors also tend to exercise more and be healthier to begin with, and a confounder produces its own gradient. What these studies actually measured is taken up in the claim check below.

The price of living indoors

With the evidence laid out, Seheult turns to how we live. Americans spend 93% of their time indoors, he says, and Britons 92%. Incandescent bulbs have given way to LEDs with a narrow spectrum, so for the first time humans routinely get blue light without red. New windows in California are built to block infrared because it heats the room. His summary:

The scurvy of the 21st century is the lack of sunlight.— Roger Seheult · [00:52:55]

His advice splits into two pathways, the clearest stretch of the episode. One goes through the eyes and sets the body clock: bright light in the morning, a 10,000-lux SAD lamp for about twenty minutes on dark days or high-latitude winters, and real darkness at night, since screen light suppresses melatonin and pushes the clock later. The other goes through the skin and, on his account, acts on mitochondria: any patch of skin will do, so a broad-brimmed hat costs nothing. He favors morning and evening sun, when UV is weak and infrared makes up a larger share.

Bartlett holds up the sponsor's red-light mask and asks whether he'd recommend it. Seheult says it's reasonable, then uses the scurvy image to draw a boundary around it:

if you're on a ship with a bunch of people with scurvy and you already have a diet that's rich in vegetables and fruits, eating an extra lemon is not going to be that beneficial.— Roger Seheult · [01:00:49]

Red-light devices aren't baseless, either. In a study by UCL's Glenn Jeffery, fifteen minutes of 670-nanometer light on the back before a glucose drink cut the rise in blood sugar by 27.7%. It involved 30 healthy adults and measured two hours after a single glucose challenge.

Fever is a feature

Under "water," Seheult makes a counterintuitive case. Interferon is the workhorse of innate immunity, and he cites a mouse study in which several proteins regulating it jumped when body temperature rose from 37 to 38 degrees. Hence his view that a modest fever needn't be knocked down. A sauna, to him, is an artificial fever; a minute of cold water afterward constricts surface vessels so core temperature stays high for longer. The Finnish sauna data he cites show lower cardiovascular death among people who sauna four to seven times a week than among those who go once.

Claim check

Seheult says a Swedish study following twenty to thirty thousand women for twenty years found the sunniest group had the least cancer and cardiovascular death, and that sun-seeking smokers had the same mortality as sun-avoiding non-smokers. [00:48:36]

Supported, but stretched

Lindqvist et al. (2016) followed 29,518 women, and the smoking comparison is in the abstract. But the paper says the sun-exposed had mainly less cardiovascular and non-cancer death; cancer's share of deaths actually rose. The life-expectancy gap was 0.6–2.1 years, and the authors say unmeasured bias can't be ruled out.

Seheult says the Edinburgh study found more sunlight meant fewer COVID deaths in the US, England and Italy, "completely independent of vitamin D," so something else is at work. He offers it as support for the infrared idea. [00:47:10]

Supported, but stretched

Cherrie et al. (2021) analyzed only areas where UVB was too weak for vitamin D synthesis and found the mortality rate ratio fell 29% per 100 kJ/m² of daily UVA. But what they measured was ultraviolet A, not infrared, and the mechanism they propose is nitric oxide released from skin. The data are area-level, from the first months of 2020.

Seheult says in a 30-patient Brazilian randomized trial, those wearing a 940 nm infrared vest went home after 8 days instead of 12, which was enough for him to take every hospitalized COVID patient outside. [00:40:42]

Supported, but stretched

Pereira et al. (2023) report 11.7 versus 8 days (p = 0.02), so the numbers hold. But the trial was single-blinded, excluded overweight patients, enrolled only ages 50–80, and tested an LED vest on the chest and abdomen, not sunlight. Getting from the vest to the sun is Seheult's own step.

Crossovers

  • Genetic epidemiology · Mendelian randomization: Seheult's "vitamin D is only a marker" has a dedicated test. Some people inherit variants that keep their vitamin D higher, a randomization nature ran for us. A 2021 Mendelian randomization study on COVID host-genetics data found no link between genetically predicted vitamin D and severe COVID, which agrees with him. Where it parts ways: the method can tell you "it isn't vitamin D," but it won't tell you "it's infrared." Clearing one suspect isn't catching the culprit.
  • Control theory · Set point: Seheult's account of fever is a feedback loop. Infection raises the thermostat from 37 to 38 degrees; actual temperature lags, so you feel cold and shiver; when the set point drops back, you sweat. In that frame an antipyretic overrides the controller's output rather than fixing a fault. The difference: biological set points can fail too, and a fever high enough to cause seizures has to be treated.
  • Evolutionary biology · Evolutionary mismatch: "The scurvy of the 21st century" is a textbook mismatch claim: bodies shaped outdoors under full-spectrum light, now spending over nine-tenths of their time under narrow-band LEDs. Where it isn't like scurvy: scurvy is missing one molecule with a known mechanism, while "sunlight" is a bundle, with UV, infrared, exercise and greenery all tangled together.

Ten takeaways

  1. Roger Seheult says the sickest COVID patients in his ICU were obese or had heart disease, kidney disease or dementia, not the lung patients doctors had been warned about.
  2. Seheult traces those chronic diseases to mitochondrial dysfunction, picturing oxidative stress as engine heat and melatonin as the cooling system.
  3. A 2019 review by Zimmerman and Reiter proposes that near-infrared light prompts mitochondria to make melatonin inside cells; it is a hypothesis.
  4. Seheult argues that because vitamin D supplements didn't rescue severe COVID patients, vitamin D levels are only a marker of time outdoors.
  5. Seheult takes vitamin D every day and is not against supplements; his claim is that supplements can't replace sunlight.
  6. Seheult answers Bartlett's "wasn't it exercise?" with Bradford Hill dose-response, but a dose-response curve cannot rule out confounding.
  7. Seheult splits light's effects into two pathways: through the eyes to set the body clock, and through the skin to act on mitochondria.
  8. Seheult advises bright light in the morning, darkness at night, and a 10,000-lux SAD lamp for about twenty minutes on dark days or in high-latitude winters.
  9. Seheult uses the scurvy analogy to bound red-light devices: for someone already getting enough sun, extra red light may add little.
  10. Seheult argues a modest fever needn't be suppressed because temperatures around 38 degrees help interferon production, and treats the sauna as an artificial fever.
