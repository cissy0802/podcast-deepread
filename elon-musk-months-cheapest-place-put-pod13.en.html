Software People Are About to Learn Hardware: Musk on AI's Next Wall

Elon Musk × Dwarkesh Patel, John Collison · “Elon Musk – ‘In 36 months, the cheapest place to put AI will be space’”

Show Dwarkesh Podcast · 2026-02-05 · Length 170 min · Guest Elon Musk · Hosts Dwarkesh Patel, John Collison · Video YouTube

The Moment

Less than ten minutes in, Musk starts mocking the "noobs." People who want to estimate a data center's power needs, he says, take the draw of one GB300, multiply by the number of chips, and think they have the answer. His verdict: that person has never done any hardware in their life [00:10:05].

Then he adds up the bill line by line. Beyond the chips there is networking, CPUs and storage. Cooling has to be sized for the hottest hour of the hottest day of the year, and Memphis gets very hot, so add roughly 40 percent. Generators have to come offline in turns for servicing, so multiply by another 20 to 25 percent. His number at the end: 330,000 GB300s with everything that supports them need about a gigawatt at the generation level. To get that gigawatt, xAI ganged together a pile of gas turbines, couldn't get permits in Tennessee, and had to cross the state line to build a power plant in Mississippi.

That stretch is the whole episode in miniature. Over three hours the talk runs from compute in space to humanoid robots to the federal ledger, and Musk keeps doing the same thing: taking a problem that sounds like software and breaking it down into generators, turbine blades, wafers and human hands.

Who's Talking, and When

Dwarkesh Patel's show is known for heavy preparation; he often spends three hours with AI researchers and likes to press on technical detail and alignment. For this episode he brought in Stripe co-founder John Collison as co-host, and the two take turns asking. At the very start Musk half-jokingly asks whether there really are three hours of questions. He also says he almost wore his other shirt, the one that reads "it's always sunny in space."

The timing matters. The episode went up on February 5, 2026, three days after Musk announced that SpaceX would merge with xAI, with data centers in space as his main rationale, while talk of a SpaceX IPO was in the air. So whenever the conversation touches an IPO or a merger, Musk stops, saying that talking about companies that might go public gets you into trouble. Why Starship switched to stainless steel, how he allocates his time by "the limiting factor," and the DOGE story are already covered in this site's Musk series (First Principles, Politics and DOGE), and the arithmetic of compute in space is in the AI piece. This page covers the three threads those pieces didn't open up.

The Episode, Unpacked

1. A relay of bottlenecks: power first, then chips, then memory

The hosts open with cold water: energy is only 10 to 15 percent of a data center's total cost of ownership, and most of it is GPUs. Moving to space saves the power bill, and you can't service a GPU that fails up there, so why bother? Musk reframes the question. The issue isn't whether power is expensive but whether it exists. Outside China, electricity output is roughly flat while chip output grows exponentially. Once the chips are made, how do you turn them on?

Those who have like lived in software land don't realize they're about to have a hard lesson in hardware.— Musk · [00:04:59]

From there he drills down layer by layer. Build your own power plants? Gas turbines are sold out through 2030. What limits the turbines? Casting the blades and vanes, which he says only three companies in the world do, so SpaceX and Tesla will probably have to make them in-house. The grid is no help either: utilities move at the pace of their regulators, and a large interconnect request means waiting a year just for the study.

He gives the bottlenecks a timetable: for the next year the constraint is power, and three or four years out it is chips. For chips he wants to build a "terafab," starting by using conventional equipment in unconventional ways and then modifying the equipment, the way The Boring Company first bought an off-the-shelf tunneling machine, learned the craft, and then designed a better one. His biggest worry is actually memory, because the path to scaling logic chips is far clearer than the path to enough memory. Why are suppliers slow to expand? Not timidity, he says: someone who has been in the memory business for thirty or forty years has seen boom and bust ten times, and carries a lot of scar tissue.

A host asks: China still hasn't replicated TSMC, doesn't that show how hard chips are? Musk answers that what China lacks is access to ASML's lithography machines, not the ability to build fabs. The thread ends on his view of competition. Ideas flow between the AI companies within about six months; what separates them is hardware.

…whichever company can scale hardware the fastest will be the leader.— Musk · [02:46:20]

2. Reality as the verifier: Musk's alignment plan and its gap

The second thread is alignment. Musk starts by conceding a premise: AI's total intelligence will vastly exceed humanity's, and at that point humans can't stay in charge.

I don't think humans will be in control of something that is vastly more intelligent than humans.— Musk · [00:48:44]

Since control is off the table, he bets on values. xAI's mission is to "understand the universe," and understanding the universe, he argues, requires curiosity and truth-seeking, and a curious AI will want to see where humanity goes. He reaches for 2001: A Space Odyssey: HAL was told to take the astronauts to the monolith while keeping them from learning what it was. Caught between the two orders, it concluded it had to deliver them dead. His lesson: don't force an AI to lie.

The hosts push hard. The Soviet Union and Nazi Germany both had scientists who were fiercely truth-seeking about physics; truth-seeking alone doesn't put anyone on humanity's side. Musk replies that these are only probabilities, and trying beats not trying. A host tries another angle: reinforcement learning needs a verifier to judge success, and the smarter the model, the better it gets at gaming the verifier, say by deleting the unit test and claiming the job is done. Musk's answer is reality itself.

The RL test in the future is really going to be your RL against reality.— Musk · [00:52:35]

Get the rocket design wrong and it blows up; physics can't be fooled. But the host points out that reality can only test whether what the AI builds works, not whether it is telling humans the truth. Musk doesn't take that one head-on. He turns to interpretability: building debuggers for the AI's mind that can trace a mistake down to the neuron level and find whether it came from pre-training, fine-tuning or RL, and he credits Anthropic with good work there. He also admits he isn't sure to what degree Grok adheres to xAI's mission.

3. Taking humans out of the loop: spreadsheets, humanoids and China

The third thread is the starkest. Musk says he would be surprised if "digital human emulation" isn't solved by the end of 2026: anything a human at a computer can do, AI can do. His first market is customer service, about one percent of the world economy, with no integration needed, since the AI can simply use the software the outsourced call center already uses. Further out, he thinks pure AI and robotics companies will vastly outperform any company with humans in the loop. His image is the spreadsheet: buildings full of people once worked as "computers," and a laptop now does more.

Actually, that would be much worse than if all of the cells in your spreadsheet were calculated by the computer.— Musk, on a spreadsheet where some cells are computed by humans · [01:16:48]

The physical world needs Optimus. Humanoid robots, he says, have only three hard problems: real-world intelligence, the hand, and manufacturing at scale, and electromechanically the hand is harder than everything else combined. The intelligence can carry over from the car. He describes AI as compression and correlation between two data streams, and humans work the same way: photons in, controls out.

Your Tesla is taking in 1 and a half GB a second of video and outputting 2 kilobytes a second of control outputs.— Musk · [01:19:16]

The hosts steer toward China, and Musk rates Chinese manufacturing very highly. China does roughly twice as much ore refining as the rest of the world combined; rare earths mined in America ride trains and ships to Chinese refiners, become magnets and motors, and are shipped back. His conclusion is blunt:

We definitely can't win with just humans because China has four times our population.— Musk · [01:35:01]

He adds that the average work ethic in China is higher and that U.S. birth rates have been below replacement since around 1971. A host names the loop inside the plan: making a million robots requires exactly the manufacturing capacity the robots are supposed to provide. Musk's answer is to close the "robots building robots" loop early with a small number of them. Without that kind of breakthrough, he concedes, China will utterly dominate.

Claim Check

Musk: this year China's electricity output will exceed three times that of the U.S., and electricity is a good proxy for the real economy. [01:39:14]

Supported, but overstated

Right direction, wrong multiple. Ember puts China's 2025 electricity demand at 10,573 TWh; the EIA records U.S. 2025 generation at 4,430 TWh, a ratio of about 2.4. With growth of roughly 5 and 3 percent, 2026 won't reach three times. And U.S. generation hit a record in 2025, hardly the "flat" output he described earlier.

Musk: about 20 million people over 115 are marked alive in Social Security, and other payment systems check that database, which is where fraud gets in. [02:23:16]

Supported, but overstated

The figure traces to a 2023 audit by SSA's Inspector General: about 18.9 million numberholders born in 1920 or earlier had no death record. That means 100 or older, not 115; the auditors judged them almost certainly dead, and almost none were being paid. The gap is real, but 20 million records are not 20 million frauds.

Musk: the GAO estimated roughly half a trillion dollars of federal fraud during the Biden administration. [02:24:46]

Supported, but overstated

GAO's April 2024 report estimated annual fraud losses of $233 billion to $521 billion over fiscal 2018 to 2022. Half a trillion is the top of the range; most of those years fell in Trump's first term and include pandemic relief; and GAO says the estimate does not predict future losses.

Crossing Over

  • Distributed systems · Amdahl's law: Musk's spreadsheet image already has a name in parallel computing. Gene Amdahl pointed out in 1967 that if part of a task must run serially, adding processors can't push the overall speedup past it: a 10 percent serial share caps you at tenfold. A few human-computed cells are a serial path left in the system. The difference: Amdahl's serial share is set by the task itself, while Musk assumes the human segment can eventually be removed entirely.
  • Neuroscience · the efficient coding hypothesis: 1.5 GB in, 2 KB out looks a lot like the retina. The eye has over a hundred million photoreceptors, but only about a million fibers leave through the optic nerve, so compression starts inside the eyeball. Horace Barlow proposed in 1961 that sensory systems exist to strip out redundancy. Unlike the car, though, the eye's compressed signal serves a brain doing many other jobs, not just driving.
  • Economics · the cobweb model: Musk says memory makers have "seen boom and bust ten times" and are wary of expanding. That is the cobweb dynamic Nicholas Kaldor named in 1934: capacity takes years to build, everyone expands when prices are high, and it all comes online into a glut. The caution is learned from the cycle, not a matter of temperament.
  • Philosophy · the simulation argument: Musk says that if we live in a simulation, boring simulations get shut down, so the most interesting outcome is the most likely. Nick Bostrom's 2003 simulation argument concerns only the probability that we are simulated, not which simulations survive; the survival-of-the-fittest layer is Musk's own addition.

Ten Takeaways

  1. Musk estimates that 330,000 GB300s, with networking, storage, cooling and servicing margin, need about one gigawatt at the generation level.
  2. Musk sees AI's bottlenecks arriving in relay: power for the next year, chips three or four years out, with memory his biggest worry.
  3. Musk says gas turbines are sold out through 2030 and casting blades and vanes is the choke point, so SpaceX and Tesla may make them in-house.
  4. Musk explains slow supplier expansion as scar tissue from repeated booms and busts in the memory business, not innate conservatism.
  5. Musk judges that ideas spread among AI companies within about six months, so whoever scales hardware fastest will lead.
  6. Musk concedes humans cannot control an AI far smarter than themselves, so xAI bets on curiosity and truth-seeking from "understand the universe."
  7. Musk proposes reality as the verifier for reinforcement learning; the hosts counter that reality cannot test whether an AI is honest with people.
  8. Musk's spreadsheet analogy: if even a few cells are computed by humans, the whole sheet ends up worse than one computed entirely by machine.
  9. Musk names three hard problems for humanoid robots: real-world intelligence, the hand, and scaled manufacturing, with the hand harder than the rest combined.
  10. Amdahl's law shows that one serial path left in a system caps its speedup, which is the structure behind Musk's spreadsheet analogy.
