Jensen Huang Series · No. 5

Pain, Character and His Advice to the Young

What "ample doses of pain and suffering" means once you put it back where it was said

Topic pain, expectations and resilience · Material 10 talk and interview transcripts (2011–2026) · Main venues SIEPR Economic Summit (2024) · Stripe Sessions (2024) · Caltech commencement (2024) · Acquired (2023) · Joe Rogan (2025) · Lex Fridman (2026) · Hoover "Only in America" (2026)

His Claims

Jensen Huang's most quoted line is probably the one where he wished Stanford students "ample doses of pain and suffering." Most retellings place it at the Graduate School of Business; even Patrick Collison, asking him about it on stage at Stripe, thought it was a GSB event. It actually comes from the March 2024 Economic Summit of the Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research (SIEPR), where the moderator asked what advice he would give Stanford students who want to start companies. This page puts the line back in that room, lines it up against what he has said elsewhere, and asks what he is really claiming, what he rests it on, and where his own words fail to fit together.

1. Low expectations are what let you take the hits

He began with himself. One of his great advantages, he said, is that he has very low expectations, while Stanford graduates have very high ones, and deservedly so: a great school, the top of the class, brilliant peers. Then came the line:

People with very high expectations have very low resilience, and unfortunately resilience matters in success.— Jensen Huang · SIEPR Economic Summit, 2024 [00:36:16]

He said he did not know how to teach resilience except to hope that suffering happens to them. He added that to this day he uses the phrase "pain and suffering" inside NVIDIA with great glee, as in "this is going to cause a lot of pain and suffering," because what he wants to refine is the company's character. The reasoning ran like this:

Greatness is not intelligence, as you know. Greatness comes from character.— Jensen Huang · SIEPR Economic Summit, 2024 [00:37:07]

And character, he went on, is not formed out of smart people but out of people who suffered. Hence the closing wish.

What does "expectations" mean here? In 2026, talking about his immigrant childhood on the Hoover Institution's show, he gave a clearer version. At nine he was in Kentucky; after a swim meet the coach took the team to a brightly lit restaurant that looked like a spaceship. It was McDonald's. Life, he said, is largely about expectations:

So the thing that's really great about immigrants is you came with very little expectation.— Jensen Huang · Hoover "Only in America", 2026 [00:09:21]

In the next breath he added that immigrants come with great hopes and dreams. So low expectations do not mean thinking small. They mean not believing the world owes you a smooth road, and being grateful for what you have. On the same show he told young people that it is possible to be grateful, dissatisfied and hungry for greatness all at once.

That spring, Collison asked him at Stripe Sessions to explain the pain-and-suffering line. He called it a misunderstanding. People say you should choose a career by passion and then equate passion with happiness, and he thinks something is missing there:

I don't love every day of my job. I don't think every day brings me joy, nor does joy have to be the definition of a good day.— Jensen Huang · Stripe Sessions, 2024 [00:06:45]

He loves the company every second, he said, but is not happy every year; nothing great is easy, so wishing you greatness is, in his way of saying it, wishing you pain. Read that way the claim narrows. He is not asking for suffering as such; he is asking you not to read suffering as a sign you chose wrong.

2. Suffering forges character: what he rests it on

That June, in his Caltech commencement address, he turned it into a ranking of abilities:

Of all of the things that I value most about my abilities, intelligence is not top of that list.— Jensen Huang · Caltech commencement, 2024 [00:30:20]

Above it he put three things he calls his superpowers: enduring pain and suffering, working on something for a very long time, and handling setbacks while seeing the opportunity around the corner. The evidence he offers is company history. In the 2000s NVIDIA's motherboard chipset business went from zero to a billion dollars almost overnight, until AMD bought ATI and no longer needed it. NVIDIA turned to Intel CPUs, and Intel terminated the license. It built a mobile chip, and was pushed out because it could not connect to Qualcomm's modems. With setbacks arriving roughly once a year, it finally chose a market with no customers, and therefore no competitors: a robotics computer running an algorithm almost nobody understood yet, deep learning. What that run built, he said, was a culture of resilience, a company that is hard to distract and hard to discourage.

A second story in the same speech is about how endurance becomes craft. At Kyoto's Silver Temple he watched a lone gardener picking dead moss with bamboo tweezers across a vast garden. Asked how he could ever finish, the gardener said he had cared for the garden for 25 years and had plenty of time. At Stripe Huang made a related point: he does not like starting over with new people, because pain, suffering, joy and knowledge have been deposited in colleagues of many years, and craft only comes with tenure.

Asked where the attitude came from, he went back to childhood. At nine he and his older brother were sent to a boarding school in Kentucky, and as the youngest he was assigned to clean the dormitory toilets. He has told this in several versions: in 2024 he told Collison it was something he had never told anyone, not even his family; in 2025 he gave Rogan the most detail; in 2026 at Carnegie Mellon it shrank to "coal country, a town of a few hundred people." The story itself belongs to the series page on the Denny's founding and near-death years. At Carnegie Mellon he put the conclusion most gently: failure is not the opposite of success, and each failure is a humility moment and a character-strengthening moment.

The vocabulary has shifted over time. In a 2011 Stanford entrepreneurship lecture he talked about building a tolerance for failure: fail quickly and inexpensively, and change course as soon as you hit a dead end [00:35:07]. That was standard Silicon Valley fail-fast. From 2023 on, in the material we read, the key words become endurance, persistence and suffering, and the stories become long bets like the decade in which nobody paid for CUDA (see the series page on the CUDA bet).

3. But he also says no one would start if they knew

Read only the commencement speeches and you would think he is telling people to go looking for pain. The long interviews are more complicated. In 2023 Acquired asked what company he would start if he were thirty again, sitting in a Denny's with his two best friends. He said he would not start one:

Building NVIDIA turned out to have been a million times harder than I expected it to be.— Jensen Huang · Acquired, 2023 [01:19:05]

Had they known the pain and suffering, the vulnerability, the embarrassment and shame, he said, nobody in their right mind would start a company. The entrepreneur's superpower is not knowing how hard it is and only asking "how hard can it be," a trick he still plays on his own brain every morning. In 2026 he gave Lex Fridman the method:

You don't want to simulate all that in advance. You don't want to know that.— Jensen Huang · Lex Fridman #494, 2026 [01:42:37]

His recipe is to walk in thinking it will be perfect and fun, then, when the setbacks and humiliations arrive, flip another switch: forget it and keep moving, while constantly re-checking his assumptions about the future. As long as the inputs have not changed, the expected output has not either. NVIDIA lost 80% of its market value in the financial crisis, and recalling that period at Stanford GSB in 2024 he described the same move: wake at the same time, prioritize the day the same way, gut-check the core beliefs, even tick off "family loves me," and then ask whether physics or gravity changed [00:25:22].

One more layer appears only with Rogan. He said he has used the phrase "30 days from going out of business" for 33 years because he still wakes up with that feeling every morning:

I have a greater drive from not wanting to fail than the drive of wanting to succeed.— Jensen Huang · Joe Rogan #2422, 2025 [01:49:52]

He said he is not ambitious, he just wants to stay alive, and admitted to being always in a state of anxiety [01:52:04]. He named the source too: the constant reassessment. Were you wrong yesterday? Are you still right? Is it worse than you thought? In his view, a leader willing to be wrong is what lets a company pivot.

How the Claims Fit Together

The three claims form a chain. Low expectations mean setbacks do not surprise you; not being surprised lets you absorb them; absorbing them lets you stay on one thing for a decade; staying that long is what grows craft and character, in a person and in a company. The chain reaches into the rest of the series. CUDA's unpaid decade is its biggest exhibit, and his preference for torturing people into greatness rather than firing them is the same logic applied to management, covered on the series page on his flat organization.

The weakest link is the word "expectations." To Stanford students he says keep them low; to Lex he says walk in expecting perfection and do not rehearse the setbacks. "How hard can it be" is his bridge: underestimate the difficulty without expecting a smooth ride. But that collides with the wish for pain. By his own account on Acquired, anyone who truly knew how deep the pain goes would never set out, so wishing people pain in advance removes the very superpower he credits founders with. Research shows how common that blindness is. Cooper and colleagues surveyed 2,994 new business owners in 1988: 81% put their own odds of success at 7 in 10 or better, and a third at 10 in 10 (paper). Not knowing helps people start, but the one who ends up at the commencement podium is always the one who survived.

Boundaries

"Character is formed out of people who suffered... I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering." — SIEPR Economic Summit, 2024, quoted above

Supported, but stated too broadly

Seery, Holman and Silver (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2010) followed 2,398 US adults from 2001 to 2004. People with some lifetime adversity had better mental health and life satisfaction than those with none and than those with a lot, a U-shaped curve; overall, more adversity still meant worse outcomes. "Some" helps; "ample" is past the sweet spot. And his evidence is his own retrospective story of growth, while Frazier and colleagues (2009), measuring 122 students before and after trauma, found that self-reported growth was largely unrelated to measured change.

"People with very high expectations have very low resilience." — SIEPR Economic Summit, 2024, quoted above

Insufficient evidence

No study tests this directly, and the closest evidence splits "expectations" in two. In four studies (job-seeking graduates, students with a crush, undergraduates before an exam, hip-replacement patients), Oettingen and Mayer (2002) found that positive expectations, judging success as likely, predicted more effort and better outcomes, while positive fantasies, dwelling on a pleasant imagined future, predicted the reverse. If he means the first, the research points the other way; if the second, it agrees. His advice to Lex to walk in thinking it will be perfect sounds more like the harmful fantasy.

Crossing Over · Interdisciplinary Links

  • Stoicism · premeditatio malorum: Seneca urged rehearsing loss and misfortune in advance so that they do not catch you off guard. That matches what Huang told Stanford students about low expectations, and is the exact opposite of what he told Lex about not simulating setbacks. The same man stands on both sides in two different rooms.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: the Sallatha Sutta says the untrained person, struck by one arrow (pain itself), shoots himself with a second (distress about the pain). Huang's "forget it and move on" looks like pulling the second arrow out, yet the anxiety of waking every day 30 days from going out of business is precisely the second arrow. The deeper difference is direction: Buddhism aims to end suffering, Huang uses it as fuel.
  • Psychology · Atkinson's achievement motivation: in 1957 Atkinson split achievement motivation into a motive to succeed and a motive to avoid failure, predicting that failure-avoiders prefer very easy or very hard tasks, since failing at a very hard one carries no shame. Huang describes himself as failure-driven and seeks out zero-billion-dollar markets, a surprisingly close fit; the difference is that he then stays on the very hard task for a decade.
  • Distributed systems · chaos engineering: inject faults into production on purpose so the system builds resilience through small doses of failure. Huang's gleeful talk of pain inside NVIDIA resembles fault injection for an organization. But chaos engineering insists on limiting the blast radius and capping the dose, which is what Seery's U-curve says too, and exactly the rate limiter "ample doses" lacks.

Ten Takeaways

  1. Jensen Huang's wish for "ample doses of pain and suffering" comes from the March 2024 SIEPR Economic Summit at Stanford, answering what advice he would give student founders.
  2. At SIEPR Jensen Huang said people with very high expectations have very low resilience, and called his own low expectations a great advantage.
  3. Jensen Huang's chain of reasoning: greatness comes from character, and character is formed not out of smart people but out of people who suffered.
  4. Jensen Huang's low expectations do not mean thinking small; he describes immigrants as arriving with very little expectation but great hopes and dreams.
  5. At Stripe Sessions Jensen Huang clarified that he rejects equating passion with happiness, and that joy need not define a good day.
  6. At Caltech Jensen Huang said the ability he values most is not intelligence but enduring pain, persisting for years, and seeing opportunity in setbacks.
  7. Jensen Huang cites NVIDIA being pushed out of chipsets and mobile before turning to robotics as how the company built a culture of resilience.
  8. In 2023 Jensen Huang said building NVIDIA was a million times harder than expected, and that a founder's superpower is not knowing how hard it is.
  9. In 2026 Jensen Huang told Lex Fridman not to simulate setbacks in advance, to forget them when they come, and to keep going while assumptions hold.
  10. The Stoics rehearsed misfortune in advance, while Jensen Huang tells Lex Fridman not to rehearse setbacks: two resilience strategies pointing in opposite directions.
