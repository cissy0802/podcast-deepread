Peterson sets a "terrified tyrant" test for leaders, and Lex hands it straight back

Jordan Peterson × Lex Fridman · Jordan Peterson: Life, Death, Power, Fame, and Meaning | Lex Fridman Podcast #313

Show Lex Fridman Podcast · #313 · 2022-08-19 · Length 184 min · Guest Jordan Peterson · Host Lex Fridman · Original YouTube

The moment

Half an hour in, Lex asks a gentle question: can anger harden a mind until it no longer notices the quiet beauty of the world? Peterson reaches for Nietzsche — battle not with monsters lest ye become a monster; gaze into the abyss and the abyss gazes back. Nietzsche is absolutely right, he says, and then turns it around:

If you gaze into the abyss long enough, you see the light, not the darkness.— Jordan Peterson · [00:30:25]

Lex asks whether he's sure. Peterson says he is betting his life on it.

That exchange is the skeleton of the whole episode. Nearly everything Peterson says, from the flaming sword in Genesis to how he treated depression, makes one claim: walk voluntarily toward what you most fear and it will make you better. Lex keeps steering back to a different question: how does someone staring into the abyss know that what he sees is still the light?

Who's talking, and when

Jordan Peterson is a Canadian clinical psychologist who taught for years at the University of Toronto; 12 Rules for Life made him one of the most famous psychologists alive. One detail captures where he was: on tour he eats only steak and drinks only water, often cooking it in an air fryer in the hotel room, and jokes that he has left a trail of air fryers in hotels around the world.

Lex Fridman is an MIT-trained AI researcher whose show circles death, love and Dostoevsky; his first question here is whether beauty will save the world. His family history is tangled up with the old Soviet lands, and shortly before this recording he had been to the front in Ukraine.

In August 2022 Peterson was just out of what he describes as three years of severe illness; a year earlier, he says, he had been ready to die. He was touring with his wife Tammy, writing a new book, and fighting a string of complaints at the college of psychologists — ten cases at once, by his count. That summer Dutch farmers were protesting a nitrogen plan and Sri Lanka had tipped into a food crisis. All of it ends up in his answers.

The episode, worked through

1. Dying a little at a time: Peterson's theory of growth

Peterson's image for growth is a hard one. In Genesis, after Adam and Eve are expelled, God sets cherubim with a flaming sword at the gate of Eden so humans cannot return. His reading: the sword judges, cuts, separates wheat from chaff; the fire burns. To get back into paradise, everything in you that is not perfect has to be cut away and burned off.

Lex asks whether destruction is part of loving something. Peterson's answer is that this is humility:

Invite in the judgment. Invite in the judgment, because maybe you can die a little bit instead of dying completely.— Jordan Peterson · [00:25:14]

He reaches for the body: cells that fail to die on schedule become cancer. People are the same; if you are humble enough to let your stupidity die first, you live longer.

He applies this to himself. He notes that Elvis had become an Elvis impersonator by the time he died, and Lex asks whether he fears becoming a Jordan Peterson impersonator. He says he worries about that more than anything. Fame means people do what you ask, and what keeps a person "dying properly" is people pushing back. So he deliberately keeps critics around him, telling him where he was too harsh or hadn't done the homework.

In the clinic the idea becomes concrete. The key word is voluntary: an abyss forced on you traumatizes; one you walk into transforms.

If it's involuntary and accidental, it can kill you. The more it's voluntary, the more transformative it is.— Jordan Peterson · [02:23:14]

Psychology's lesson of the past century, he says, is that voluntary, measured exposure to what terrifies you is curative. So with depression he shrinks the dragon until the patient can beat it. Someone who can't get out of bed: can you sit up once today? If not, can you prop yourself on your elbows once? He describes a client he saw for fifteen years who at first couldn't sit in a café and talk; by the end she was doing stand-up comedy.

2. The "terrified tyrant" test, and Lex's reversal

The liveliest stretch begins with a test Peterson proposes himself. Someone says there's a dragon and he's the man to deal with it. Should you trust him? Leave aside whether the dragon is real, Peterson says, and watch how he reacts to it. If he is frozen and panicking, he is not the man for the job. And if he is willing to compel others to fight it for him:

So now not only are you terrified, you're a terrified tyrant.— Jordan Peterson · [00:38:56]

He aims it at radical environmentalists. As he tells it, a Deloitte report uses joint climate-and-economy models run a hundred years forward to argue that making people poorer now is worth it. Then a biological picture: when avian flu hits, birds die from the bottom of the hierarchy up, because they have the worst nests, the longest trip to food, the most stress. Make a society poorer and those barely hanging on die first. The Dutch plan to put 30% of farmers out of business and Canada's 30% fertilizer cut follow the same logic, in his view. The Canadian one is checked below.

Lex doesn't follow him there. He hands the test straight back:

The intensity of your anger, concern about certain things you're seeing in society, is that going to drive you off the path that ultimately takes us to a better world?— Lex Fridman · [00:50:46]

Peterson says he isn't paralyzed and doesn't want to be a tyrant. He names what he's fighting "the spirit of Cain": Cain held something back in his sacrifice, refused to change when God rejected it, nursed his resentment and killed the brother who was favored. Lex points out that the resentment might live in Peterson too. Peterson concedes it, listing his reasons — the threat to universities comes from the left, it cost him his clinical practice, he has been "cancelled" some forty times — and then:

Does it go too far? I don't know, I'm trying to figure that out.— Jordan Peterson · [01:02:13]

Lex sets another exercise: steelman Justin Trudeau. Peterson starts fairly — a real-looking marriage, no sexual scandals, a lot of ocean placed in marine protected areas — but soon slides back to criticism, saying that a cabinet half female halved Trudeau's talent pool. When Lex suggests it may have come from compassion:

I don't regard compassion as a virtue. Compassion is a reflex, not a virtue.— Jordan Peterson · [01:05:31]

Only when Lex presses — isn't love a blend of compassion, encouragement and truth? — does he give ground, calling mercy and justice "the two hands of God" that must work together. The thread ends somewhere he never closes: the test is his own invention, but the certainty he brings to judging others becomes "I don't know" when it is turned on him.

3. God as a pattern you have to imitate

The third thread runs through the whole conversation with the least friction. Peterson doesn't start from whether God exists. He offers a nearly functional definition: God is the spirit you must emulate in order to thrive, and spirit is a pattern. How do you find it? Notice whom you admire. Admiration already means wanting to be like someone, which for him is the root meaning of worship. Gather all the admirable people, extract what they share, purify it again and again (he gestures at AI learning as an analogy), and what remains is the best representation of God you will get.

Scientists, he adds, believe in the transcendent too: they trust the object that will break their theory more than the theory, and trust that hitting it will set them free. Later the idea lands on power. Citing the primatologist Frans de Waal, he says chimps who rule by compulsion tend to meet a bad end, and the alpha males with preferential mating access are often the best peacemakers and the most reciprocal:

Even among chimps, the principle of sovereignty is something like iterative, iterated reciprocity, and that's a way better principle than power.— Jordan Peterson · [01:45:09]

Asked at the end for the meaning of life, he puts love highest and truth beneath it, serving it. That can only be believed first and tested after, he says, like a marriage: you don't confirm someone is right and then commit; you bind yourself and find out.

Fact-check

Peterson says young men who do just 90 minutes of Future Authoring are 50% less likely to drop out. [02:30:54]

Supported, but overstated

The source is a 2015 Dutch study he co-authored: 703 first-years compared with three earlier cohorts, not randomized. Male first-year retention rose from 54.2% to 71.8%, about 40% fewer dropouts. A randomized trial of about 1,400 students using similar online goal-setting found no effect at all.

Peterson says dogs and rats starved to about 20% below optimal weight live 30% longer. [02:07:08]

Supported, but overstated

The classic dog study paired 48 Labradors; one of each pair ate 25% less from eight weeks of age for life. Median lifespan was 13.0 vs 11.2 years — about 16% longer, not 30%. And that was lifelong restriction, not the intermittent fasting he and Lex were discussing.

Peterson says Canada is ordering farmers to cut fertilizer 30% regardless of how much food they grow — compulsion. [01:14:21]

Supported, but overstated

The 30% figure is real, but Agriculture Canada's target is a 30% cut by 2030 in greenhouse-gas emissions from fertilizer application — emissions, not use. The government says it is voluntary, not a ban and not a mandated cut in fertilizer.

Crossovers

  • Cognitive psychology · the average face: Peterson's "purify the admirable until you reach God" resembles Langlois and Roggman's 1990 average-face experiment: a computer blends many faces into one, and the more faces blended, the more attractive the result. The difference sits right there too: later work found that blending only the most attractive faces beats the average of everyone. Peterson wants the latter — a direction, not a mean. Whose eye picks the direction is exactly what Lex's reversal was probing.
  • Distributed systems · chaos engineering: voluntary, dosed exposure to fear is nearly chaos engineering — inject failures into production on purpose, under control, so a real outage doesn't catch you cold. The difference: chaos engineering bounds the blast radius and watches metrics; Peterson's "dose" rests on a therapist's judgment.
  • Game theory · Axelrod's tournaments: Peterson, via de Waal, says sovereignty rests on iterated reciprocity. In Axelrod's iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments around 1980, the winner was the simplest strategy, tit for tat. Both say that in groups that meet again and again, reciprocity is more stable than coercion.
  • Philosophy of science · Popper's falsification: Peterson says scientists believe most in the object that can overturn their theory. That is the skeleton of Popper's falsificationism: theories stand provisionally and you go looking for where they break. Popper, though, would never call hitting a counterexample "redemptive."

Ten takeaways

  1. Jordan Peterson holds that if you gaze into the abyss long enough you see the light, not the darkness, and says he is betting his life on it.
  2. Peterson reads the flaming sword at the gate of Eden as judgment: to return to paradise, everything imperfect in you must be cut away and burned off.
  3. Peterson's theory of growth is to invite judgment in, letting the stupid parts of yourself die so that you don't have to die completely.
  4. Peterson fears fame could turn him into a "Jordan Peterson impersonator," so he deliberately keeps critics close.
  5. Peterson argues that an abyss you stumble into traumatizes while one you face voluntarily transforms; treating depression means shrinking the dragon until it can be beaten.
  6. Peterson's "terrified tyrant" test says a leader who is panicking and willing to compel others to fight the dragon should not be trusted.
  7. Lex Fridman turned the terrified-tyrant test back on Peterson, who admitted an animus against the left but said he can't tell whether it goes too far.
  8. In this episode Peterson calls compassion a reflex rather than a virtue, then describes mercy and justice as the "two hands of God" that must work together.
  9. Peterson defines God as the pattern you must imitate to thrive, found by repeatedly purifying what all admirable people share.
  10. Peterson, citing Frans de Waal, says alpha chimps last through iterated reciprocity rather than force, echoing tit for tat's win in game theory.
