Are atoms mostly empty space? Physicists still can't say

Sean Carroll, solo lecture · The Biggest Ideas in the Universe | 7. Quantum Mechanics

Show The Biggest Ideas in the Universe · Lecture 7 · 2020-05-05 · Length 65 min · Speaker Sean Carroll (solo lecture, no host) · Video YouTube

The moment

Textbook covers and science-agency logos keep reaching for the same picture: a nucleus in the middle, a few electrons tracing ellipses around it. Carroll draws this Rutherford atom too, then tells you it cannot be right. An electron going in circles is accelerating; an accelerating charge radiates light and loses energy; so it spirals into the nucleus. He puts the whole collapse at about 10⁻¹¹ seconds. On that picture, every bit of matter in the universe, Ernest Rutherford included, should have fallen apart within a hundredth of a billionth of a second.

Some thirty minutes later he comes back to the picture, smudges the orbiting dot into a cloud, and asks what sounds like the simplest question in physics: is the atom mostly empty?

So, if you believe that the wave function represents reality, then you don't say that atoms are mostly empty space. Atoms are mostly the wave function of the electron.— Sean Carroll · [00:51:42]

Everything hangs on that "if". Whether the wave function is a real thing is a question physicists still haven't settled. The lecture spends an hour on the working manual of quantum mechanics and ends on exactly that unease.

Who's talking, and when

Sean Carroll is a theoretical physicist, at Caltech when he recorded this and at Johns Hopkins since 2022. On the interpretation of quantum mechanics he is not neutral: he is one of the best-known advocates of the Many-Worlds view, and he opens by plugging his 2019 book Something Deeply Hidden, which argues for it.

This is lecture 7 of the series he recorded at home during the 2020 lockdown, writing on a tablet as he talks. He says up front that this one covers only the black box, the rules undergraduates are taught for using quantum mechanics; what it all means is saved for the next lecture. He can't quite hold back, and by the end his position is on the table. We have also written up a later lecture in the series, No. 16, Gravity. There he simply handed over the answer; here he takes a "pseudo-historical" route, so you can watch the data force quantum mechanics into being one step at a time.

Glossary

  • Wave function: Schrödinger's mathematical description of a quantum system's state, assigning a complex number to every point in space.
  • Born rule: the probability of finding a particle at a place is the squared magnitude of the wave function there.
  • Collapse: after a measurement, the wave function jumps from spread out to concentrated on the outcome you saw.

The episode, worked through

1. "Quantum" doesn't mean the world is pixelated

"Quantum" just means a discrete chunk, and around 1900 the data kept pushing physics toward chunks. To explain the glow of hot objects, Planck guessed that light is given off in packets whose energy is proportional to frequency, and introduced a new constant of nature to do it; Einstein went further and said light simply is particles. In 1913 Bohr, trying to stop atoms from collapsing, declared that electrons may sit only in certain orbits, with angular momentum a whole-number multiple of Planck's constant. Why? Because Bohr said so, as Carroll puts it.

The strange part is that two unrelated phenomena, each fitted to its own data, came out governed by the same constant. De Broglie supplied a reason in his PhD thesis: electrons are waves too, with wavelength equal to Planck's constant divided by momentum, and the allowed orbits are exactly those that fit a whole number of wavelengths around the loop.

Yet the Schrödinger equation itself is smooth and continuous; nothing in it comes in chunks. So where does discreteness come from? Carroll reaches for a guitar string. Pin both ends and it can only vibrate at the fundamental, twice that, three times that. An electron held near the nucleus by the electromagnetic force is in much the same position.

But, the quantumness of it is not because nature is fundamentally discrete or pixelated, it's because when you solve this wave equation in a box or near the nucleus of an atom, you get a discrete set of solutions.— Sean Carroll · [00:34:52]

A free electron in empty space can have any energy at all. Quantization is a consequence of boundary conditions, not a pixel grid built into nature.

2. One theory, two rulebooks

Which raises the obvious problem. If the electron is a wave, why does it leave a thin track in a cloud chamber or a detector at the Large Hadron Collider instead of a puffy fog? Schrödinger hoped a wave packet would narrow into a point on its own. It does the opposite: left alone, a free packet spreads out. Carroll describes the historical fix with some irony:

So, the greatest minds in the history of physics came together and decided that the right way of thinking about this was to say that when we're not looking at the electron, it's a wave, and when we look at it, it's a particle.— Sean Carroll · [00:38:54]

That is the Copenhagen interpretation, or simply the textbook one. Classical physics has two ingredients: the state of a system and an equation that evolves it. Quantum mechanics adds a separate set of rules that only switch on during a "measurement". You always see a definite outcome; you can only predict probabilities, given by the Born rule; and afterwards the wave function collapses onto what you saw.

None of that is controversial, Carroll says; it is in every textbook. The trouble is that "measurement" has never been defined. Do eyeballs count? A video camera? His cats? A single atom?

And if you ask if you're, you know, an undergraduate taking quantum mechanics and you ask these questions, you're told to stop asking those questions.— Sean Carroll · [00:48:47]

That is the measurement problem. Carroll flags a second one that tends to get swept under the rug, which he calls the reality problem: is the wave function reality itself, or just a tool for computing odds? Einstein and de Broglie hoped for hidden variables underneath, and Bohm later built such a theory; Copenhagen says forget reality and stick to observations; a third camp faces the music and takes the wave function as real. Carroll leans to the third, which is why he keeps saying the electron "is" a wave when no one is looking. The empty-atom question from the opening sits exactly here.

3. Position is not a thing

Next comes a line Carroll expects to get flak for: position and momentum do not exist when they are not being observed. They are observables, possible outcomes of a measurement, not properties a particle carries around.

From there he derives the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, by way of your ears. What reaches the ear is just air density rising and falling, yet the ear separates it into distinct pitches; the mathematics behind that is Fourier analysis. The wave function works the same way. The sharper it is in position, the more wavelengths it takes to build it, and wavelength corresponds to momentum. A sine wave with one wavelength everywhere has a definite momentum and no location at all; a spike has a definite location and any momentum whatsoever. So Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 is a constraint on every possible wave function.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle is not the statement that you disturb a quantum system when you measure it.— Sean Carroll · [01:02:14]

Disturbance is real, but it already lives in the collapse rule. How far to take that line is something the fact-check below looks at.

He closes by naming Steven Weinberg, one of the living physicists he admires most. In a general relativity textbook, Weinberg said outright that he didn't care about talk of curved spacetime; what he cared about was predicting where spectral lines would appear. Carroll could not disagree more:

In my view, physics is about understanding reality.— Sean Carroll · [01:03:45]

No twelve-year-old, he says, falls for physics dreaming of growing up to predict observational outcomes. Worth adding: Weinberg himself was less detached about quantum mechanics than that textbook suggests. In a 2017 essay for the New York Review of Books, "The Trouble with Quantum Mechanics", he wrote that neither the realist nor the instrumentalist approach seemed to him quite satisfactory.

Fact-check

The uncertainty principle "is not the statement that you disturb a quantum system when you measure it"; it is a constraint on all wave functions. [01:02:14]

Supported, but overstated

Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 is indeed a theorem about states, proved with standard deviations by Kennard in 1927. But Heisenberg himself introduced it with a disturbance argument, a photon knocking the electron in a microscope. The precision-versus-disturbance tradeoff is a separate question: a 2012 experiment showed Heisenberg's original form can be violated, and the right formulation is still argued over.

Whether atoms are mostly empty space is something physicists "don't agree" on, because they disagree about whether the wave function is real; textbooks still teach Copenhagen. [00:52:32]

Supported

A 2025 Nature survey of more than 1,100 quantum researchers: 36% favored Copenhagen, the largest share, yet only 24% thought their favored interpretation was actually correct. Among Copenhagen supporters, 29% said the wave function is real and 63% said it encodes information about probabilities.

By classical electromagnetism, an electron in a Rutherford atom would spiral into the nucleus in about 10⁻¹¹ seconds. [00:18:03]

Supported

A standard Princeton problem set runs the classical radiation formula from the Bohr radius and gets 1.6×10⁻¹¹ s, the same order of magnitude. The solution also notes that the real hydrogen ground state appears to live forever, which is exactly where the classical picture fails.

Crossovers

  • Signal processing · the Gabor limit: Carroll explains uncertainty with your ears, and signal processing has the very same theorem. Dennis Gabor showed that a signal cannot be both very short in time and very narrow in frequency; the product has a floor, and a Gaussian pulse hits it exactly. That is why a spectrogram with a short window resolves timing but smears pitch, and a long window does the reverse. Same mathematics. The difference: sound has no collapse, and no sense in which the frequency "doesn't exist" until measured; time and frequency are just two ways of writing one function.
  • AI · sampling: a language model outputs a probability distribution and only becomes a definite token when sampled, which looks a lot like the Born rule plus collapse. The mismatch is the heart of quantum mechanics: quantum theory computes amplitudes first, and amplitudes can cancel (interference) before being squared into probabilities; softmax probabilities only ever add.
  • Philosophy of mind · Wigner's friend: Carroll asks whether his cats can measure. In 1961 Eugene Wigner pushed the same question all the way to consciousness: if a friend makes a measurement in a sealed lab, does the wave function collapse when the friend looks, or when Wigner hears about it? His conclusion at the time was that the Schrödinger equation cannot apply to a conscious being. Mainstream physics doesn't follow him there, but it shows how the measurement problem, pressed hard enough, ends up at the door of the philosophy of mind.
  • Buddhism · Nagarjuna's emptiness: "position doesn't exist until observed" sounds a lot like Nagarjuna's claim that things lack inherent existence and arise only in relation. Carlo Rovelli devotes part of Helgoland to Nagarjuna to illuminate his relational interpretation. But Carroll runs the other way: he grants the wave function full reality and strips away only classical properties like position. In his picture, not everything is empty.

Ten takeaways

  1. By classical electromagnetism, orbiting electrons radiate continuously, so the Rutherford atom would collapse in about 10⁻¹¹ seconds and cannot be what atoms really look like.
  2. Planck's light quanta and Bohr's quantized orbits are governed by the same Planck constant, and de Broglie unified them by proposing that electrons are waves too.
  3. The Schrödinger equation is continuous; quantization comes from boundary conditions, the way a guitar string pinned at both ends can only vibrate at certain frequencies.
  4. The Copenhagen interpretation adds a measurement rulebook on top of state and equation: outcomes are definite, probabilities follow the Born rule, and the wave function collapses afterwards.
  5. The measurement problem is that measurement plays a central role in quantum mechanics yet has never been defined; nobody can say whether a cat, a camera or an atom counts.
  6. If the wave function is real, atoms are not mostly empty space but filled with the electron's wave function, and physicists still have no consensus on this.
  7. Sean Carroll argues that position and momentum are possible measurement outcomes, not properties a particle carries while unobserved.
  8. Sean Carroll stresses that the Heisenberg uncertainty principle follows from Fourier analysis as a constraint on all wave functions, and is not by itself a claim about measurement disturbing a system.
  9. Sean Carroll rejects Steven Weinberg's stance of caring only about predicting observations, arguing that the goal of physics is to understand reality.
  10. The Gabor limit in signal processing is the same mathematics as the Heisenberg uncertainty principle: a signal cannot be both very short in time and very narrow in frequency.
