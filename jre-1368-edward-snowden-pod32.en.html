Snowden: the scandal isn't that they broke the law, it's that they didn't have to

Edward Snowden × Joe Rogan · Joe Rogan Experience #1368 - Edward Snowden

Show The Joe Rogan Experience · #1368 · 2019-10-23 · Length 169 min · Guest Edward Snowden · Host Joe Rogan · Original YouTube

The moment

In 2012 Snowden was working in Hawaii, in an office almost nobody had heard of: the Office of Information Sharing, staffed by exactly one person, him. With little to do, he picked up side jobs for the Windows admin team next door. One of them was running "dirty word searches": automated sweeps across the network for highly classified files sitting on machines not cleared to hold them.

One day a search came back with a marker he had never seen: STLW.

"Unless you know what stellar wind is, you don't know how to handle it. All I knew is it wasn't supposed to be on my system."— Edward Snowden · [01:11:11]

It was a draft report by the NSA's inspector general on Stellar Wind, the domestic mass-surveillance program the Bush White House launched in secret after 9/11. Snowden says he had read the unclassified version back in Japan, and that the classified one was not the same text with lines blacked out; it was a different document altogether. That, he says, is when he understood what real conspiracies look like: not smoking men behind closed doors, but lawyers and politicians afraid of losing their jobs.

Who's talking, and when

Snowden comes from what he calls a "federal family": a father who spent thirty years in the Coast Guard, a grandfather who was an admiral and later joined the FBI, a mother who works for the federal courts, the same kind of courts that now want to jail him for life. He never finished high school and climbed on technical skill plus a security clearance. Around 2005 he was on the night shift at CIA headquarters, running servers for the whole Washington area. He remembers hallway lights blinking on as he walked and going dark behind him, and flags on the walls stirring in the air conditioning like ghosts.

Joe Rogan's show is famous for its length. He usually lets a guest run, cutting in now and then with a common-sense pushback. This episode is an extreme case: Rogan admits he hasn't read the book, and Snowden holds the floor for long stretches.

It was recorded in October 2019. A month earlier Snowden's memoir Permanent Record had come out, and the Justice Department sued him and his publisher the same day, arguing he had skipped the government's prepublication review and seeking his proceeds. Snowden says that is why he came on: he feared the book would be smothered. By then he had lived in Russia for six years; in 2022 he became a Russian citizen.

Glossary

  • FISC: the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, a secret court that approves intelligence surveillance orders and normally hears only the government.
  • Metadata: who called whom, when, and for how long, without the content of the call.
  • Third-party doctrine: records you hand to a company lose Fourth Amendment protection against warrantless search.

The episode, worked through

Fear is a dial

Snowden's account of 9/11 departs from the official story. The official line is that legal walls between agencies kept information from flowing. Snowden says that is half true. Most of those walls were procedural; if the FBI had really wanted to hand the CIA everything, it could have. What actually blocked the flow was turf: every agency wanted to be the one that broke the case. Rogan cuts in to say that's exactly what he was going to ask, and Snowden adds that this happens in any big company, where everyone is writing up their achievements at year's end.

Yet the remedy prescribed was not to make someone responsible for bridging the agencies. It was mass surveillance. Snowden pictures what Dick Cheney did as a dial labeled "what we're not allowed to do," which Cheney turned until it snapped off.

The strongest part of this thread is a pattern that keeps recurring. After the New York Times exposed warrantless wiretapping in 2005, the phone companies, afraid of being sued into bankruptcy, told the government they would stop cooperating unless Congress shielded them. What followed was the Protect America Act of 2007 and the FISA Amendments Act of 2008: the unlawful programs did not stop; they got a legal basis.

"They make the law comply with what the agencies want to do rather than make the agencies comply with the law."— Edward Snowden · [01:18:12]

Rogan twice argues the other side. First he asks whether the NSA evacuated its headquarters on 9/11 because it really might have been a target. Snowden grants it was possible but calls it a matter of risk assessment: at the moment the country needed its spies most, their leaders chose not to be blamed. Later Rogan says the presidency is an impossible job; no one can know what every agency is doing. Snowden runs with it: the problem is that the president has too much power. Each new president gets "feared up" on arrival, read the full litany of threats, then handed the asks. The briefers have given that briefing many times and are no longer scared; the president is hearing it for the first time. That, he says, is all the "deep state" really is: not lizard people, just the career government that outlasts every administration.

Between knowing and proving

What bothers Snowden most is not that surveillance existed but that the public could not prove it did. He describes a closed loop. To get a court to rule on a program's legality, you must first show it exists. The program is classified, the evidence sits with the government, the government answers everything with "neither confirm nor deny," and handing the evidence to a reporter or a court is a felony under the Espionage Act, up to ten years per count. So the courts say: you have no evidence.

"The distance between what you know and what you can prove to everybody else in the country is everything in our model of government."— Edward Snowden · [01:51:52]

To people who say "we already knew," he answers that you may have guessed, even been sure, but a guess cannot move Congress to vote. In March 2013 Senator Ron Wyden sent his question to Director of National Intelligence James Clapper a day in advance, then asked him publicly whether the NSA collected data on millions or hundreds of millions of Americans. Under oath, Clapper said no. Wyden knew it was false and, bound by secrecy, could not say so.

That loop also explains why he did not simply post the files online. His rules for the journalists were to publish only stories their papers would stand behind as being in the public interest, and to ask the government before each one, giving it a chance to object. He calls it an adversarial step deliberately built into the process.

As for why he took so long, he says skepticism builds up like radiation exposure, and he kept waiting for someone else to come forward.

"But there are no heroes, right? There's only heroic decisions."— Edward Snowden · [02:21:56]

The permanent record

He explains the book's title concretely. Surveillance used to be targeted because it had to be: sending someone to clip onto an embassy line, plant a bug, build a satellite listening post. Cost limited it to a few people. Technology changed the cost, and surveillance could become indiscriminate. A phone with its screen off is still announcing two globally unique numbers to the nearest tower, one burned into the handset (IMEI) and one on the SIM (IMSI), and the tower notes that this phone is here now. Turn off GPS and nearby Wi-Fi access points still place you, because Street View cars have already mapped them.

"Everything that we do now lasts forever. Not because we want to remember, but because we're not allowed to forget."— Edward Snowden · [01:33:11]

The legal foundation is Smith v. Maryland (1979). A man kept making harassing calls to a woman; police got his dialing records from the phone company without a warrant, and the Supreme Court held the records were the company's, not his. Forty years later the same principle carries bulk collection of everyone. Hence the sharpest line of the episode:

"The scandal isn't how they're breaking the law. The scandal is that they don't have to break the law."— Edward Snowden · [02:43:22]

His fix is an engineer's fix: a phone should show which servers each app is talking to and let you cut each connection. Apple and Google say that is a security risk and too complex for users. Snowden calls that a confession of the problem: a device too complex for its owner to govern is like a government too big for its president.

"if you have to be the president of the phone and the phone is as complex as the United States government, we have a problem, guys."— Edward Snowden · [02:38:17]

Claim check

The FISA court approved these programs 15 times, then was overruled by the first open courts to look, which found them unlawful and likely unconstitutional. [01:38:41]

Supported, but overstated

In ACLU v. Clapper (2015) the Second Circuit held that bulk phone-metadata collection exceeded what Section 215 of the Patriot Act authorized, but said explicitly it did not reach the constitutional questions. Nor does it sit above the FISC, so "overruled" is loose.

Six years on, no one has been hurt by the disclosures; the government has shown no bodies. [01:17:23]

Insufficient evidence

The House Intelligence Committee's 2016 review says the leaks did "tremendous damage," but its most specific public point is that the Pentagon found 13 high-risk issues and that troops "will be at greater risk" if Russia or China has the material, with details redacted. The public record proves neither harm nor its absence.

The Espionage Act is a "strict liability" crime; the jury may not consider why you did it. [02:10:30]

Supported, but overstated

Section 793(e) requires that disclosure be "willful" and that the person have reason to believe the information could injure the United States, so it is not strict liability in the legal sense. In practice motive is often excluded: in the 2019 Hale case prosecutors asked the court to bar any public-interest argument.

Crossing over

  • Neuroscience · forgetting as a feature: In a 2017 review, Richards and Frankland argue the brain forgets on purpose: to keep outdated information from steering decisions and to avoid overfitting to particular past events, so it can generalize. That gives "not allowed to forget" a biological edge: a system that discards nothing gets pinned to every accident of its past. The difference is that the brain's forgetting serves the memory's owner; a database's memory serves someone else, who also decides what gets forgotten.
  • Data science · "Unique in the Crowd": de Montjoye and colleagues analyzed mobility data for 1.5 million people in 2013 and found that four spatio-temporal points uniquely identify 95% of them. That is the quantitative version of Snowden's point that your phone's movements are your movements: metadata carries no content, yet it is enough to say who you are.
  • Distributed systems · Certificate Transparency: browsers stopped simply trusting certificate authorities not to misissue and instead require every certificate to be written to public, append-only logs, turning misbehavior from suspected into provable. That is exactly Snowden's gap between knowing and proving. The difference: CT designs auditability into the system, while Snowden could only get the evidence out by breaking its rules.
  • Philosophy · Bentham's Panopticon: one side sees everything, the other sees nothing, the same asymmetry Snowden describes. But the Panopticon works because prisoners know they may be watched and police themselves; secret bulk collection works the other way, drawing its power from your not knowing, and losing some of its legitimacy the moment it is exposed.
  • Economic history · Robert Higgs's ratchet: in Crisis and Leviathan, Higgs argues that each crisis expands government, which never fully retreats afterward. Snowden's snapped dial and laws bent to fit the agencies are that ratchet as it looks in intelligence.

Ten takeaways

  1. Edward Snowden stumbled on the NSA inspector general's classified draft report on Stellar Wind while running dirty word searches in Hawaii.
  2. Snowden argues the 9/11 intelligence failure came mainly from turf wars between agencies, not legal walls, yet the remedy became mass surveillance.
  3. Snowden sees a recurring pattern: when an unlawful program is exposed, Congress changes the law to make it legal instead of stopping it.
  4. Snowden says every new president gets "feared up" by the intelligence community, by briefers who have given the briefing many times before.
  5. Snowden describes a closed loop: suing requires proving a program exists, the proof is classified, and handing it over is a felony.
  6. Snowden holds that democracy turns on the gap between knowing something and proving it to everyone, a gap mostly closed by whistleblowers.
  7. Snowden's rule for journalists: publish only public-interest stories, and give the government a chance to object before each one.
  8. Technology drove the cost of surveillance toward zero, turning it from targeted to bulk; even a sleeping phone announces its identifiers to the tower.
  9. Smith v. Maryland (1979) established the third-party doctrine: records held by a company are not protected by the warrant requirement.
  10. Neuroscientists Richards and Frankland argue forgetting keeps the brain from overfitting to its past, a protection a permanent record takes away.
