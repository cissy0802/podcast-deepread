A Multiplanetary Species: It All Comes Down to Cost per Ton

Why Mars, why the rocket has to be fully reusable, and where Starlink and the Moon fit on the way

Topic A multiplanetary species: SpaceX, Starship and Mars · Sources 7 interview transcripts (2021–2026), SpaceX's 2026 S-1 prospectus · Interviews Lex Fridman #252 (2021) · Ron Baron Investment Conference (2022) · TED (2022) · Joe Rogan #2404 (2025) · Nikhil Kamath (2025) · Peter Diamandis #220 (2026) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

What he claims

When Musk talks about Mars, the examples change from year to year, but the skeleton barely moves. Humanity has to become a multiplanetary species; a city on Mars has to be able to keep itself alive if the supply ships from Earth stop; and the only way to get there is to make shipping a ton of cargo a thousand times cheaper. This piece follows that skeleton, then looks at how the Starlink numbers made public in 2026, and a new "Moon first" plan, have reshaped it.

1. Why Mars: an insurance policy for consciousness

His fullest version came at the end of 2021, talking to Lex Fridman. Sooner or later Earth will meet a catastrophe, whether self-inflicted or the kind of visitor from space that ended the dinosaurs; and even if nothing like that happens, the Sun is slowly expanding, and in roughly 500 million years Earth will be too hot for life. Borrowing Stephen Hawking's estimate of a 1% chance per century of a civilization-ending event, he framed becoming multiplanetary as a kind of insurance:

Taking out insurance for life itself, like life insurance for life.— Musk · Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [00:31:53]

There is a clock hidden in the argument. In four and a half billion years, this is the first time it has been possible to carry life off the planet; the window may stay open a long time or close soon, so the sensible move is to go while it is open. At Ron Baron's investment conference in 2022 he split the case in two. One reason is defensive: keep the light of consciousness from going out. The other is that it is exciting, the way Apollo was, something that makes people wake up looking forward to the future. For him the second reason is not decoration; it carries as much weight as the first.

So why Mars in particular? His answer is unromantic. Mars is not a nice place, just the least inhospitable planet in the solar system, "a fixer-upper." The real hurdle is self-sufficiency. The test he keeps coming back to is whether the Mars city dies if the ships from Earth stop coming for any reason, and missing a single ingredient counts as failing:

If you're on a long sea voyage and you've got everything except vitamin C, it's only a matter of time, you know, you're going to die.— Musk · Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [00:35:19]

Hence his estimate that a self-sustaining city needs at least a million tons of material on the surface, with chip fabs and iron refineries, nothing left out. He admits it may not happen in his lifetime. At TED in 2022 he put it in people instead, roughly a million of them, and warned that early Mars would be dangerous, cramped and hard work, no luxury trip.

From 2025 on, the case picks up a curious twist. He has taken to saying that Mars is actually rather dull:

I think we should extend life to Mars, but it's basically a bunch of rocks. It's not as interesting as Earth.— Musk · Nikhil Kamath, 2025 [01:19:41]

He said it while talking about AI: a curious, truth-seeking AI would find humans more interesting than a pile of rocks, and so would want humanity to continue. In 2026 Dwarkesh Patel pushed on exactly that. If AI is the risk you worry about most, Grok will be on the ship to Mars too, so doesn't the risk travel with you? Musk first said he was not sure AI was his main worry, then that most intelligence in the future will be AI, and then restated the insurance policy:

So you want to take the set of actions that maximize the probable light cone of consciousness.— Musk · Dwarkesh Patel, 2026 [00:37:07]

A light cone, in physics, is all of space and time that an event can go on to influence. When Dwarkesh asked whether that meant AI would carry on the journey on Mars even if humans did not make it, Musk added that he is "very pro-human" and wants humans along for the ride. Still, what the policy insures has quietly widened from human civilization to intelligence and consciousness in general.

2. It all comes down to cost per ton

Asked by Lex what stands in the way of landing people on Mars, he said it is engineering, and that Starship optimizes for a single number: the cost of putting a ton into orbit, and ultimately a ton onto the surface of Mars. His starting point:

Rough approximation would be a billion dollars per ton to the surface of Mars right now. This is obviously way too expensive to create a self-sustaining civilization.— Musk · Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [00:33:39]

That billion covers not just the rocket but the heat shield, guidance, deep-space communications and a landing system. It has to come down by at least a factor of a thousand, to around a million dollars a ton, ideally much less. The arithmetic runs like this. Falcon 9 recovers its booster and its fairing but throws away the upper stage, which alone costs at least $10 million, so the marginal cost of a flight is around $15 to $20 million. Full and rapid reuse would cut the cost per ton to orbit by another factor of a hundred: a Starship flight could in theory cost one or two million dollars and lift more than a hundred tons. His favorite analogy is the car. If you had to buy a new car every time you went for a drive, nobody could afford to drive.

If reuse is worth that much, why has nobody else done it? Because, he says, Earth's gravity is strong and its atmosphere thick:

With known physics, it is only barely possible to make a fully reusable orbital rocket. Only barely possible.— Musk · Ron Baron Investment Conference, 2022 [00:23:18]

If this were a video game, he says, the difficulty would be set to extreme, but not impossible. "Barely possible" means everything has to be the best it can be: engine efficiency, structural mass, the heat shield. The ship also has to be refilled with propellant in orbit, like aerial refueling, or it cannot reach Mars. To save the weight of landing legs, SpaceX catches returning rockets with two arms on the launch tower. Even the choice of methane and liquid oxygen is about Mars: its atmosphere is carbon dioxide and it has water ice, so both can be made there as fuel for the trip home. And because the Earth–Mars launch window opens only about every two years, he reckons the city would take on the order of a thousand Starships.

How those parts get built fast and cheap belongs to the pieces on first principles and the "five-step algorithm" and on the factory as the product. In his telling, the Raptor engine, stainless steel and the tower catch all reduce to the same number in the end: cost per ton.

3. Starlink and the Moon: revenue and detours on the road to Mars

Who pays for a Mars city? In the interviews used here, Musk rarely calls Starlink a cash cow feeding the Mars program; that framing mostly comes from others. In 2026 Dwarkesh summed it up this way: the endpoint of SpaceX is Mars, but on the way it keeps finding the next revenue stream; Falcon 9 begat Starlink, and Starship may beget data centers in orbit. When Musk himself talks about Starlink, he talks about reach. It operates in 150 countries, works best in the countryside where fiber never arrives, and is free during natural disasters. He also talks about scale, noting that most of what Falcon launches that year is Starlink:

SpaceX this year will deliver roughly 90% of all Earth mass to orbit.— Musk · Joe Rogan #2404, 2025 [00:27:51]

The books opened only in 2026. SpaceX's S-1 prospectus, filed that May, shows its Starlink-driven Connectivity segment earning $11.39 billion in revenue and $4.42 billion in operating income in 2025. The same year, the Space segment spent $3.0 billion on Starship research and development and swung from a small profit to an operating loss of $657 million. In other words, one year of Starlink profit more than covers one year of Starship R&D. The cash-cow story is true; it is just the ledger telling it, not him.

Even before the books opened, his tone on Mars had shifted. In an episode released in January 2026, Peter Diamandis asked whether SpaceX would go for Mars in the next window, at the end of 2026. His answer:

We could, but it would be a low probability shot and somewhat of a distraction.— Musk · Peter Diamandis #220, 2026 [01:36:31]

2028 or 2029 is "not out of the question," he said, before pivoting: space needs a new game plan, starting with a permanently crewed Moon base. A month later, with Dwarkesh, what lit him up was something else: a million tons a year to orbit, with solar-powered AI satellites carrying a hundred kilowatts per ton, and later a mass driver on the Moon flinging locally built satellites into space. That same month he posted on X that SpaceX had already shifted focus to building a "self-growing city" on the Moon, because that might be done in under ten years while Mars would take twenty-plus, with work on a Mars city starting in about five to seven. Mars is still the destination, but the main cargo on the road there has become compute.

How the claims interlock

The three claims form a chain worked backward from the goal. The point is for consciousness to survive a catastrophe on Earth; for that, a Mars city must survive being cut off; for that, it needs at least a million tons of material and about a million people; to afford that, cost per ton must fall a thousandfold; for that, the rocket must be fully and rapidly reusable; and to build such a rocket and keep it flying often enough, you need side businesses along the way, first Starlink and now AI satellites. The space-compute end of the chain is left to the piece on AI, from "summoning the demon" to xAI. Even his pay hangs on the end of the chain: according to the prospectus, in January 2026 the SpaceX board granted him a billion restricted shares, one condition for which is a permanent human colony on Mars with at least a million inhabitants.

The weakest link is self-sufficiency. For the insurance argument to work, the Mars city's risks have to be independent of Earth's. Until it can survive a cutoff, it depends on Earth for everything, and if Earth goes down, so does it. He concedes the threshold may not be crossed in his lifetime, and the 2026 pivot pushes it further out. The Moon is two days away and allows much faster iteration, but whether a city two days from Earth counts as a backup for Earth is even less clear. A second tension sits in the beneficiary of the policy. Once what matters is the light cone of consciousness and intelligence, AI included, "humans must go to Mars" and "intelligence must continue" are no longer the same claim.

Limits

"Best case is about five years, worst case ten years" for landing humans on Mars. — Lex Fridman #252, December 2021 [00:28:08]

Best case now due, not met

The five-year best case falls at the end of 2026. In September 2024 he also posted that the first uncrewed Starships would leave in that window. Starship first reached orbit only on its 14th flight, on 28 September 2026, and the June prospectus still listed catching the ship and in-orbit propellant transfer, both prerequisites for Mars, as milestones it "plans to demonstrate." The ten-year worst case falls in 2031 and is not yet due.

Full and rapid reuse can cut the cost per ton to orbit "by a factor of 100." — Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [00:38:59]; to Baron in 2022 he said perhaps a thousand

Insufficient evidence

Full reuse has not happened yet. In October 2025 he told Rogan it would come "next year"; the ship on the September 2026 orbital flight still ended in the Pacific, unrecovered. The prospectus, written under securities law, is more cautious: its stated aim is 99% or more below the historical average of $18,500 per kilogram, about $185 or less, while by NASA's estimate Falcon 9 was already near $2,700 per kilogram in 2010. On that basis the target is roughly fifteen times cheaper than the 2010 Falcon 9 as a floor, and it never says a hundred.

Crossovers

  • Actuarial science · Correlated risk: Insurance can pay out because the insurer and the insured do not get hit at the same time; houses on the same street insuring each other are wiped out by one fire. Musk's "life insurance for life" becomes real insurance only once the Mars city is self-sufficient; until then it is the same risk as Earth. The parallel is that his own threshold is exactly this test: can it survive a cutoff? The difference is that an insurer spreads risk across thousands of unrelated policies, while humanity currently has one spare.
  • Conservation biology · Minimum viable population: Traill and colleagues (2007) pooled thirty years of studies and found a median minimum viable population of about 4,169 individuals across species, and later critics argued that no universal number exists. Musk's million is far above that because he is counting not genetic diversity but how many people it takes to staff chip fabs and iron refineries.
  • Economics · Smith on the division of labor: In Book I, Chapter 3 of The Wealth of Nations, Adam Smith argued that the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market; in a remote village, every farmer has to be his own butcher, baker and brewer. A Mars city that makes even its own chips needs a market large enough to support that fine a division of labor. It is the best theoretical footnote to "a million people," and it explains why the threshold is so hard to lower.

Ten takeaways

  1. Musk calls becoming multiplanetary "life insurance for life," because Earth will eventually face catastrophe and the expanding Sun will make it uninhabitable in about 500 million years.
  2. Musk treats Mars as merely the least inhospitable planet; the real threshold is whether a Mars city survives if ships from Earth stop coming.
  3. Musk estimates a self-sustaining Mars city needs at least a million tons of material, about a million people, and its own chip fabs and iron refineries.
  4. Since 2025 Musk has called Mars a bunch of rocks and widened what must survive to "the light cone of consciousness," AI included.
  5. In 2021 Musk put the cost of landing a ton on Mars at about a billion dollars, and said it must fall at least a thousandfold before a city is affordable.
  6. Musk sees full and rapid reuse as the key to cheaper tons in orbit: if every drive meant buying a new car, nobody could afford to drive.
  7. Musk says Earth's strong gravity and thick atmosphere make a fully reusable orbital rocket only barely possible, and Mars also requires refilling propellant in orbit.
  8. SpaceX's prospectus shows its Starlink-driven Connectivity segment earned $4.4 billion in operating income in 2025, more than that year's roughly $3 billion of Starship R&D.
  9. In 2026 Musk shifted SpaceX's focus to a Moon city and space-based AI satellites, saying the Moon might take under ten years and a Mars city twenty-plus.
  10. Insurance only works when the two parties' risks are uncorrelated; until it is self-sufficient, a Mars city carries the same risk as Earth.
