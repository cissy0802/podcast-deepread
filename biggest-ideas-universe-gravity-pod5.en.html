The coffee cup on your table is accelerating

Sean Carroll, solo lecture · The Biggest Ideas in the Universe | 16. Gravity

Show The Biggest Ideas in the Universe · Lecture 16 · 2020-07-07 · Length 110 min · Speaker Sean Carroll (solo lecture, no host) · Video YouTube

The moment

Twenty-three minutes in, Carroll sketches a table on his tablet and puts a coffee mug on it, with a nod back to the topology lecture: topologists can't tell a coffee cup from a donut. Then he asks: is the cup accelerating?

Newton says no. Its velocity is zero and stays zero. Einstein says yes. The natural state of motion is free fall, and the table is constantly pushing the cup away from the path it would otherwise follow. Standing up, you feel that push in your feet; sitting down, in your backside [00:23:05]. A few minutes later he draws the conclusion:

So you are accelerating right now, unless you just jumped off a building or something like that.— Sean Carroll · [00:26:10]

The remaining eighty minutes turn that throwaway joke into an equation.

Who's talking, and when

Sean Carroll is a theoretical physicist working on cosmology and quantum gravity, and the author of a general relativity textbook. He says he opened that book by calling general relativity the most beautiful physical theory ever invented. He loves quantum mechanics more, he admits, but GR is prettier, and easier.

This is a YouTube lecture series he ran in 2020: one person, a tablet, live handwriting. Ariel complains in the background and he apologizes for not playing fetch with her right now [00:00:00]. Fifteen earlier lectures had laid down spacetime, geometry and gauge theory, so this one dares to fit all of general relativity into a single episode. He writes slowly, gets sloppy when he rushes, erases and rewrites, and notes there's a life lesson in that somewhere. The series later became the first volume of his 2022 trilogy of the same name.

Glossary

  • Metric: a little 4×4 table at every point that tells you how far apart in space and time two neighboring events are. Everything in GR is computed from it.
  • Geodesic: the closest thing to a straight line in curved spacetime. Objects that nothing is pushing follow one.
  • Action: a single number that the system's real history makes stationary. Write down the action and the equations of motion follow.

The episode, worked through

1. "In a small region": how a thought experiment became geometry

The starting point is Einstein's sealed box. If there's an electric field, drop in an electron and a proton: they accelerate in opposite directions, and you know the field is there. Gravity won't cooperate. Heavy and light objects fall the same way, and a rocket accelerating at 1g would produce exactly the same result [00:07:25]. That's the principle of equivalence.

Carroll keeps returning to one clause in it: in a small region of spacetime. People keep proposing counterexamples, he says, and usually they've dropped that clause. Make the box bigger than the Earth and two balls dropped at opposite ends drift toward each other as they fall toward the center, which wouldn't happen in a rocket [00:13:43]. Then he names the trick: this is calculus. Zoom in far enough on a curve and it looks straight. Gauss and Riemann's differential geometry says the same of curved space: zoom in and it looks flat, which is why some people think the Earth is flat. Einstein made it a statement about spacetime: locally, physics is special relativity. He didn't know the math. His old Zurich friend, the mathematician Marcel Grossmann, taught him Riemannian geometry [00:19:57]. As mathematics it fits in one line: you can always choose coordinates in which the metric is exactly flat at one point, and all the curvature lives in how it changes away from that point [00:45:52].

So is gravity a force or not? Right after learning GR, Carroll admits, he went around correcting people who called it one:

Now that I'm a little bit more mature than that, I know that really the answer is: who cares.— Sean Carroll · [00:21:34]

"Force" is a sometimes-convenient word, and in quantum field theory there's no F=ma at all. The shift that matters is the one behind the coffee cup: what counts as being left alone.

2. The equation was guessed, and could have been derived

"Gravity is curvature" still needs an equation. Einstein's method was to guess. Curvature on the left, energy and momentum on the right. His first guess, Ricci tensor proportional to the energy-momentum tensor, didn't conserve energy. He noticed quickly and fixed it with the combination now named after him. Not bad, Carroll says, for someone who didn't love math [01:03:29]. The constant is fixed by demanding that weak, slow gravity reduce to Newton, which gives 8πG [01:05:07].

Hilbert went another way. Instead of guessing tensor equations full of indices, guess one scalar action. There are far fewer scalars around, and the curvature scalar R is sitting right there. Vary it and out comes the same equation, with energy conservation built in automatically.

That's why figuring out new theories by writing down their Lagrangians is a much more sensible thing to do than by guessing the equations of motion like Einstein did.— Sean Carroll · [01:09:45]

On priority he sides with Einstein: Hilbert may have said it out loud first, but he was working with Einstein's ideas from the start [01:11:19].

Where this thread lands: the equation gives back more than you put in. Mercury's perihelion had a precise, known discrepancy with Newton. Astronomers, copying the playbook that found Neptune, posited a hidden planet near the Sun, Vulcan. Einstein said: stop looking, change the theory of gravity. Carroll hangs the lesson on dark matter: we've faced the same choice there and concluded there really is new stuff, though we're not 100% sure [01:22:39]. Light bending and cosmology make the point more sharply: Newtonian gravity doesn't even give a unique answer for them, and GR gives exactly one. One small slip: he says Schwarzschild found his exact solution at the front in 1917 and died of something like pneumonia; it was 1916, and the disease was pemphigus.

3. Coordinates lie

Carroll's running warning: the metric's numbers depend on your coordinates; only curvature is real. That single idea clears up three familiar puzzles.

"What is the universe expanding into?" Nothing. Differential geometry since Gauss works from inside the manifold; expansion just means the distance between two galaxies at fixed coordinates grows with time [00:49:18]. "Clocks run slower in a gravitational field." Slightly misleading:

Clocks run at one second per second, no matter where they are.— Sean Carroll · [01:35:28]

Only when you bring two clocks together can you see which one recorded less time.

And black holes, which he calls the week-eight reward for students in a GR course [01:38:45]. At r=2GM one term of the Schwarzschild metric goes to zero and another to infinity. It looks alarming, but it's just a bad choice of coordinates. Draw light cones on a spacetime diagram: the closer to the horizon, the more they tip toward the hole, and at the horizon every allowed future points to smaller r [01:40:26]. Inside, the signs of the t and r terms swap, and r becomes time:

The singularity is a moment in your future.— Sean Carroll · [01:43:58]

That's why you can't avoid it once you're inside, any more than you can avoid tomorrow: it isn't at a center in space but ahead of you in time. In Kruskal coordinates the horizon is an ordinary light-cone surface and the singularity a line across the top of the diagram. He closes by placing the whole theory as an effective field theory: it's classical, so it must change at short distances, but there's no reason to change it at long distances, and no deviation has been seen [01:46:48].

Fact-check

"We've tested at distances about that big, about a tenth of a millimeter, to be honest" — and above that, Einstein's equation holds. [01:48:15]

Supported

Four months before this lecture (March 2020), the University of Washington torsion-balance group had tested the inverse-square law down to 52 micrometers, still matching Newton. If anything he undersold it. One caveat: sub-millimeter tests probe the weak-field limit; strong fields are tested elsewhere.

Einstein's cosmological constant wasn't philosophical prejudice for a static universe: he was briefly excited by expansion, asked his astronomer friends, heard the universe was static, and did "the correct scientific thing to do"; in 1927 Hubble showed it was expanding. [01:30:18]

Supported, but overstated

The first half holds: the 1917 paper bases stasis on the observed fact that stellar velocities are tiny compared with light. The second half doesn't: historians find non-static solutions held little interest for him then, and he later publicly faulted Friedmann and called Lemaître's physics "abominable." Hubble's distance-velocity relation came in 1929.

On the 1919 eclipse expedition, Eddington "may have fudged his data or not, it's not clear to me." [01:24:11]

Insufficient evidence

The discarded plates came from one Sobral instrument, and the archive shows Dyson, not Eddington, made the call. A 1979 re-measurement of those plates moved their deflection from 0.93″ to 1.55″±0.34″, supporting Einstein's 1.75″.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Lamport clocks: Lamport's 1978 logical clocks paper itself says "happened before" will feel natural to anyone who knows the spacetime formulation of special relativity. Carroll's "one second per second" is the same stance: no global clock, only local time, and comparisons have to travel along paths a signal can take; the light cone is the region of causal reach. Where it differs: relativity has a metric and can compute how far two clocks diverge, while Lamport needs only a partial order and never measures intervals. In this analogy a horizon is a one-way network partition: messages get in, nothing comes out.
  • Neuroscience · otolith organs: the inner ear's otoliths sense only the sum of gravity and acceleration, so on their own they can't tell head tilt from forward acceleration. Pilots taking off hard into a dark sky can feel the nose pitching up: the somatogravic illusion. Here the equivalence principle is a perceptual blind spot the brain patches with the semicircular canals and vision.
  • AI · write the loss, not the update: Hilbert wrote one scalar and let variation produce the equations; deep learning writes one scalar loss and lets differentiation produce the update rule. Alike in packing the constraints into a scalar and letting math do the rest; unlike in that an action only has to be stationary, not optimized step by step.
  • Philosophy of science · Lakatos's research programmes: Neptune and Vulcan are Lakatos's two outcomes. Add an auxiliary hypothesis (one more planet), see it confirmed, and the programme progresses; keep adding ones that fail, and it's time to touch the hard core. Dark matter is still at the stage where observation has to tell the two apart.
  • Buddhism · the two truths: Carroll's "force" as a useful word rather than nature's ontology sits close to Madhyamaka's conventional truth: agreed-upon and workable, but not ultimate. The difference: physics still treats invariant curvature beneath the coordinates as real, while Madhyamaka grants no intrinsic nature even there.

Ten takeaways

  1. Sean Carroll uses a coffee cup to explain general relativity: a cup resting on a table is accelerating, because the table pushes it off its free-fall path.
  2. The equivalence principle holds only in a small patch of spacetime; over a larger region, gravity and acceleration can be told apart.
  3. Locally spacetime is flat, as a curve looks straight up close; all the curvature lives in how the metric changes away from that point.
  4. Asked whether gravity is a force, Carroll answers "who cares": "force" is just a sometimes-useful word.
  5. Einstein guessed his field equations; Hilbert derived the same equations from a single scalar action, which Carroll calls the wiser route.
  6. General relativity explained the precession of Mercury's perihelion and gives a single answer for the bending of light.
  7. The metric's values depend on coordinates; only curvature is real, and the universe is not expanding into anything.
  8. Inside a black hole's horizon, r becomes the time direction: the singularity is not a place at the center but a moment in the future.
  9. Checked: the inverse-square law of gravity has been confirmed down to 52 micrometers, smaller than the tenth of a millimeter Carroll cited.
  10. Checked: Einstein was never excited by expanding solutions; Carroll overstates the story of the cosmological constant.
