Huberman Lab · 2024

Only a stick, and a brain region handed over on the spot

David Goggins × Andrew Huberman · "How to Build Immense Inner Strength"

Show Huberman Lab · 2024-01-01 · Length 158 min · Guest David Goggins · Host Andrew Huberman · Video YouTube

The moment

Huberman opens by asking what Goggins is learning these days. Not running: a paramedic textbook. Goggins says he has ADHD and can't retain things, so he copies the same page by hand, again and again, until he can see it with his eyes closed. During the national exam he flips through pages in his head, to page 69, to find the answer [00:15:26]. At two in the morning a drug name surfaces and he gets up to check the book [00:20:19].

Passing doesn't stop it. Four-plus hours a day, he re-studies material he has already passed, as if the test were tomorrow. He is, by his own account, 49 and a multimillionaire who doesn't have to do any of this [00:12:08]. Huberman sums it up as "all stick, no carrot." Goggins takes it from there:

There's no stick, or there's only a stick. There's never been a carrot.— David Goggins · [00:37:01]

The next two and a half hours are mostly an attempt to explain that sentence: Goggins with his life, Huberman with a patch of cortex.

Who's talking, and when

Goggins is a retired Navy SEAL and ultramarathoner, author of Can't Hurt Me and Never Finished. He says that at 24 he weighed 300 pounds, could barely read or write, and stuttered. At recording he has had several knee surgeries and two heart surgeries, and still spends three to five months a year as a smoke jumper, parachuting from planes to fight wildfires no one can reach by road [02:02:23].

Huberman is a Stanford neurobiologist whose episodes usually land on a protocol: when to get sunlight, how long to sit in cold water. This time he deliberately offers none, and says he had misread Goggins as all forward momentum; today he sees it is the stick. They go back to late 2016, when Goggins visited his lab. Goggins says this is only his second podcast since the new book, the first being Rogan's. The episode went up on New Year's Day 2024, resolution day.

The episode, worked through

1. Two voices, and a curriculum of failure

Goggins's model is simple. Everyone starts with one voice that says you can't. In the freezing water of Hell Week, he says, he built a second voice to argue with the first, and made the second one win, every time [01:54:54]. Huberman recasts this as a committee: strong and weak voices at one table; you sit through the whole meeting, turn the decision into action, and the world only sees the action [02:16:37].

Goggins adds a rule at once: there is no seat on that committee for being kind to yourself. At 300 pounds, he asks, where would soft words have taken him?

I tell you where it gets me: right back to 7-Eleven, another box of mini chocolate donuts and the chocolate milkshake.— David Goggins · [02:18:18]

What he says next is subtler than the tough talk:

I didn't teach myself victory first. I taught myself failure, I taught myself how to fail.— David Goggins · [02:18:18]

His point: a 300-pound man who can't swim and wants to be a SEAL faces years of failure first, so he has to learn to sit in failure without staying there too long. That is budgeting for failure, not just harshness. And the half of his claim that says kindness to yourself makes you stop is the half the experiments disagree with (see the fact-check).

Listen closely and "no carrot" isn't quite right either. Each night, he says, he tells the mirror "you did it again today" and gets about two minutes of reward, gone by the time he lies down; then he is up in the night checking the drugs he studied [00:40:56]. More important is what he calls his medicine cabinet. No coffee, no pre-workout; he has run for 70 hours on a cabinet of past evidence: 300 pounds, 275, 250, every step taken alone, with no one patting him on the back [01:40:45]. Huberman adds that we're raised to chase other people's praise, like a dog waiting for a biscuit; Goggins draws his reward from inside. Later Huberman compares the mockery aimed at him to cap-gun fire [01:59:02].

Near the end Huberman cites the psychiatrist Paul Conti: the real supercomputer is the unconscious, and most people won't open those cupboards. Goggins is quickest to respond: he cleans the dirtiest cupboards first, every day, and the same demons climb out of them each time [02:27:32]. So the system is really three things: a voice that attacks, a voice that talks back, and a cabinet of evidence only he can see.

2. The brain region Huberman hands him

Forty-five minutes in, Huberman offers "a little neuroscience tidbit": the anterior midcingulate cortex, aMCC. His key line:

It's not about adding more work, it's about adding more work that you don't want to do.— Andrew Huberman · [00:46:31]

He goes on: the region is smaller in obese people, grows when they diet, is larger in athletes, and keeps its size in people who live a very long time. If you love the ice bath and go from one minute to ten, it doesn't grow; enjoy it the next day and it shrinks back [00:48:11]. Then he pushes it a level up:

In many ways, scientists are starting to think of the anterior midcingulate cortex not just as one of the seats of willpower, right, but perhaps actually the seat of the will to live.— Andrew Huberman · [00:46:31]

He adds a mechanism: the region can trigger dopamine release, the dopamine system has pain inputs, and dopamine from "friction" is learned [01:03:55]. He also leaves himself an out: knowing the name of a brain area doesn't fundamentally change us [00:53:26].

His hardest evidence is his colleague Josef Parvizi's stimulation work: patients felt a storm was coming, and wanted to go through it [01:03:55]. The study is real, published in Neuron in 2013, and its abstract is plain about the sample: two epilepsy patients.

Watch how Goggins receives it. He says he knew nothing about this, but it describes his whole life; minutes later Huberman says that if they imaged Goggins's brain, this region would be large [01:05:25]. An hour on, Goggins is using the region to explain why he won't retire. The science here does one job: it supplies an anatomy diagram for a life story that was already finished.

3. Capping success

The third thread is the cost of the system, and Goggins does the accounting himself. He says he "caps success": every summer he fights fires and gives up millions of dollars; no year-round speaking circuit, no supplement line; for years he refused social media, calling it poison [00:29:49]. His reason:

Something always told me this is a very, very, very perishable skill, this, this willpower that you have.— David Goggins · [02:02:23]

Huberman relays a question from people in the SEAL community: what happens when he can't run? Goggins turns it inside out. Running is the thing he hates most; at the start line of every 250- or 300-mile race he is cursing, asking why he is there. It was never about running; after each knee surgery doctors told him he'd never run again, and he was fine with that [02:00:39]. Huberman jumps in: if you loved running, the region would regress. By now the two accounts fit perfectly, hatred as fuel and enjoyment as leak. That perfect fit is itself a warning sign; the evidence below is less tidy.

There are other line items. He says sleep and diet can give way so he can build confidence [01:24:23]. With family, he gives them everything they need first, then expects to be left alone. Sometimes, he says, that has worked, and sometimes it hasn't [01:29:11]. Huberman describes a friend over 300 pounds he has urged for thirteen years without success; Goggins's answer is that there has to be a small flame inside, and he can't light it for anyone [01:17:46].

Huberman supplies the most accurate framing in the episode. Goggins is highly unusual:

Let's just say maybe even n of one, as we say in science, sample size of one.— Andrew Huberman · [01:46:29]

That should be how the whole episode is read: one man's system, plus one brain region still under study.

Fact-check

"The aMCC only grows when you do things you don't want to do; things you enjoy don't count, and a day off shrinks it." [00:48:11]

Insufficient evidence

The review behind Huberman's account calls training-induced change in the aMCC only "one intriguing possibility." The closest intervention evidence is a six-month randomized trial: in 59 sedentary adults aged 60–79, gray matter in frontal regions including the cingulate grew with aerobic training, not with stretching. It compared kinds of exercise, not whether people wanted to do it.

"It's smaller in obese people, larger in athletes, and keeps its size in people who live a very long time." [00:46:31]

Supported, but overstated

The review's obesity evidence is mostly about activation to food cues, not size. "Long-lived" should be superagers: people aged 60–80 with the memory of young adults, who have a thicker aMCC. That is about memory, not lifespan, and it is a cross-sectional correlation.

"Being kind to myself at 300 pounds would have sent me back to 7-Eleven." [02:18:18]

Opinion, not fact

His counterfactual about himself can't be checked. The generalization can: across four experiments by Breines and Chen, people prompted to treat a failure with self-compassion spent more time studying after failing and were more motivated to fix a weakness. Being kind to yourself is not the same as letting yourself off.

Crossovers

  • Reinforcement learning · the living penalty: charge a gridworld agent a small cost for every step and it rushes to the nearest exit, even a bad one. Being "haunted" is a per-step penalty that never switches off, which explains why Goggins won't wait for the top of the hour to start. Where it differs: he sets the next exit to be the harder task rather than the nearest way out, and he keeps rewriting his own reward function.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: in the Sallatha Sutta, pain is the first arrow; resisting and ruminating on it is a second arrow we fire ourselves. Buddhism says don't fire it. Goggins keeps the second arrow in hand on purpose, as fuel. Same structure, opposite direction.
  • Theory of mind · Minsky's Society of Mind: Huberman's committee of voices resembles Minsky's picture of the mind as a negotiation among many small agents. The difference: Minsky's agents have no personality; each of Goggins's voices has a face, and the 300-pound self is one of them.
  • Psychology · learned helplessness, reversed: reviewing fifty years of work (mostly animal shock experiments), Maier and Seligman rewrote the theory in 2016: under prolonged bad events, passivity is the brain's default; what must be learned is "I have control." Goggins learning to fail first reads like that step: building a sense of control, bit by bit, out of a run of failures.
  • Distributed systems · leases: a lease expires unless renewed, so the holder must keep sending heartbeats. Goggins's "perishable skill" and Huberman's sober friend, who says the cure for addiction works only one day at a time, both describe a design with no permanent lock, only leases.

Ten takeaways

  1. David Goggins says he runs on "only a stick, no carrot," yet he also leans on a two-minute nightly self-affirmation and a cabinet of past evidence.
  2. During Hell Week, Goggins built a second inner voice whose only job is to argue with the one saying "you can't."
  3. Goggins learned how to fail before he learned how to win: hurt, but not for long, which leaves room for failure.
  4. Andrew Huberman describes the anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC) as a seat of willpower, driven by doing what you don't want to do.
  5. The strongest evidence cited for the aMCC, an electrical-stimulation study, involved only two epilepsy patients.
  6. Goggins uses the aMCC to explain his whole life, which pins an anatomy chart onto a story that was already written.
  7. Goggins deliberately caps his success, giving up income and social media, because he believes willpower expires quickly.
  8. Huberman's ice-bath example: building from one minute to ten doesn't grow the aMCC, and once you start enjoying it, the region shrinks back.
  9. Goggins says running is what he hates most; running was never the point, and the hating is the fuel.
  10. Huberman himself calls Goggins an "n of one": this is one person's system, not a recipe to copy.
