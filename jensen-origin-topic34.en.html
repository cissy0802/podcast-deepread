Jensen Huang Series · No. 2

Denny's and the Near-Death Years

He has told the same founding story for fifteen years, and it comes out a little different each time

Topic NVIDIA's founding years and the principles he draws from them · Sources 8 interview and speech transcripts (2011–2026), NVIDIA's 1998 S-1, Intel's 1993 Hot Chips talk · Main venues Stanford eCorner (2011) · Acquired (2023) · Stanford GSB (2024) · Stripe Sessions (2024) · Joe Rogan #2422 (2025) · Carnegie Mellon commencement (2026) · Hoover "Only in America" (2026)

His Claims

When Jensen Huang tells how NVIDIA began, a few set pieces always come back: the boarding school in Kentucky, Denny's, a first chip built in the wrong direction, Sega's $5 million, and the RIVA 128 that went into production before anyone had held one. He has told these stories from 2011 to 2026, and each telling ends with a principle. This piece lines up the versions of each story side by side, to see what he took from the near-death years and how the stories have been smoothed over fifteen years of retelling.

1. The start: a dishwasher and "a good past"

At nine, he and his older brother were sent by their parents from Thailand to the United States and ended up at a Baptist boarding school in Oneida, Kentucky. How he tells this depends a great deal on the room. On the Stripe stage in 2024 he said he was about to share things he had never told anyone, not even his family, and then said only that he was the youngest kid and got the job of cleaning the bathrooms. With Joe Rogan in 2025 the details multiplied: a 17-year-old roommate covered in tape from a fresh knife fight, toilets for a dorm of a hundred boys, and a week of smoking at age nine before he decided his quarter was better spent on popsicles. On the Hoover Institution's show in 2026, the same years became, in his words, a wonderful childhood; he mentioned lightly that the school had never seen a Chinese kid and there were biases, but kids are kids.

Denny's is the second set piece. In his Carnegie Mellon commencement speech he said his brother got him a job washing dishes there, and his mother woke him at 4 a.m. to deliver newspapers. He rose from dishwasher to busboy to waiter. In 1993 two engineers at Sun, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, wanted to start a company, and the three of them met at Denny's. At Stanford GSB in 2024 he turned this into a principle. He did not know how to write a business plan. His former boss, LSI Logic CEO Wilf Corrigan, heard his pitch, called it one of the worst elevator pitches he had ever heard, then picked up the phone and told Sequoia's Don Valentine to give the kid money because he was one of LSI's best employees. Huang's lesson: you can blow an interview, but you cannot run away from your past, so have a good past. He said he was probably Denny's best dishwasher, organized and planned, and as a busboy never walked in or out empty-handed.

He knows this is a well-worn story. In 2026 the host, Condoleezza Rice, strung the path together from Kentucky to Oregon State to Stanford, and he finished it for her, casting himself as the Denny's busboy who met two geniuses and adding that this is how the story goes [00:35:35]. When she then asked whether this could only happen in America, he gave a cooler account:

I think that it's a chain of extremely low probability events.— Jensen Huang · Hoover "Only in America", 2026 [00:36:00]

In the same stretch he put immigrants and founders side by side. Both come by choice, both are desperate to succeed, both must work every day or perish. His constant desperation to do better at NVIDIA, he said, is the same feeling his parents had about securing a living for the family.

2. The first near-death: the wrong bet, then the ask to Sega

NVIDIA's first product, the NV1, shipped in 1995, while the company was also developing the NV2 for Sega's next console. Both failed, and why they failed depends on where he is telling it. To Rogan, the fault was his own technology. Others used triangles; NVIDIA used curved surfaces. Others did inverse texture mapping; NVIDIA did forward. Others had a Z-buffer to sort automatically; NVIDIA left sorting to the application:

All three choices were wrong. Okay. So this is how incredibly smart we were.— Jensen Huang · Joe Rogan #2422, 2025 [01:24:55]

On Acquired and at Stanford GSB, the weight shifts to Microsoft: by the time NVIDIA realized it was on the wrong road, DirectX was out and fundamentally incompatible with its architecture. The 1998 prospectus offers a third account. The NV1 was developed "in the absence of industry standards" with the goal of making NVIDIA's own technology the 3D standard; once the industry adopted Direct3D and OpenGL, NV1 revenue collapsed. The three accounts do not contradict each other. They put the blame in different places: the technology, a rival, a standards war.

Then came the ask. In the Rogan version, he flew to Japan and told Sega three things. The technology we promised does not work. You should not let us finish the contract; find another partner. But I still need the money, so please convert the last $5 million into an investment, or NVIDIA vaporizes overnight. Sega thought about it for a couple of days and said yes. His explanation of why was short:

Jensen was a young man he liked. That's it.— Jensen Huang · Joe Rogan #2422, 2025 [01:31:57]

At Carnegie Mellon in 2026, the same story was compressed into a commencement passage:

Without the money, Nvidia would vaporize. It was embarrassing, humiliating, and one of the hardest things I have ever done.— Jensen Huang · Carnegie Mellon commencement, 2026 [00:05:29]

He draws two principles from it. Being CEO is not about power but about the responsibility of keeping the company alive. And honesty and humility can be met with generosity, even in business. There is also a more methodical lesson, clearest in the Rogan telling: nobody knew the right strategy, but everyone knew which technology was wrong, so stop doing the wrong thing first and give yourself the chance to find the right one.

3. The second near-death: three textbooks, one emulator, one tape-out

After NV1 was shelved, the company laid off most of its people, and those who stayed had never built a graphics chip the "right" way. That is where the textbook story comes in. At Stanford GSB in 2024 he said he took his daughter Madison to Fry's Electronics one weekend, found the OpenGL manual on the shelf at $68 a copy, and with a couple hundred dollars bought three and handed them out. To Rogan in 2025, the books were textbooks by Silicon Graphics at $60 apiece. The details drift; the conclusion holds, and it became his line for any unfamiliar field:

It's probably just a textbook away, you know, you're probably one arXiv paper away from figuring this out.— Jensen Huang · Stanford GSB, 2024 [00:12:04]

The RIVA 128 is the most dramatic episode. There was money for one tape-out. The usual loop of tape out, test, fix, tape out again would outlast the company. His answer was a hardware emulator that pretended to be the unbuilt chip, so the team could run every driver and game on it first, sitting in the lab waiting for Windows to paint. In 2023 he told Acquired that when his leaders asked how he knew the chip would be perfect, he answered:

I know it's going to be perfect because if it's not we'll be out of business, and so let's make it perfect.— Jensen Huang · Acquired, 2023 [00:08:07]

When the hosts summed this up as "push all your chips in when you believe," he pushed back at once. Before betting everything, they had emulated the whole chip and run every game and driver on it:

When you bet the farm you're saying I'm going to take everything in the future, all the risky things, and going to pull it in advance.— Jensen Huang · Acquired, 2023 [00:10:55]

This is the most substantive principle in the whole founding story. In his telling, the courage of the bet comes from risk already absorbed, not from going all in blind. He still calls it prefetching: whatever part of the future can be simulated now, simulate it now.

Compare the 2011 version. Teaching a Stanford entrepreneurship class that year about how the company survived its early years, he mentioned no emulator and no single tape-out. Dell, HP, IBM and Gateway had all told him his spec was too expensive, and he decided to ignore customers for the first five years:

I took the last few million dollars of the company's money and built a chip that is way way way too big.— Jensen Huang · Stanford eCorner, 2011 [00:20:22]

The same years taught "3D graphics is insatiable, Moore's law is your friend, ignore the customer" in 2011, "pull the risk forward" in 2023, and by 2025 had gained Morris Chang of TSMC agreeing to go straight to production without seeing a sample. All three lessons may be true. But each time he picks one, and it is the one he most wants to make that day.

How the Claims Fit Together

Put together, these stories form a chain. His upbringing gave him the belief that your past follows you, so be a good dishwasher. The first near-death taught him to admit the mistake before asking for help. The second taught him to burn down the risk before placing the bet. Running through all of it is "thirty days from going out of business." He told Rogan he has used the phrase for 33 years because he still wakes up feeling that way [01:48:37]. In 2011 he put it as a definition: a startup is an enterprise that is nearly out of business all the time. That unease connects to the rest of the series. Sticking with CUDA through a decade with no buyers is the same survive-first patience, covered in "The CUDA Bet"; treating pain as the source of character is taken apart in "Pain, Character and What He Tells the Young".

The weakest link is memory itself. Take the number of competitors: in 2011, fifty to seventy by 1995 and about two hundred in total; in 2023, thirty; in 2024, eighty-nine; in 2025, a hundred. The books also differ from the story. The prospectus shows NVIDIA launched the RIVA 128 in August 1997 and, that August and September, raised about $7.6 million in preferred stock; revenue jumped from $3.9 million in 1996 to $29.1 million in 1997, with the first profitable quarter in the fourth. "Thirty days" describes a real situation, but what saved the company was not only a chip; it was also a timely financing round. The more he tells it, the more he tells the moral, and the details are left to the day of the telling.

Boundaries

"I asked Sega to let us out of the contract and convert the last $5 million into an investment, and they agreed." — to Joe Rogan, 2025 [01:29:08]

Supported, but stated too broadly

The money is real; the sequence does not match. The 1998 S-1 says that in May 1995 NVIDIA signed a five-year alliance with a "third party" to develop the NV2 (Sega Enterprises signs the investor rights agreement filed with it). That party bought $5 million of Series C preferred in July 1995, development payments kept being booked through 1995 and 1996, and the project ended in 1996 when the partner revised its plans. The investment came at the start of the deal, not as a conversion at the exit. He has also named two decision-makers: Shoichiro Irimajiri to Rogan, Hayao Nakayama at Carnegie Mellon. At the time Nakayama was Sega's president and Irimajiri a vice president.

"We designed chips that way out of desperation, and now everybody does it that way." — to Acquired, 2023 [00:55:37]

Supported, but stated too broadly

Others were emulating before tape-out first. At Hot Chips in 1993, Intel reported that it ran the Pentium on 14 Quickturn emulators, booting every major operating system and dozens of applications before silicon and catching several errata, four years before the RIVA 128. What was rare about NVIDIA was a dying startup staking everything on emulation and going to volume production from its first tape-out.

Crossing Over · Interdisciplinary Links

  • Economics · increasing returns and lock-in: Brian Arthur's 1989 paper showed that for technologies that get better the more people use them, small early leads are amplified into lock-in, and the winner need not be the best design. The NV1 lost exactly this kind of standards war: however clever, a proprietary approach could not beat the Direct3D every developer was using. The difference is that NVIDIA then turned the lesson around; on Acquired he called architectural compatibility across every chip the company's one non-negotiable rule for thirty years.
  • Psychology · Bartlett's reconstructive memory: In Remembering (1932), Frederic Bartlett had English participants retell a Native American folk tale again and again; with each retelling the unfamiliar details fell away and the plot bent toward the teller's own schemas. Huang's fifteen years of founding stories behave the same way: the competitor count wanders, Sega's chief changes, and the moral gets sharper. The difference is that his audience changes each time, and the story bends toward that day's listeners.
  • Evolution · the bottleneck effect: When a population crashes to a few survivors, whatever traits they happen to carry are amplified in their descendants. NVIDIA went through a bottleneck around 1996; the people who stayed after the layoffs, and their thirty-days anxiety, became the baseline of a company now tens of thousands strong. The difference is that nature's bottleneck selects by luck, while he tells his as a culture the company chose to keep.
  • Distributed systems · formal verification: In a 2014 report, AWS engineers described using TLA+ to exhaustively check designs for concurrency errors before launch, catching design flaws that testing rarely reaches. It is the same instinct as pulling future risk forward. The difference is that TLA+ checks an abstract specification, while the RIVA 128 emulator ran the actual chip, at the cost of waiting for each frame to paint.

Ten Takeaways

  1. Jensen Huang was sent at nine to a boarding school in Oneida, Kentucky, and his 2024, 2025 and 2026 accounts of it differ widely.
  2. At Stanford GSB, Jensen Huang said one call from LSI Logic's CEO got Sequoia to fund him, so you can blow an interview but should have a good past.
  3. In 2026 at the Hoover Institution, Jensen Huang called his path a chain of extremely low probability events and equated the immigrant and entrepreneurial spirit.
  4. Jensen Huang told Rogan the NV1 failed because all three technical choices were wrong, while elsewhere he blamed Microsoft's DirectX.
  5. NVIDIA's 1998 prospectus blames the NV1's failure on trying to set its own 3D standard when the industry chose Direct3D and OpenGL.
  6. Jensen Huang says he asked Sega to release NVIDIA from its contract and still pay, and concluded that a CEO's job is keeping the company alive.
  7. When Jensen Huang shelved the NV1, his principle was to stop the path known to be wrong even before knowing the right strategy.
  8. Jensen Huang's line for any unfamiliar field is "how hard can it be": usually just a textbook or an arXiv paper away.
  9. Jensen Huang says betting everything on the RIVA 128 was only possible because emulation had already pulled the future risk forward.
  10. Bartlett's work on reconstructive memory helps explain why the details of Jensen Huang's founding stories drift while the moral sharpens.
