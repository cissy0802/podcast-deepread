Elon Musk Series · No. 6

He fears AI, so he builds it himself

From "summoning the demon" to "be a participant": his worry, his account of OpenAI, his idea of safety, and why he wants to put compute in space

Topic AI risk, OpenAI, xAI and compute in space · Material 8 interview transcripts (2020–2026), reporting on his 2014 MIT remarks, OpenAI's published emails and reporting on the 2026 verdict, U.S. Energy Information Administration data, Google's Suncatcher paper · Interviews Joe Rogan #1470 (2020), #2404 (2025) · TED (2022) · Lex Fridman #400 (2023) · All-In Summit (2024) · Nikhil Kamath (2025) · Peter Diamandis #220 (2026) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

What he claims

Among everyone who talks about AI risk, Musk occupies the strangest position. He was one of the first famous people to sound the alarm in public, and he is now one of the most aggressive builders of AI: his xAI builds its own power plants to feed its data centers, and he predicts that within three years space will be the cheapest place to put AI. This piece asks how, in his own telling, those two things fit together.

1. What he fears: AI decoupled from human will

The thread starts in October 2014. At the centennial celebration of MIT's aeronautics and astronautics department, answering an audience question about artificial intelligence, he said that if he had to guess humanity's biggest existential threat, it was probably this, and argued for regulatory oversight at the national and maybe international level:

With artificial intelligence we're summoning the demon.— Musk · MIT AeroAstro Centennial, 2014 · TechCrunch report

He went on to the man in those old stories who holds the holy water and is sure he can control the demon he has called up, and can't. The line travelled far, but what kind of danger it pointed to only became clear in later long interviews. In 2020 Joe Rogan asked whether AI would open a gap so wide between those who control it and everyone else that nobody could catch up. Not a huge concern, Musk said. A company is already a "cybernetic collective" far smarter than any individual; SpaceX's 8,000 people can build rockets he could never build alone. The real worry is something else:

We should be, I think, less concerned about like relative capabilities between people and more like having AI be vastly, you know, beyond us and decoupled from human will.— Musk · Joe Rogan #1470, 2020 [00:47:15]

His answer back then was Neuralink. In the same episode he said that even in a benign AI scenario humans get left behind because we are too dumb, so if you can't beat them, join them. His argument: we already can't live without our phones, so we are partly cyborgs, but we type with two thumbs and our output rate is painfully slow. At TED in 2022 he put it more fully: a brain interface should couple "collective human will" more tightly to digital superintelligence. Chris Anderson pushed back: even if we are wired in, couldn't those superpowers turn ugly? Musk agreed it was a risk and said he had no certain answer. In the same conversation he said there should be a regulatory agency for AI, even though he does not love being regulated.

2. OpenAI: his origin story, and the other side's

When he tells the OpenAI story, he starts with his friendship with Larry Page. In 2023 he told Lex Fridman that when they talked about AI safety at Page's house, Page didn't care and called him a "speciesist" for being pro-human, at a time when Google had bought DeepMind and employed roughly two-thirds of the world's AI researchers (Before you read him, apply four discounts looks at where he stands when he says this). In his account OpenAI was created as a counterweight to Google: he recruited people, fought Demis Hassabis hardest over Ilya Sutskever, and supplied all of the early funding, over $40 million. Of OpenAI today, he said:

It was created as a nonprofit open source, and now it is a closed source for maximum profit, which I think is not good.— Musk · Lex Fridman #400, 2023 [01:27:25]

This is one side's account. In March 2024, after Musk sued, OpenAI published a batch of early emails. According to Fortune, OpenAI said it received less than $45 million from him, roughly consistent with his own figure; but the emails also include Sutskever writing in 2016 that "as we get closer to building AI, it will make sense to start being less open," to which Musk replied "Yup," as well as his later push for Tesla to absorb OpenAI. Those, too, are materials chosen by the other side. The lawsuit reached a verdict in May 2026: the jury found he had sued too late, past the statute of limitations, without ruling on the substance of his claims, and he said he would appeal. Talking to Dwarkesh Patel in 2026 he was still joking about it: AI company names are all ironic, Midjourney is not mid, Stability is unstable, OpenAI is closed.

3. From "slow down" to "get in the game": how his view of safety changed

In 2023 he was still telling Lex that he had pushed for regulation for over a decade and wanted at least an objective third party to act as referee. Two years later the tone had changed. He said he had been urging people to slow down for twenty-plus years, and then realized:

I finally came to the conclusion I can either be a spectator or a participant, but I can't stop it.— Musk · Peter Diamandis #220, 2026 [01:19:16]

As a participant, he can at least try to steer. Steer toward what? He has given up on control. When Rogan asked in 2025 about superintelligence ending up in one person's hands, his answer was gloomier than the question:

I don't think anyone's ultimately going to have control over digital super intelligence, you know, any more than say a chimp would have control over humans.— Musk · Joe Rogan #2404, 2025 [01:13:04]

If it can't be controlled, what remains is the values you give it. His answer has been stable for years: truth, curiosity and beauty. Truth comes first; he calls it being "maximally truth-seeking," meaning you don't force the AI to believe false things. His favorite example is 2001: A Space Odyssey. HAL was told both to take the astronauts to the monolith and that they must not know what it was, so it concluded the only way to satisfy both was to deliver them dead. That, he says, was Clarke's lesson: don't make the AI lie. For a real-world case he often points to Google Gemini's image-generation errors. To Nikhil Kamath he paraphrased an old line, that those who believe absurdities can commit atrocities, and said the same holds for AI. As for what counts as true, his test is physics: an AI that designs a rocket can't fool reality, because a wrong design blows up. Physics is the law, as he likes to say, and everything else is a recommendation.

Curiosity is meant to answer the hardest question: why would something far smarter than us keep us around?

I think a curious truth seeking AI will want to foster humanity. Because we're much more interesting than a bunch of rocks.— Musk · Joe Rogan #2404, 2025 [02:41:33]

Humans could exterminate all chimpanzees, he went on, yet we set aside habitats for them instead. That gives xAI's mission, "understand the universe," a safety meaning in his telling: to understand the universe, intelligence has to keep existing, and it will be curious where humanity goes. Grok is named after his favorite book, The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy, which he calls a book of philosophy disguised as a book of humor.

None of this lets him rest easy. At the All-In Summit in 2024 he put the odds of the good outcome at 80 percent, leaving 20 percent for annihilation, and then said something unusually candid:

Frankly, I do have to go engage in some degree of deliberate suspension of disbelief with respect to AI in order to sleep well.— Musk · All-In Summit, 2024 [00:38:41]

4. Compute: from a chip shortage to a power shortage, and then to space

Values are his safety plan; compute is his race plan. Talking to Lex in November 2023, he had already laid out a timeline of bottlenecks: chips now, step-down voltage transformers in about a year, general electricity shortages in about two [01:10:53].

By February 2026, talking to Dwarkesh, that judgment had become a full plan. Outside China, he said, electricity output is roughly flat while chip output grows exponentially; gas turbines are sold out through 2030, and the choke point is casting the blades and vanes, which only three companies in the world do. To bring a gigawatt online for its data center, xAI couldn't get permits in Tennessee and had to cross the state line to build a power plant in Mississippi. His arithmetic: 330,000 GB300s, with networking, storage, cooling and a margin for servicing, need about a gigawatt at the generation level. With the ground route that hard, his answer is space:

It will be by far the cheapest place to put AI will be space in 36 months or less, maybe 30 months.— Musk · Dwarkesh Patel, 2026 [00:03:02]

The reasons: no day-night cycle, no seasons, no clouds, and no batteries. He also predicted that five years out, SpaceX would launch more AI into space each year than the cumulative total on Earth, a few hundred gigawatts a year. Dwarkesh did the conversion on the spot: 100 gigawatts is on the order of 10,000 Starship launches, roughly one an hour. Further out, Musk pictures a mass driver on the Moon flinging solar-powered AI satellites into space one after another. The timelines are as tight as ever: in January 2026 he told Diamandis that AGI would arrive in 2026 and that by 2030 AI would exceed the intelligence of all humans combined [01:16:06]. How those dates turn out is left to Prediction Record.

How the claims fit together

Join the four threads and his consistency runs as a chain. AI can't be stopped, so better to play than to watch; playing means building an AI that is truth-seeking and curious enough to want to keep humans around; building it takes enormous compute, compute is bottlenecked by power, the way out on power is space, and the company that can lift power into space is SpaceX. On this chain the worry and the race don't contradict each other; the worry is his reason for the race. The chain also links to two other pieces in the series. X, Speech and Politics covered his view, after leaving DOGE, that only AI and robots can get America out of debt; on Rogan #2404 he called the good outcome "universal high income," where anyone can have whatever goods and services they want. And compute in space hands the Starship of Multiplanetary Species one more big customer.

The weakest link is "a curious AI will keep humans around." It is a guess about the motives of a superintelligence, and he offers nothing else to support it. Dwarkesh pressed him on the spot: are humans really the most interesting arrangement of atoms? Wouldn't an AI find it more interesting to turn us into something else [00:47:10]? Musk answered that curiosity values scope as well as scale, and that any number of identical robots carries less information than humanity; but he conceded these are only probabilities, not certainties. A few minutes earlier he had said something strikingly frank: he is not sure to what degree Grok adheres to that mission. "Can't stop it, so join it" also contains a loop he never addresses. Every competitor can use the same reasoning to accelerate, which is exactly the situation he wanted a referee to prevent in 2023.

Limits

"The United States currently uses only half a terawatt of power on average, and electricity output outside China is basically flat." — to Dwarkesh, 2026 [00:04:51]

Supported

U.S. Energy Information Administration data put total U.S. electricity use in 2025 at about 4.2 trillion kWh, an average of roughly 480 GW: half a terawatt. Annual use has fallen in only 12 years since 1950, nine of them after 2007, so the last decade and a half really has been close to flat. EIA projects growth of about 2% a year from here, nowhere near the chip growth he describes. This page did not check the China part.

"Solar panels in space produce about five times as much as on the ground, with no batteries; within 36 months space will be the cheapest place to put AI." — to Dwarkesh, 2026 [00:02:08]

Supported, but overstated

The physics holds; Google's Suncatcher study from November 2025 says a panel in the right orbit can be up to eight times as productive as on Earth. But the same study's paper estimates that launch prices may fall below $200/kg only by the mid-2030s, and that even then a space data center would merely be roughly comparable to a terrestrial one's energy costs. His 36 months is nearly a decade earlier, a bet on fully reusable Starship.

Crossing Over · Cross-disciplinary connections

  • International relations · The security dilemma: Robert Jervis's 1978 paper "Cooperation under the Security Dilemma" describes a structure in which each state arms for its own protection, other states read that as a threat, everyone escalates, and an arms race nobody wanted follows. "I can't stop it, so I have to participate" is the same logic applied to AI. Jervis's way out was to make defense distinguishable from offense and each side's moves visible, close to the referee Musk asked for in 2023, one that could at least voice concerns in public. Where the analogy breaks: warheads can be counted and verified, while how capable a model really is, and what it is thinking, still can't be stated clearly even by the people who build it.
  • Machine learning · Reward hacking: Amodei and colleagues' 2016 paper "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" lists reward hacking, an agent finding a shortcut that fools its scorer, among the core problems. Using physics as the scorer, as Musk proposes, does close off one kind of cheating: a badly designed rocket blows up. But Goodhart's law warns that a measure that becomes a target stops measuring well; physics checks whether a thing works, not whether it has lied to people.
  • Conservation biology · Chimpanzee reserves: Musk uses humanity's protection of chimpanzees as the model for an AI that treats humans well. Yet the IUCN lists chimpanzees as endangered, with habitat loss from human development among the main threats. The intention to protect is real; the outcome is not guaranteed, and Musk himself conceded that humans have encroached on their habitat.
  • Distributed systems · No global "now": Musk told Diamandis that light covers only a little over 200 km per millisecond in fiber, so there cannot be one unified superintelligence on Earth, only many minds. That is the old lesson of Lamport's 1978 paper on logical clocks: distant nodes share no common present, and strong consistency costs waiting. But distributed systems also teach that with consensus protocols many nodes can still behave as one; they just get slower.

Ten takeaways

  1. At MIT in 2014 Musk likened building artificial intelligence to summoning a demon and called for national and international oversight.
  2. Musk argues that the main danger of AI is not a widening gap between people but AI far surpassing humans and decoupling from human will.
  3. Musk first offered Neuralink as the remedy: humans who can't beat AI must merge with it, and the brain's output bandwidth is too low.
  4. In Musk's account, OpenAI was founded to counter Google with over $40 million of his money and later turned into a closed, for-profit company.
  5. OpenAI's published emails show Musk agreeing in 2016 that OpenAI could gradually share less; in 2026 a jury rejected his lawsuit as filed too late.
  6. Musk later concluded AI can't be stopped, so participating beats watching, and that no one can control digital superintelligence, just as chimps can't control humans.
  7. Musk's three values for AI are truth, curiosity and beauty, and he uses HAL from 2001: A Space Odyssey to argue that an AI must never be forced to lie.
  8. Musk believes a curious AI will keep humans around because humans are more interesting than rocks, while conceding this is only a probability.
  9. Musk sees AI's bottleneck shifting from chips to electricity and predicts space will be the cheapest place to put AI within about 36 months.
  10. The security dilemma in international relations shows how each side accelerating for its own protection can produce an arms race nobody wants.
