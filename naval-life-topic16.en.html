Naval Topic · No. 8

The long game: moving compound interest from money to body and relationships

Health, relationships and life choices: how Naval arranges the part of life that money does not cover

Topic The long game: health, relationships and life choices · Material 12 nav.al texts (2005–2025), 5 interview transcripts (2017–2026) · Interviews The Knowledge Project (2017) · Joe Rogan Experience #1309 (2019) · BeerBiceps (2024) · Chris Williamson (2025) · Eric Jorgenson Megasode (2026)

His claims

One line from Naval’s wealth tweetstorm ended up travelling further than the wealth advice itself: work in an industry where you can play long-term games with long-term people. When he and his partner Nivi recorded the tweets one by one in 2019, he did not leave that line in business. He stretched it into a rule for a whole life. This page asks how much of that rule survives once it leaves money behind and lands on the body, on relationships, and on a few large life choices.

1. Long games: compounding, trust, and filtering first

He started with compound interest. Grow 20% a year for 30 years and you do not get thirty lots of 20% added up; you get something far larger. Then he pushed the idea off money:

Essentially, all the benefits in life come from compound interests. Whether it’s in relationships, or making money, or in learning.— Naval · Play Long-term Games With Long-term People, 2019

Two reasons hold this up. One is compounding itself; the other is trust. He borrowed the iterated prisoner’s dilemma: tit-for-tat, doing to you what you did to me last time with a little forgiveness for mistakes, only works if we expect to play again. People in Silicon Valley know each other and know the other person will be at the next game, so they behave. His life examples were getting married, having children and raising them, which he also called compounding; and with someone you have worked with for twenty years you barely need to read the contracts, a handshake will do.

He also said when the rule breaks. In Silicon Valley one move can make you rich enough that you stop caring, so some people betray. Switching industries resets you to zero, because your network and reputation do not travel. Buffett could play a long game because the US market, the table itself, was not seized or destroyed for decades. Put differently, a long game needs players who stay, rules that hold and a table that does not collapse; lose any one and the compounding stops.

The same year he folded ethics into the frame, calling it being “long-term greedy.” Honesty leaves you with one thread running in your head and pushes people who want pretty lies out of your circle; fair deals cost you early but turn you into the hub everyone routes through. He did not make the arithmetic look too good:

In the long run, being ethical pays off—but it’s the very long run. In the short run, being unethical pays off, which is why so many people go for it.— Naval · Being Ethical Is Long-Term Greedy, 2019

A long game, in his hands, does not mean patience with everyone. On BeerBiceps in 2024 he said he is lazy and hates conflict, and his rule is close to cold:

I don't fight with people. If somebody wants to fight with me, we get one fight and then it's over.— Naval · BeerBiceps, 2024 [00:56:18]

His reason was that there are a lot of people out there and life is long. So cut losses fast, show your worst side up front, and do not spend the first year performing, or sunk cost will trap you both. This does not contradict compounding; it is the other half of it. Filter hard, and you can put a limited number of decades on a small number of people.

2. The body: ranked first, discussed least

The body was the first thing Naval wrote into a formula. In Life Formulas I (2008), happiness equals health plus wealth plus good relationships, and health equals exercise plus diet plus sleep, broken down further into high-intensity resistance training, intermittent fasting and plants. By The Knowledge Project in 2017 he had a daily morning workout, had mostly cut out drinking, had dropped caffeine, and explained where health sat:

My number one priority in life, above my happiness, above my family, above my work, is my own health.— Naval · The Knowledge Project, 2017 [00:27:28]

It sounds hard, but the reasoning was practical. Everyone says “I don’t have time,” which is another way of saying “it isn’t my top priority.” So he made it the top priority, and since then the day does not start until the workout is done. He drew concentric circles: physical health in the middle, then mental, then spiritual, then his family’s well-being. The interesting part is how this reached into relationships. The same routine every morning became a checkpoint; drink too much the night before and it is suddenly harder [00:24:42]. Asking why he drank, he found he was doing it to last longer in social settings he did not enjoy. So he kept to people he could be around without drinking, and his circle of friends narrowed sharply. In 2020 he turned this into a short piece, Breaking Addiction Is Socially Unacceptable: some relationships and activities are held together by alcohol, and quitting means replacing friends.

In the 2019 long-game episode he counted fitness as compounding too: the fitter you are, the easier it is to stay fit, and the more your body deteriorates, the more it takes “heroic acts” to claw back to baseline. That can be checked against data, which the Limits section does. By the 2020 happiness series, though, he had become careful about the body:

Although physical health is the most important, I speak the least about it because it’s the area where I have the least specific knowledge.— Naval · Happiness Without Material Comfort Is Playing on Hard Mode, 2020

“Specific knowledge” is a term from his wealth framework: know-how that cannot be learned from a textbook and only grows out of your own experience. He said his shape and diet are just “OK,” and that teaching it would feel fraudulent. The admission is worth keeping, because the body advice readers are most likely to copy is the part he says he is least qualified to give. His later advice also got looser. In 2017 the best workout was the one you are excited enough to do every day; in 2024, if you like running, run, if you like lifting, lift, find the sport that does not feel like work. He also said he would rather live like an athlete or a lion, resting most of the time and occasionally pouring everything into something, than live a mechanical life of blueberries and push-ups on schedule [01:04:34]. The idea is already in his 2005 post The 80-hour Myth: nobody really works eighty productive hours a week, and the best work comes in bursts.

3. Life choices: who, what, where, and what retirement means

On Chris Williamson’s show in 2025 he reduced the big early-life decisions to three: who you are with, what you do, and where you live. Many people, he said, drift into marriage; they have been together a while, it feels fine, so they marry. Cities get picked just as casually. His method is to keep trying, but within a limit:

You iterate on a closed time frame so you don't run out the clock.— Naval · Chris Williamson, 2025 [01:40:59]

He thinks where you live is the most underrated of the three, since a city sets your friend circle, your dating pool, your job options, your air and water, and your distance from family. On who you are with, he said that once you have a child with someone, the most important thing in the world to you is half that person, whether you like them or not [01:42:25]. So choose on values, such as whether an ailing parent moves in or goes to a care home, rather than on a checklist. Seen through the long-game frame, this is a repeated game you cannot leave, so it pays to think hard at the start.

On children his views shifted a lot. In 2007, in The Aging Entrepreneur, he wrote that entrepreneurship favours the young and single, and that family demands squelch the creative drive more than anything else. A decade later on The Knowledge Project he said that having a child moves the most important thing in the universe out of your own body and into the child’s, and that even his idea of freedom changed, from doing whatever he wanted to freedom from anger and reaction [00:44:40]. By 2024 he said he does not like to discuss his personal life and is not the person for marital advice, yet stated plainly that everyone should have kids, and that if you are older and have no next generation to look forward to, you had better find God fast [00:52:14]. It is one of the places where he speaks most absolutely and gives the fewest reasons.

Last, retirement. He has always rejected the sixty-five-and-a-pension version, and in 2019 he gave Joe Rogan his own definition:

Retirement is when you stop sacrificing today for some imaginary tomorrow. When today is complete in and of itself, you're retired.— Naval · Joe Rogan Experience #1309, 2019 [01:49:16]

He listed three routes: save enough that passive income covers your burn rate, drive your burn rate toward zero like a monk, or do something you love so much it is not about the money. By that definition retirement has nothing to do with stopping work. In a 2025 tweet he said that when you truly work for yourself you have no hobbies, no weekends and no vacations, but no work either (When You Truly Work for Yourself). In 2026, asked by Eric Jorgenson whether he was semi-retired, he called himself “minimally retired,” running a new company and probably putting in as much work as he ever has [00:45:20].

How the claims fit together

The chain closes on a tweet. When the 2019 podcast reached the last tweet of the wealth storm, he said that once you have made money you find you are still the same person, and then cited the tweet he calls his favourite:

A fit body, a calm mind, a house full of love. These things cannot be bought—they must be earned.— Naval · A Calm Mind, a Fit Body, a House Full of Love, 2019

His argument is simple: even Jeff Bezos still has to work out and still has to work on his marriage. So the system becomes one line. Money buys freedom, which is the subject of The Mechanics of Wealth. The time that freedom frees up goes into three things that can only be earned slowly, and the way to earn them is the same compounding that builds wealth, through long games with long-term people. The body is the sturdiest link, because it depends only on what you do each day. The calm mind belongs to Happiness Is a Skill You Can Train and Self, Meditation and Eastern Philosophy. Rules for trading off cities and partners belong to Judgment and Decision-Making.

The weakest link is the house full of love. In the same episode he said that if you have a calm mind and have already made money, good relationships should follow, there is “no reason why you shouldn’t,” because many divorces happen over money and peaceful people pick fewer fights. That treats a two-player game as a one-player game. His own long-game theory says compounding needs the other player to stay at the table, and he himself says the secret to a happy relationship is two happy people. A body and a mind can be earned alone; a household has to be earned by two. And his one-fight rule, which works among friends and business partners you can walk away from, may not carry over to a household with children that neither person can leave.

Limits

“Kids make your life better in every possible way.” Asked whether parents regret having children, he guessed it would be 99 to 1 against. [02:34:54]

Supported, but overstated

The direction is right: most parents do not regret it. But Piotrowski’s two national-sample surveys in Poland (about 1,200 parents each, 2021) asked whether parents would choose parenthood again; 13.6% and 10.7% said they would choose a life without children. Earlier US and German surveys cited in the paper put it at 7–8%, and single and financially strained parents were higher. That is roughly 6 to 1 up to 13 to 1, nowhere near 99 to 1.

“The fitter you are, the easier it is to stay fit”; the more the body deteriorates, the more it takes “heroic acts” to get back to baseline. Fitness compounds.

Supported, but overstated

Seaborne and colleagues (2018) had 8 untrained young men train for 7 weeks, stop for 7, and train for 7 more. After the break, leg lean mass returned to baseline, yet the second round of growth was clearly larger than the first, and epigenetic marks in the muscle kept a “memory.” So the body does remember training, but the gains were gone in 7 weeks; unlike money, the principal does not sit there earning interest. The sample was tiny and only young men.

Crossovers

  • Mathematics · optimal stopping: the secretary problem says that when candidates arrive one at a time and you cannot go back, you should look without choosing for about the first 37%, then take the first one better than everyone before. Naval’s “iterate on a closed time frame” has the same structure: exploration has a cost, so cap it. The difference is that the secretary problem ends at the choice, while his long game only starts there, and both you and your partner keep changing.
  • Economics · Grossman’s health capital: in 1972 Michael Grossman modelled health as a capital stock that depreciates, which people invest in with time and money, and whose depreciation rate rises with age. Like “fitness compounds,” it treats the body as principal; unlike it, Grossman’s principal wears down on its own, so holding the same level gets more expensive over time.
  • Sociology · Granovetter’s weak ties: in 1973 Mark Granovetter found that job information often reaches people through acquaintances rather than close friends. Naval bets on a few deep ties; this work suggests new opportunities often come from the wide band of shallow ties he filters out.
  • Evolutionary biology · disposable soma theory: Thomas Kirkwood proposed in 1977 that evolution splits limited energy between maintaining the body and reproducing, and tends to treat the body as a disposable carrier. Naval ranks his own health above his family, the reverse of evolution’s default ordering.

Ten takeaways

  1. Naval holds that all the returns in life, in relationships, wealth or learning, come from compound interest, so play long-term games with long-term people.
  2. Naval uses the iterated prisoner’s dilemma to explain trust: playing fair only pays when both sides expect to deal with each other again.
  3. Naval admits long games break down: someone who can get rich in one move may betray, and switching industries resets you to zero.
  4. Naval calls ethics being “long-term greedy,” with a payoff that takes a very long time, while short-term cheating pays.
  5. Naval filters hard before betting long on people: one fight and it is over, cut losses early, show your worst side up front.
  6. Naval said in 2017 that his own health was his number one priority, above happiness, family and work, and he trains every morning first.
  7. Naval used his daily morning workout as a checkpoint, realised he drank to endure social settings he disliked, and his friend circle narrowed.
  8. Naval admitted in 2020 that physical health is where he has the least specific knowledge, which is why he talks about it least.
  9. Naval reduces the big early-life decisions to who you are with, what you do and where you live, and says to iterate within a time limit.
  10. Naval defines retirement as no longer sacrificing today for an imaginary tomorrow, which has nothing to do with stopping work.
