Work, Risk and Pain: How He Tells His Own Story

The 120-hour week, the factory floor, "my mind is a storm", and the reasons he gives for all of it

Topic Work, risk, pain and the meaning of life · Material 9 interview transcripts (2017–2026) · Interviews TED (2017, 2022) · Joe Rogan #1470 (2020) · Lex Fridman #252 (2021), #400 (2023) · Ron Baron Investment Conference (2022) · Nikhil Kamath (2025) · Peter Diamandis (2026) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

His Claims

There is no shortage of material on Musk's private life, most of it hearsay and gossip. This page leaves that alone and looks only at how he describes himself in long interviews: why he works so much, how he thinks about risk and pain, what his childhood gave him, and what he says it is all for. Over nine years he has told the same story on different shows. The details shift; the skeleton holds. The story is worth reading on its own terms, because it is both a self-explanation and a recruiting pitch.

1. Time is the scarcest thing: from 80 hours to 120

In November 2022, Ron Baron asked him on stage at his investment conference: you are 51, you work sixteen-hour days, why do you do this? Musk had just taken over Twitter, and half its staff had been let go that day. His answer came with a number:

My workload went up from about, I don't know, 70 to 80 hours a week to probably 120.— Musk · Ron Baron Investment Conference, 2022 [00:42:50]

He described the routine as sleep, wake, work, sleep, wake, work, seven days a week. In the same conversation he recalled the hardest years of the Model 3 ramp, when the Fremont and Nevada factories were his primary residence and for a stretch he slept on the floor under his desk on the production floor [00:14:53]. He chose the floor over the couch in a conference room, he said, so that the whole team could see him at shift change. He added that he is not a masochist and would much rather be drinking on a tropical island, but a team that thinks its leader is elsewhere will not give its all. That is a point about leadership; the factories themselves belong to the page "The Factory Is the Product".

Why live like this? At TED in April 2022 he gave an accountant's answer. Chris Anderson asked how he copes psychologically with his net worth swinging by billions a day. Musk said he hardly thinks about that. What makes sleeping difficult is something else: Tesla was taking in roughly two billion dollars a week, so an hour of brainstorming that moved that by five percent was an enormously valuable hour, and soon every high-quality minute of his thinking would be worth about a million dollars of impact.

I really try to work as much as possible, you know, to the edge of sanity, basically.— Musk · TED, 2022 [00:58:43]

The same arithmetic explains his attitude to possessions. In 2020 he told Joe Rogan he was selling almost all his houses [00:05:17], for two reasons: possessions weigh you down, and they are an "attack vector" for critics. He admitted he was slightly sad about it. Asked why he did not build himself a dream house, he said the time spent obsessing over its details would be better spent on the rockets to get to Mars. By 2022 he said he owned no home at all and rotated through friends' spare bedrooms in the Bay Area. The one exception was his plane, because without it he would have fewer hours to work.

Notice the premise. This is a leverage argument: a minute is worth a million dollars because he sits at the top of a company with two billion dollars of weekly revenue. For ordinary founders his advice is far more modest. To Nikhil Kamath's young Indian audience in 2025 he said only that making a startup succeed at something difficult takes serious hours. With Dwarkesh Patel in 2026 he called it a "maniacal sense of urgency" and said it projects from whoever leads the company through the rest of it.

2. Risk and pain: he never encourages anyone to start a company

Musk is probably the most cited inspiration for would-be founders in the world, yet he almost never encourages anyone to found anything. When Baron asked what could be done to encourage entrepreneurs, he first passed on a friend's description of starting a company as "eating glass and staring into the abyss", then said:

If you need encouragement, don't start a company.— Musk · Ron Baron Investment Conference, 2022 [00:18:23]

Three years later, speaking to young Indian founders, his message was the same: expect to grind very hard, and accept that there is a meaningful chance of failure. He talks about risk coolly, but about the experience of carrying it he can be surprisingly candid. In 2021 Lex Fridman asked how he felt before SpaceX first flew astronauts in May 2020. Extremely stressful, he said. He is not religious, but he got on his knees and prayed for that mission, and he did not sleep. Success, when it came, did not feel like triumph:

For high stress situations, I find it's not so much elation as relief.— Musk · Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [00:02:26]

The reason he gave was his role. As chief engineer of SpaceX he signs off on nearly every design decision, so where others see a beautiful rocket, he sees a readout of risks. The much-retold year 2008 belongs to this thread too. An old interview clip played at the start of the Baron event shows him being asked whether, after a third launch failure in a row, he thought of packing it in; never, he answered. But in the interviews used here he does not retell that year in his own words, so the Boundaries section below fills it in from the public record.

In 2023 Lex tried a different angle: what is something difficult you go through that people don't see? The answer is the darkest line in the whole story.

My mind is a storm, and I don't think most people would want to be me. They may think they would want to be me, but they don't know, they don't understand.— Musk · Lex Fridman #400, 2023 [02:12:43]

Asked whether he gets lonely, he said sometimes, but his kids and friends keep him company; there are many nights he sleeps alone, which he does not have to do, but does. In 2026, when Dwarkesh closed by observing that his companies keep choosing short-term acute pain in order to break a bottleneck, Musk replied:

I have a high pain threshold. That's helpful.— Musk · Dwarkesh Patel, 2026 [02:48:13]

He followed with a piece of advice: it is better to err on the side of optimism and be wrong than on the side of pessimism and be right, because you will be happier. He frames optimism as a quality-of-life choice, not a judgment about the facts, which is worth keeping in mind.

3. Childhood and meaning: why carry all this

Where does the pain threshold come from? He rarely volunteers much about his childhood and keeps it short when asked. In 2026 Peter Diamandis passed on a question from his fourteen-year-old son: what were middle school and high school like for you?

I found school to be quite painful. And it was very boring and in South Africa it was very violent.— Musk · Peter Diamandis #220, 2026 [00:41:05]

Diamandis asked whether successful people need early hardship. Probably a little, Musk said, but it is tricky, because if you love your kids you do not want to create artificial adversity for them. He had a lot of adversity, he allowed, and it probably helped somewhat; then he amended the old saying to "what doesn't maim you makes you stronger". In the same interview he offered another image: arriving in Montreal at seventeen with around 2,500 Canadian dollars in traveler's checks, one bag of books and one bag of clothes. In 2023, when Lex mentioned the difficult childhood described in Walter Isaacson's biography and asked whether he could forgive what happened, Musk asked what forgiveness is and said he did not think he held a resentment, because holding on to grudges does not affect the future in a good way [02:14:00].

The remark about his own brain that gets quoted most is from hosting Saturday Night Live in May 2021, when he joked that he was the first person with Asperger's syndrome to host the show (as the Associated Press reported). He does not return to it in the interviews used here, so this page does not elaborate on his behalf.

What he prefers to talk about is an existential crisis at twelve or thirteen. He told Kamath he was searching for the meaning of life then and read Nietzsche and Schopenhauer [01:37:46]. The answer he settled on is borrowed from Douglas Adams's Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy: the universe is the answer, and the hard part is asking the right question. From Lex in 2021 to Kamath in 2025 the inference barely changes: to learn which questions to ask, we must expand the scope and scale of consciousness. In 2022, at both Baron's conference and TED, he called consciousness "a small candle in a vast darkness" that could easily go out. Mars and a multiplanetary civilization follow from here, a thread developed in the page "Multiplanetary Species".

Yet whenever he talks about motive, he pulls the tone back down. At TED in 2017, when Anderson summed him up as wanting both to save humanity and to inspire it, he said the value of beauty and inspiration is underrated, that he was not trying to be anyone's savior, and then:

I'm just trying to think about the future and not be sad.— Musk · TED, 2017 [00:39:45]

Five years later at the Texas factory, Anderson asked what drives him day to day. Nearly the same answer: curiosity above all, plus a desire to think about the future and not be sad. Asked whether he is sad, he said: sometimes.

How the Claims Fit Together

The three claims form a chain running downward. At the top is meaning: consciousness must continue and grow so that humanity can eventually ask the right questions. Meaning produces a mission, and the mission produces the time arithmetic: his minutes move giant companies, so every minute belongs to the mission, and houses, holidays and sleep come after. Pain is the cost of that arithmetic. A high pain threshold and early adversity let him pay it, and treating optimism as a quality-of-life choice keeps him going. The chain also connects to other pages in this series: his consistently optimistic schedules can be checked in "The Prediction Record", and the background to the 120-hour Twitter weeks is in "X, Speech and Politics".

The weakest link is in the middle. The arithmetic rests on the words "high quality", and he never says whether the minutes of the hundredth hour are still high quality; his argument shows what one good hour is worth, not what the extra dozens of hours are worth. A second tension is that the story is almost impossible to refute. With a purpose as large as "the future of consciousness", any sacrifice can be justified; at TED he even described his companies as philanthropy. And while he says most people would not want to be him and that anyone who needs encouragement should not start a company, his story travels furthest among exactly the people looking for encouragement.

Boundaries

"Every high-quality minute of thinking is worth a million dollars, so I work to the edge of sanity." — TED, 2022, quoted above

Supported, but overstated

Pencavel's 2014 analysis of British munitions workers in the First World War found that output grew proportionally with hours up to about 49 hours a week, then more slowly, peaking near 63; output at 70 hours differed little from output at 56. That is manual work and may not transfer to decision-making, but it points the same way. WHO and ILO estimated in 2021 that working 55 hours or more a week carries about a 35% higher risk of stroke than working 35 to 40. The leverage math holds; the extra hours are not automatically high quality.

"If you need encouragement, don't start a company", because the chance of failure is real. — Ron Baron conference, 2022; Nikhil Kamath, 2025

Supported

In US Bureau of Labor Statistics data, of private establishments opened in the year to March 2014, 79.7% survived one year, 50.8% five years and 37.4% ten years. That counts every new business location, a wider net than tech startups, but it shows the warning is not exaggerated. Musk himself hung on that line in 2008: Falcon 1 reached orbit only on its fourth attempt, in September, and NASA's $1.6 billion, twelve-mission cargo contract came in December.

Crossing Over · Interdisciplinary Connections

  • Psychology · McAdams's redemption narratives: Dan McAdams studies how people tell their lives as stories and finds that many recast bad events as turning points toward good outcomes, a "redemption sequence" associated with well-being. Musk calling a violent schoolyard "probably good" has exactly this shape. The difference is that McAdams studies the teller's well-being; he does not vouch for the story's accuracy.
  • Buddhism · The two arrows: the Sallatha Sutta says an ordinary person struck by the first arrow (bodily pain) adds a second (mental suffering), while the trained person feels only the first. Musk's high pain threshold is enduring the first arrow; the Buddhist move is to stop firing the second. He treats pain as fuel; Buddhism treats suffering as something that can be put down.
  • Complexity science · Anderson's "More Is Different": Philip Anderson argued in 1972 that new properties emerge as systems grow in scale, and Musk's view that more cells mean more consciousness, and more people more of it, runs along the same line. But in theories such as Integrated Information Theory, what matters is how information is integrated, not the number of parts, so "more people, more consciousness" does not follow automatically.
  • Distributed systems · Single point of failure: an organization that runs on its founder's 120-hour weeks and on urgency cascading down from him resembles a system where every request passes through one host. Throughput is capped by that host, and when it fails, everything stops. Baron asked what happens if he is not there; Musk did not answer directly.

Ten Takeaways

  1. In November 2022 Musk said that after taking over Twitter his workload rose from 70–80 hours a week to about 120, seven days a week.
  2. During the hardest years of the Model 3 ramp, Musk lived at the factories and slept on the floor under his desk so workers could see him at shift change.
  3. Musk justifies long hours with leverage arithmetic: with Tesla earning about two billion dollars a week, a high-quality minute of his thinking is worth roughly a million dollars.
  4. In 2020 Musk sold almost all his houses, saying possessions weigh you down and attract attacks, and that the time is better spent on Mars rockets.
  5. Musk passes on a friend's line that starting a company is like eating glass and staring into the abyss, and says those who need encouragement should not start one.
  6. Musk says he prayed on his knees before SpaceX's first crewed launch in 2020, and that success under high stress brings relief rather than elation.
  7. In 2023 Musk told Lex Fridman that his mind is a storm and that most people would not actually want to be him.
  8. Musk describes school in South Africa as painful and violent, grants that early adversity probably helped, but says loving parents cannot manufacture it.
  9. Musk had an existential crisis at twelve or thirteen and later concluded, via the Hitchhiker's Guide, that expanding consciousness is how we learn to ask the right questions.
  10. The Buddhist "two arrows" separates bodily pain from mental suffering; Musk's high pain threshold endures the first arrow, while Buddhism aims to stop the second.
