Elon Musk Series · No. 9

The Prediction Record: What Became of Every "Next Year"

Self-driving, rockets, Mars, Optimus, AGI: his deadlines lined up by the date he gave them, and checked against October 2026

Topic The prediction record: timelines he gave, and what followed · Sources 9 interview transcripts (2017–2026), Tesla Master Plan Part 2 (2016), Tesla Q2 2026 Update · Interviews TED (2017, 2022) · Lex Fridman #252 (2021) · Starfactory tour (2024) · All-In Summit (2024) · Joe Rogan #2404 (2025) · Nikhil Kamath (2025) · Peter Diamandis #220 (2026) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

His Claims

This page does one thing: it balances the books. Musk's deadlines are scattered across a decade of interviews. Before Reading Him: Four Discounts covered how to read them; here they are lined up by the date he said them and checked against the public record as of mid-October 2026. There is one rule. We do not grade right or wrong, only whether a deadline has come due and how much of it was delivered. Where it has not come due, we say so.

1. Self-driving: "this year," every year

The earliest entry is on paper. In July 2016 Musk published, under his own name, Master Plan, Part Deux, and on autonomy he was notably restrained. Every Tesla would get the hardware needed for full self-driving, he wrote, and then added a warning:

It is important to emphasize that refinement and validation of the software will take much longer than putting in place the cameras, radar, sonar and computing hardware.— Musk · Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

He also wrote that even once the software was far better than the average driver, regulatory approval would lag, by different amounts in different places, and that worldwide approval would take something like 6 billion miles of driving. In interviews, though, the dates kept getting closer. At TED in April 2017 he said a Tesla would drive itself from Los Angeles to New York by the end of that year; to Lex Fridman at the end of 2021 he said Level 4 (no human takeover needed under defined conditions) looked likely the following year. Both are quoted in Four Discounts. In April 2022, at the Texas Gigafactory, Chris Anderson pointed out that he had said "this is the year" nearly every year for five years. His answer:

I actually am confident that we will solve it this year.— Musk · TED, 2022 [00:12:04]

By "solve" he meant a crash probability below that of the average driver. He added, himself, that they might be sitting there again a year later saying another year had gone by without it.

Here is the ledger in October 2026. Tesla's Robotaxi opened in Austin in June 2025 with safety monitors on board. From 2026, cars with no one supervising began running in Austin, Dallas, Houston, Miami, Orlando and Tampa, and in September the company announced 1 million unsupervised miles. The software sold to owners has kept "Supervised" in its name. Tesla's Q2 2026 Update shows owners approaching 12 billion cumulative miles on it, twice the 6-billion-mile threshold of the master plan, and the chart still carries the footnote "Active driver supervision required; does not make the vehicle autonomous." In other words, "this year" came due several times, and what arrived was a smaller version: no driver, inside mapped city zones, for a limited fleet.

2. Rockets and Mars: "next year" moved a year, and Mars yielded to the Moon

Four Discounts listed the Mars dates, and A Multiplanetary Species checks them one by one: in 2017, people boarding the ship in eight to ten years; at the end of 2021, a crewed landing in five years at best, ten at worst. What can actually be checked is the gate before Mars, full reusability. In his argument, a Mars city is only affordable if the rocket is recovered whole like an airliner, refuelled and flown again. On the Starfactory tour in June 2024 he acknowledged that nobody had achieved full reusability, SpaceX included, and said he hoped to get there the next year [00:48:58]. Sixteen months later, on Joe Rogan at the end of October 2025, he said nearly the same thing: not achieved yet, but at last a rocket where it is possible, and he thought they would achieve it the next year [00:25:25].

As of late September 2026, half of it is done. The Super Heavy booster has been caught by the tower's arms since Flight 5, and on Flight 9 in May 2025 a previously caught booster flew again for the first time. The Starship upper stage has not yet been caught or reflown. On Flight 14 on September 28 it reached orbit for the first time and released 26 Starlink satellites, then ended the mission early and exploded after splashing down in the northern Pacific. The 2025 "next year" has expired; the 2026 one has two and a half months left.

Mars itself changed places in the queue. On December 22, 2025, at the Texas Gigafactory, Peter Diamandis asked whether he would try for the late-2026 Mars window. He said they could, but it would be a low-probability shot and something of a distraction [01:36:31]; pressed on 2029, he said it was "not out of the question." In the same stretch he said space needed a new game plan, starting with a permanently crewed Moon base. Less than two months later, according to The Wall Street Journal, SpaceX told investors it would prioritise its lunar mission for NASA, and Musk wrote on X that SpaceX would begin building a Mars city in about five to seven years (Payload's report). Measured from the 2017 "boarding in eight to ten years," the whole Mars timeline has slid back another round.

3. Optimus and AI: mostly not yet due

The first Optimus deadline was met. At the end of 2021 he told Lex there would probably be a decent prototype toward the end of the following year, and at AI Day in September 2022 prototypes did walk on stage. After that, the deadlines grew. At TED in April 2022 he said there would be an interesting prototype that year, maybe something useful the next, and quite likely within two years [00:24:23]. At the All-In Summit in September 2024 he put a million units a year about five, maybe six years out [00:49:39]. To Nikhil Kamath in November 2025 he said Optimus would hopefully start production at scale the next year [00:22:27]. Tesla's July 2026 update says the Model S and X lines in Fremont have been decommissioned and first-generation Optimus lines are being installed, with production expected soon; the first robots built go to an internal "Optimus Academy" for training data collection. The million-a-year deadline does not come due until around 2029.

The AI dates are the nearest and the biggest. In the Diamandis episode he said AGI (artificial general intelligence) would arrive in 2026, adding that he had been saying so for a while. Then he gave a further number:

I'm confident by 2030 AI will exceed the intelligence of all humans combined.— Musk · Peter Diamandis #220, 2026 [01:16:20]

To Dwarkesh Patel in February 2026 he said he would be surprised if "digital human emulation" (AI that can do anything a human with a computer can) were not solved by the end of the year, and predicted that within 36 months, maybe 30, space would be the cheapest place to put AI [00:03:02]. None of these has come due, and AGI has no agreed test, so even at the deadline it will be hard to settle. The 36-month claim is checked against physics and cost in AI: From "Summoning the Demon" to xAI, which also covers the reasoning behind these dates.

Then there is a category he set down himself. Hyperloop (pods running through near-vacuum tubes) is often cited as something he promised and never built, but nowhere in this series' sources does he give it a timeline. Asked about it at TED in 2017, he said they had been puttering around with it, had built a test tube next to SpaceX for a student competition, and that it was a hobby [00:06:58]. His bet then was The Boring Company's tunnels: halve the diameter, have the machine dig and reinforce at the same time, and cut the cost per mile by more than an order of magnitude. The companies that actually staked themselves on Hyperloop were other people's, and the best known, Hyperloop One, shut down at the end of 2023 without ever winning a construction contract.

4. How he explains the delays

His explanations come in three layers. Four Discounts covered the first two: set the most aggressive internal timeline, because schedules expand like a gas; and the media report his misses and ignore his hits. At the 2022 TED he offered a hit: for Tesla's vehicle growth he had predicted 50% and they did 80%. The third layer is more personal. At the All-In Summit in September 2024, talking about California's high-speed rail, he said in passing:

I realize sometimes I'm perhaps a little optimistic with schedules, but, you know, I mean, I wouldn't be doing the things I'm doing if I was, you know, not an optimist.— Musk · All-In Summit, 2024 [00:10:46]

That sentence treats optimism as a precondition for the work rather than a bias to be corrected. In 2026 he put a number on it: his deadlines are the most aggressive he can think of that could be met with 50% probability, a line quoted in First Principles and the Five-Step Algorithm. By that standard, half his deadlines are meant to be late.

Is overpromising a deliberate management tool? Internally, by his own account, yes. Externally he has never said so, but he knows the outside world keeps score. Telling Kamath in November 2025 that within twenty years work would become optional, he voiced the critics' line first:

people can play this back in 20 years and say, look, Elon made this ridiculous prediction and it's not true— Musk · Nikhil Kamath, 2025 [00:31:52]

Then he said he thought it would turn out true, perhaps in as little as ten or fifteen years.

How the Claims Fit Together

Line the dates up and three patterns appear. First, the same target gets rolled over: full self-driving was "this year" for several years running, full reusability "next year" two years running. Second, what finally arrives is usually a scaled-down version: not driverless travel between any two cities, but a few hundred cars in six metro areas; not Mars in 2026, but the Moon first. Third, what he wrote was more cautious than what he said. The 2016 master plan warned that software validation and regulatory approval would take a long time; the interviews of the following decade kept offering deadlines under a year. The deadlines met most promptly were the ones fully in his own hands, such as building a prototype.

These patterns connect to the rest of the series. First Principles shows him setting deadlines at 50% confidence; The Factory Is the Product covers his view that prototypes are easy and production is hard, and Optimus, a prototype in 2022 and still installing lines in mid-2026, is a fair footnote to that. The weakest link is the defence that "it happened eventually." When what happens is a smaller version, and the target shifts from Mars to the Moon, "it happened" is nearly always true, and so it stops being testable. A practical way to read him is to split each date apart: take his direction seriously, discount his timing by the record, and judge what "it" actually is only when the deadline arrives.

Limits

"Refining and validating the software will take much longer than installing the hardware; even when the software is far better than a human, regulatory approval will lag." — Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

Supported

Tesla's Q2 2026 Update shows about 12 billion cumulative FSD miles, twice his estimate of "around 6 billion miles for worldwide approval," and the owner version still requires driver supervision. Service with no safety monitor began only in 2026, in six metro areas, reaching about 1 million miles by September. The delay he predicted came true, and ran longer than he wrote; the 6-billion-mile threshold was clearly too low.

"Full reusability, I'm hopeful we'll achieve it next year." — Starfactory tour, June 2024; repeated to Rogan in October 2025, see above

Contrary to the evidence

This checks the 2025 deadline. That year the booster was caught and reflown, half the goal; the Starship upper stage was never caught or reflown. On Flight 14 on September 28, 2026, the upper stage reached orbit for the first time and exploded after splashdown, still unrecovered. The 2026 deadline he set in 2025 has two and a half months to run.

Crossing Over · Interdisciplinary Connections

  • Forecasting research · Tetlock and the Brier score: Philip Tetlock's Good Judgment Project scores forecasters with the Brier score, which requires each question to state in advance a deadline and a resolution criterion. Musk's dates usually have only the first half: what counts as "solving self-driving" or "reaching AGI" is decided by the person who made the call. Alike in that both give dates; unlike in that there is no referee agreed beforehand, so his forecasts are close to unscorable.
  • Software engineering · Hofstadter's law: In Gödel, Escher, Bach, Douglas Hofstadter wrote that it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take this law into account. The self-driving delays fit this self-referential law best: each time there is a new architecture and visible progress. Unlike it, the equally delayed rocket reuse has been achieved on the booster; what drags longest is always software and validation.
  • Monetary policy · Forward guidance: Central banks announce a future path for interest rates to shape behaviour today through expectations; it works only as far as it is credible, and markets discount it after repeated reversals. Musk's dates manage expectations for teams, suppliers and investors in the same way. Unlike a central bank, which revises guidance through formal procedure and explanation, his dates are usually just quietly replaced by the next one.
  • Sociology · The self-fulfilling prophecy: Robert Merton argued in 1948 that a prophecy, once believed and acted on, can make itself come true. Aggressive deadlines push a team faster, which is the self-fulfilling side. But physics and regulators do not yield to belief, and software validation and approvals take the time they take.

Ten Takeaways

  1. In Master Plan Part 2 (2016), Musk wrote that refining and validating self-driving software would take far longer than installing hardware, and that regulatory approval would lag further.
  2. In interviews in 2017, 2021 and 2022, Musk set full self-driving deadlines for the same or the following year; all of those deadlines have passed.
  3. As of September 2026, Tesla runs unsupervised Robotaxis in six metro areas, while the FSD sold to owners still requires driver supervision.
  4. Musk said in 2024 he hoped Starship would be fully reusable the next year, and in 2025 said "next year" again; in between, only the booster was recovered and reflown.
  5. On September 28, 2026, Starship's 14th test flight reached orbit for the first time and deployed 26 Starlink satellites; the upper stage was still not recovered.
  6. In late 2025 Musk called a 2026 Mars attempt a low-probability shot and a distraction; in February 2026 SpaceX put the Moon first.
  7. The Optimus prototype appeared in 2022 on the deadline Musk gave in 2021; the later "useful" and "volume production" deadlines kept slipping, and lines were still being installed in mid-2026.
  8. Musk's predictions of AGI in 2026 and AI exceeding all humans combined by 2030 have not come due and lack an agreed test.
  9. Musk treats optimism as a precondition for his work and sets deadlines at "most aggressive with 50% probability," so half are meant to be late.
  10. Tetlock's forecast scoring requires an agreed deadline and resolution criterion in advance; Musk's dates usually come without the criterion.
