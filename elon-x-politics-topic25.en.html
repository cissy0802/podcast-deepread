Elon Musk Series · No. 8

X, Speech and Politics: Musk After 2022

Why he bought Twitter, why he went into government to cut spending, and why he now says politics is best avoided

Topic The Twitter purchase, free speech, DOGE and his politics · Material 8 interview transcripts (2021–2026) · Interviews Lex Fridman #252 (2021), #400 (2023) · Ron Baron Investment Conference (2022) · All-In Summit (2024) · Joe Rogan #2223 (2024), #2404 (2025) · Nikhil Kamath (2025) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

What he claims

Before 2022, Musk talked about rockets, batteries and Mars, and politics came up only in passing. In 2022 he bought Twitter for $44 billion, and over the next two years politics took up more and more of his long interviews: first free speech, then the size of government, then DOGE, the Department of Government Efficiency he helped run to cut federal spending. This page does not rule on whether his positions are right. It lays out the reasons he himself gives, in order, and checks the parts that can be tested against public records.

1. Why buy Twitter: no free speech, no democracy

His stated reason has stayed consistent over the years, but the stakes keep rising. At Ron Baron's investment conference in late 2022, on the day he laid off half of Twitter's staff, he was still talking business. Twitter already had revenue problems, he said, and the $8 monthly verification was meant to make crime not pay: a bot account cost less than a penny to create, but if every account cost $8, running a hundred thousand fake ones would be too expensive. He said he wanted about 80% of Americans to feel comfortable in a "digital town square," and admitted that major advertisers had pulled out under pressure from activist groups, even though, he stressed, the moderation rules had not changed [00:41:29].

A year later, talking to Lex Fridman, he framed it as principle. Asked whether X had moved right, he said it had moved to the center, and that from the far left everything looks like the right. Then he gave his definition:

Free speech only matters if people you don't like are allowed to say things you don't like, because if that's not the case you don't have free speech.— Musk · Lex Fridman #400, 2023 [01:42:26]

In the same stretch he explained why he trusts Community Notes: a note only shows if people who have historically disagreed in their ratings agree on it, and all the code and data are public, so anyone can recompute a note. He said he could not change a note even with a gun to his head.

By the eve of the 2024 election, on Joe Rogan's show, the reason had become the survival of democracy. He said he had believed at the time that the purchase was existential for the United States:

If you don't have freedom of speech, people cannot make an informed vote.— Musk · Joe Rogan #2223, 2024 [01:50:14]

In the same episode he conceded the deal was "way overpriced," said that if he hadn't done it things would have gone badly, and blamed the fall in value on an advertiser boycott that he says left-wing groups organized. By 2025 he was comparing old Twitter to Wormtongue, the adviser in The Lord of the Rings who whispers falsehoods to the king, spreading what he called a nihilistic "mind virus" to the world.

So where is the line on speech? His answer has always been the law. During X's clash with Brazil's courts in 2024, he told the All-In Summit that X takes down whatever a country's law forbids, and that a platform should not be writing laws for countries. In 2025 he summed up X's principle for the Indian investor Nikhil Kamath: obey each country's laws, but

not to put our thumb on the scale beyond the laws of a country— Musk · Nikhil Kamath, 2025 [00:06:09]

There is a premise here he doesn't unpack. If the boundary of speech is local law, then "free speech" means three different things in Germany, Brazil and the United States. His only answer is that people who want different laws should change them.

2. From "garbage collection for laws" to DOGE

His view of government predates Twitter. In late 2021 he told Lex that rules and regulations are like the code that runs a civilization, but with "code accumulation but no code removal," so over time they turn into archaic bloatware. He imagined Martian laws with sunset clauses that need a fresh vote to survive, and said repealing a law should be easier than passing one, say 60% to pass and 40% to repeal.

Three years later the metaphor came down to earth. At the All-In Summit in September 2024 he said the last serious effort to shrink government was Reagan in the early 1980s, and that America was like Gulliver tied down by a million little strings, none of them bad on its own. His example was SpaceX being fined $140,000 by the EPA for cooling water spilled at the launch pad, with Starship launch applications held until it paid. His plan was to review rules in public, keep the ones people really want, and put back any that turned out to matter, the same "delete first" instinct described in the series page on first principles and the five-step algorithm. For laid-off federal workers he suggested a year or two of severance, and pointed to East versus West Germany and North versus South Korea: the same people, he said, produce far more under a more efficient system.

His case for urgency was the debt. He kept saying interest on the national debt now exceeds the defense budget and that without cuts America goes bankrupt. The Limits section below checks that number.

DOGE was set up in January 2025, and Musk took part as a "special government employee," a status he says limited him to about 120 days. Several months after leaving, he looked back on Rogan #2404. What he was proudest of were two unglamorous changes: every payment from the Treasury's main payment systems must now carry a congressional appropriation code, and the comment field can no longer be blank. He said huge numbers of "zombie payments" kept going out after whoever approved them had retired, died or moved on. He also described roughly 20 million people in the Social Security database who, by birth date, could not be alive, saying most were not receiving money and that the real hole was other agencies using that database to check whether someone is alive. On how far to push fraud enforcement, he chose his words carefully:

You actually can't manage to zero fraud. You can manage to low fraud number but not to zero fraud.— Musk · Joe Rogan #2404, 2025 [02:15:12]

His verdict on DOGE is gloomier than his early pitch. Cutting waste and fraud only lengthens the runway, he says; it cannot pay off the debt, and the only way out is AI and robotics growing the economy. That thread continues in the series page on AI.

3. How he describes his own politics

Musk's line is that the left changed, not him. In 2023 he told Lex he was probably left of center on social issues and a little right of center on economic ones, which he thought described half the country; and if fighting the "woke mind virus" counts as politics, then yes, he had become more political. In 2024 he told Rogan he had been on the left until about three years earlier, but that the left had gone from the free-speech party to the anti-free-speech party. In that episode he called the election the "last chance" to preserve democracy in America, arguing that mass immigration would reshape swing-state electorates. That is his judgment, not a fact this page can verify.

After leaving Washington his tone shifted. In October 2025 he told Rogan that 10 to 20% of the money from organizations whose funding he cut went to the Republican side, and that they complained just as loudly. A month later, when Nikhil Kamath asked how he deals with politicians, he said:

Politics is a blood sport, you know? It's like you enter politics, they're going to go for the jugular.— Musk · Nikhil Kamath, 2025 [01:43:02]

His conclusion was to avoid it where possible. But he has no regrets. In early 2026 Dwarkesh Patel asked him to grade the experience. Politics makes people lose objectivity, he said, and you can almost never reason with the other tribe; still, buying Twitter and helping elect Trump

needed to be done to maximize the probability that the future is good— Musk · Dwarkesh Patel, 2026 [02:31:11]

How the claims fit together

In Musk's own telling these are one chain. If a few people control the information environment, voters cannot make informed choices and democracy becomes a shell, so he bought Twitter. If government rules only ever grow, spending and interest compound until the country goes broke, so he joined DOGE. And since cuts only buy time, the real cure is AI and robots, which brings the story back to his companies. He looks at a platform and at a government with the same engineer's eye: rules are code, bloat should be deleted, and mistakes can be put back. It is the same fast-iteration logic described in the series page on manufacturing.

The weakest link is the numbers. Deleting is justified only if what gets deleted really is waste, yet the DOGE results he cites differ from interview to interview and rarely come with accounts anyone can check. A second tension sits in the boundary itself: he treats the law as the neutral floor for speech on his platform, while also arguing that the law is bloated code in need of heavy deletion. On which rules stay and which go, he has in practice been the referee; and whether a wrongly deleted rule can be "put back" depends on whether the people and agencies behind it are still there.

Limits

Since going private, X no longer publishes financial statements, so user and revenue figures are the company's own claims and cannot be checked from outside. The one public valuation came from Musk himself: in March 2025 his xAI absorbed X in an all-stock deal valuing it at $33 billion, with Musk controlling both sides. For contrast, a critics' view: in February 2025 Kara Swisher and Ezra Klein argued on Klein's show that the purchase should not be judged by financial return, because what it bought was political influence. That is the critics' judgment. The claims below can be tested against data.

Musk's guess: DOGE prevented fraud and waste on the order of $200–300 billion a year. — Joe Rogan #2404, October 2025 [02:22:25]

Insufficient evidence

GAO's August 2026 audit could only examine the $110 billion of DOGE's $215 billion headline that is itemized. Just 43.2% of reported contract savings tied to contracts actually terminated in whole or part; 96% of grant savings could not be verified. Treasury's year-end statement shows FY2025 federal outlays of $7.01 trillion, $275 billion more than the year before. His separate claim that the payment-code change alone saves $100–200 billion a year has no published accounting either.

Musk: about 20 million people in the Social Security database could not possibly be alive, based on their birth dates. — Joe Rogan #2404, October 2025 [02:09:40]

Supported, but overstated

A 2023 audit by Social Security's Inspector General found about 18.9 million numberholders born in 1920 or earlier with no death record, against a Census estimate of about 86,000 Americans aged 100 or older; only about 44,000 of them were receiving payments. The audit said missing death data hampers government-wide fraud prevention, but it did not estimate any resulting losses. The count holds; "massive fraud" still lacks the accounts.

Musk: interest on the national debt has just passed the defense budget, at over a trillion dollars a year. — All-In Summit, September 2024 [00:18:55]

Supported, but overstated

Treasury's monthly statements put FY2024 net interest at $881 billion against $874 billion for national defense, and FY2025 at $970 billion against $917 billion. Interest does exceed defense, but "over a trillion" is well above the net interest figure Treasury reports.

Crossing Over · Cross-disciplinary connections

  • Political economy · Mancur Olson, The Rise and Decline of Nations: Olson argued in 1982 that long-stable societies accumulate distributional coalitions that make economies rigid, and that war and upheaval tend to sweep them away. Musk told Rogan much the same: war used to clear the decks of regulation, and America has gone too long without an existential threat. The likeness: both see sclerosis as a by-product of peace. The difference: Olson means organized interest groups, Musk means the number of rules, and deleting rules does not necessarily dismantle the groups behind them.
  • Economics · Hirschman's Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: when an organization declines, members either leave or speak up. Advertisers pulling spend and users moving to Bluesky or Threads are exit; by calling the ad boycott an attack on speech, Musk treats one form of exit as the suppression of voice.
  • Social science · Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The ever-rising "total savings" on DOGE's website was such a target, and GAO found it padded with contracts that were never terminated and leases already being wound down before DOGE existed.
  • Constitutional law · The state action doctrine: the First Amendment binds the government, not private companies. So Musk's charge that old Twitter removed posts at government request is the part that touches the First Amendment legally; his own law-based takedowns and advertisers' decisions to leave are private choices.

Ten takeaways

  1. Elon Musk bought Twitter for $44 billion in 2022, later conceded it was way overpriced, and blamed the fall in value on an advertiser boycott.
  2. Musk introduced $8 paid verification early on so that every bot account would cost money and crime on the platform would stop paying.
  3. Musk defines free speech this way: it only matters if people you dislike are allowed to say things you dislike.
  4. Musk says X's principle is to obey each country's laws but not tip the scale beyond them, so the boundary of speech varies by country.
  5. Musk trusts Community Notes because a note shows only when people who historically disagreed agree, and its code and data are public.
  6. As early as 2021 Musk compared regulation to code that is only ever added, proposing sunset clauses and easier repeal than passage.
  7. Musk says DOGE's most lasting change is requiring an appropriation code and a comment on every federal payment, to stop unattended zombie payments.
  8. After leaving DOGE, Musk concluded that spending cuts only delay the debt crisis and that only AI and robotics can grow the way out.
  9. Musk calls himself left of center socially and right of center economically, and says the left moved from pro-speech to anti-speech.
  10. Political economist Mancur Olson argued that long peace accumulates entrenched interest groups, an idea close to but distinct from Musk's view that rules only grow.
