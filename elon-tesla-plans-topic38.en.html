Elon Musk Series · No. 5

Four master plans, each one rewriting what Tesla is

A sports car in 2006, self-driving in 2016, an energy ledger in 2023, robots in 2025: what was delivered, and what was quietly swapped out

Topic Four master plans: from sports cars to energy to robots · Sources Tesla's four master plans (2006–2025), 6 interview transcripts (2017–2026), NHTSA's 2024 closing report, Tesla's Q2 2026 update · Interviews TED (2017, 2022) · Lex Fridman #252 (2021) · Ron Baron Investment Conference (2022) · All-In Summit (2024) · Dwarkesh Patel (2026)

What he claims

Tesla has published four master plans so far, ten years, seven years and two and a half years apart. The first two are blog posts Musk wrote under his own name; the last two are company documents. Lay them out in order, set them beside what he has said in interviews about self-driving, Robotaxi and Optimus, and you can watch a company answer the question "what is Tesla?" again and again: an electric sports car company, then an energy company, then a self-driving company, and now a robot company.

1. 2006: use the sports car to pay for the cheap car

In August 2006 Musk posted a blog entry on Tesla's site with a wink in the title: "just between you and me." His day job was SpaceX; at Tesla he was chairman and the main funder, and the Roadster had not shipped. The post opens by conceding that the world does not need another high-performance sports car and that it will not do much for global carbon emissions. So why start there? Almost every new technology starts with a high unit cost, so Tesla would enter at the top of the market, where buyers pay a premium, then move down model by model, raising volume and cutting price, with all free cash flow going back into R&D.

When someone buys the Tesla Roadster sports car, they are actually helping pay for development of the low cost family car.— Musk · The Secret Tesla Motors Master Plan, 2006

He promised that the second model would be a sporty four-door family car at roughly half the Roadster's $89k price, and the third cheaper still. The second half of the post runs a well-to-wheel efficiency calculation, concluding that even on electricity made entirely from natural gas, the Roadster is twice as efficient as a Prius. The plan itself fits in four lines: build a sports car; use that money to build an affordable car; use that money to build an even more affordable car; meanwhile offer zero-emission power generation. The last line is "Don't tell anyone." The car was the means. The stated goal was to speed the move from a "mine-and-burn" hydrocarbon economy to a solar electric one.

That road was mostly travelled. Roadster deliveries began in February 2008, then came Model S and Model X, and Model 3 deliveries began in July 2017. Writing the second plan in 2016, Musk said the first was in its final stages, and added something he had not said ten years earlier:

Starting a car company is idiotic and an electric car company is idiocy squared.— Musk · Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

Starting with an expensive low-volume car, he explained, was all he could afford with his PayPal money, and the odds were so poor he did not want to risk anyone else's. He also admitted that one aim of the first plan had been to fend off the charge that Tesla only built cars for the rich, and that it "pretty much completely failed" at that.

2. 2016: the car should drive itself, and earn money for its owner

The second plan, again signed by Musk, comes down to four items: solar roofs with integrated batteries; an electric lineup covering every major segment; self-driving ten times safer than a human, learned from a huge fleet; and a car that makes money for you when you are not using it. The first item paved the way for the SolarCity merger then under way, which the plan called an "accident of history" that the two were separate at all; the deal closed that November. The second named a compact SUV, a new kind of pickup, a heavy truck and a small urban bus, and said engineering had turned to "the machine that makes the machine," a thread left to The factory is the product.

The last two items are what rewrote Tesla's identity. The plan said every Tesla would carry the hardware needed for full self-driving, and that refining and validating the software would take much longer than the hardware; worldwide regulatory approval would need something like 6 billion miles of driving, while the fleet was then learning from just over 3 million miles a day. It also defended shipping partial autonomy now: used correctly, Autopilot was already significantly safer than a person driving alone, and holding it back for fear of bad press or legal liability would be morally reprehensible.

The fourth item turned the car into an asset. Once self-driving was approved, owners could add their car to a Tesla shared fleet with a tap in the app and let it carry passengers while they were at work or on vacation, offsetting much of the monthly payment and at times exceeding it; in cities where owners' cars fell short, Tesla would run its own fleet. The reasoning was a utilization sum: most cars are in use only 5% to 10% of the day. Right after that comes the line that is easiest to miss:

A lower cost vehicle than the Model 3 is unlikely to be necessary, because of the third part of the plan described below.— Musk · Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

In other words, the endpoint of the first plan had been rewritten. A cheap car was no longer the goal, because autonomy and sharing would push down the true cost of owning a Tesla. At TED in 2017, Chris Anderson asked whether a car really would go off and drive strangers around to pay its owner's lease. He did not hesitate:

That's 100% what will occur. It's just a question of when.— Musk · TED, 2017 [00:19:03]

3. 2023 and 2025: from an energy ledger to "sustainable abundance"

The third plan was presented in March 2023, with a long white paper following in April. It no longer carries Musk's name; it is Tesla's white paper, and its acknowledgements list a group of Tesla staff and advisors. It also barely mentions Tesla, doing the energy accounts for the whole planet instead: to leave fossil fuels behind entirely takes 240 TWh of storage, 30 TW of renewable generation and $10 trillion of manufacturing investment, using 0.21% of land and half the energy we use today. In this paper autonomy gets one sentence, saying it could shrink the global fleet by raising vehicle utilization. The first row of the vehicle table is a compact car, and the column for the matching Tesla model reads "TBD." The cheap car the second plan said was unnecessary had come back as a blank cell.

Its way back was bumpy. At Ron Baron's investment conference in 2022, Musk said Tesla expected to make cars more affordable than the Model 3 and Model Y, then said that by far the biggest factor in a car's value was autonomy [00:07:55]. In April 2025 TechCrunch reported that Musk had reportedly dropped plans for a human-driven cheap car on the new platform in favor of stripped-down versions of existing models. The Model 3 and Model Y Standard that arrived that October started at $38,630 and $41,630 with fees, in exchange for cloth seats, seven speakers instead of fifteen, and less range.

Meanwhile, something the second plan never mentioned grew into the lead role. When Lex Fridman asked about the Tesla Bot at the end of 2021, Musk still framed it modestly:

It's not quite in Tesla's primary mission direction of accelerating sustainable energy— Musk · Lex Fridman #252, 2021 [01:37:27]

He said the robots would start with dangerous, boring, repetitive jobs, and explained where the idea came from: to make cars drive themselves Tesla had to solve "real-world AI," and a car is already a robot on four wheels, so legs are a natural extension. Four months later at the Austin Gigafactory, Chris Anderson recalled him calling Optimus Tesla's most important product development that year, and he went on:

People have no idea, this is going to be bigger than the car.— Musk · TED, 2022 [00:21:22]

In the same conversation he said there would be an interesting prototype that year, maybe something useful the next, and quite likely within two years. By the 2024 All-In Summit he had taken it as far as it goes:

I think the sort of useful humanoid robot opportunity is the single biggest opportunity ever— Musk · All-In Summit, 2024 [00:49:39]

In early 2026 he told Dwarkesh Patel that cars would soon have some 10 million vehicles on the road gathering data, and robots have no such fleet, so Tesla would first build 10,000 to 30,000 of them for an "Optimus Academy" where they practice tasks in the real world [01:23:00].

On September 1, 2025, the fourth plan appeared, signed "The Tesla Team." It contains not a single number or year. Its body is a set of principles, and the first reads:

Growth is infinite. Growth in one area does not require decline in another.— The Tesla Team · Master Plan Part IV, 2025

Here Optimus is to take over monotonous and dangerous work and "give people back more time." The ending retells the first three plans as a staircase: Roadster, then Model S and Model X, then Model 3 and Model Y, and this time "not a single step but a leap forward." Less than a year later, Tesla's Q2 2026 update reported that the Model S and Model X lines at Fremont had been decommissioned and first-generation Optimus lines were going in, with the first robots headed for the Optimus Academy to collect training data. The "second model" of the first plan had cleared its factory floor for the robot of the fourth.

How the claims interlock

All four plans share one skeleton: use the product that makes money now to fund the next, bigger bet. The sports car pays for the family car, the family car's fleet feeds data to self-driving, and the vision and chips trained for self-driving move into a robot with legs. What changes is the name of the overall goal. In 2006 it was the move from a hydrocarbon economy to a solar electric one; in 2016, accelerating the advent of sustainable energy; in 2023, "Sustainable Energy for All of Earth"; by 2025, "sustainable abundance." The word "sustainable" stayed, but its meaning drifted from cutting carbon to unlimited growth.

Check the second plan's list as of October 2026. The compact SUV (Model Y) and the pickup (Cybertruck) are on sale. The Semi was to be unveiled the following year; Tesla's Q2 2026 update still says it is on track for production this year at a new factory in Nevada. The small urban bus has not appeared. The half of the shared fleet that Tesla runs itself started in Austin in June 2025 with safety monitors and by Q2 2026 was live in seven metros, six of them ramping unsupervised service; the half where owners add their own cars has not begun. On the solar roof, Musk said in 2017 that fifteen years on it would be unusual for a roof not to have solar [00:24:37]. That deadline is 2032, but Tesla's Q2 2026 update does not mention it.

The weakest link is that each new plan was written before the previous one was finished, and each staked its next step on self-driving being nearly here. In 2016, sharing made a cheap car unnecessary. At the end of 2021 he told Lex that FSD being safer than the average driver "certainly appears" to be coming the next year [01:27:26]; in spring 2022 he told TED he was confident it would be solved that year [00:12:04]. Every year autonomy slips, whatever step it displaced has to be put back, which is how the cheap car returned as a stripped-down trim; and in those same years the robot became the new destination. So reading these plans means asking not only which steps were delivered but which were quietly replaced by the next plan. How those deadlines turned out is tallied in The prediction record.

Limits

"When used correctly, it is already significantly safer than a person driving by themselves." — Master Plan, Part Deux, 2016

Insufficient evidence

The plan's support is that U.S. roads see one death every 89 million miles and Autopilot miles would soon exceed twice that. That is total mileage, not a crash rate, with no matching for road type or driver. NHTSA's EA22002 investigation, closed in April 2024, analyzed 956 crashes and found Autopilot's weak driver-engagement controls mismatched with its permissive capabilities, leading to "foreseeable misuse," with at least 13 fatal crashes. "Used correctly" is precisely the least secure assumption.

"As of today, all Tesla vehicles produced in our factory – including Model 3 – will have the hardware needed for full self-driving capability." — Tesla blog, October 2016, previewed in the second plan three months earlier

Contrary to evidence

In 2019 Tesla began swapping in the newer HW3 computer for owners of cars with the 2016-generation hardware who had bought FSD. In January 2025 Musk said that "the honest answer" was that HW3 would need upgrading too. One hardware swap was not enough and another is due, so the 2016 claim that every car already had what it needed did not hold.

Crossovers

  • Economics · Wright's law: In 1936 T. P. Wright, studying aircraft production, found that each doubling of cumulative output cut unit cost by a roughly constant fraction. The first plan's "start expensive, then scale down in price" is a bet on exactly that learning curve. But the curve runs on cumulative volume: a sports car can be as pricey as you like, and if it does not sell in numbers it will not move you down the curve. Tesla's real test was surviving to Model 3 volumes.
  • Evolutionary biology · Exaptation: Gould and Vrba coined "exaptation" in 1982 for a structure that evolved for one use and was later co-opted for another, as feathers may have served warmth before flight. A vision network and data engine built for self-driving, repurposed as a robot's brain, is the same kind of co-option. The difference is that evolution's co-options have no designer; this one, Musk says, took him a while to realize.
  • Philosophy · The ship of Theseus: replace the planks one by one, and is it still the same ship? Tesla's name has not changed, but its mission has gone from cutting carbon to abundance, its lead product from a sports car to a robot, and in 2026 even the Model S line made way for Optimus. The difference is that Theseus's ship was repaired to stay what it was, while Tesla replaces planks to become something else.

Ten takeaways

  1. Tesla has four master plans: Musk signed the 2006 and 2016 ones, the 2023 one is a company white paper, and the 2025 one is signed "The Tesla Team."
  2. Tesla's first master plan in 2006 proposed building a pricey sports car, using its profits for a cheaper family car, then a cheaper one still, while offering zero-emission power.
  3. Tesla's first master plan states that the car is only a means; the goal is to speed the shift from a hydrocarbon economy to a solar electric one.
  4. In the 2016 second master plan, Musk admitted Tesla began with an expensive car because his PayPal money could afford nothing else, calling an electric car startup "idiocy squared."
  5. Tesla's 2016 second master plan added self-driving and a shared fleet in which idle owner cars earn money, and said a car cheaper than the Model 3 was unlikely to be necessary.
  6. Tesla's 2023 third master plan does the global energy accounts, needing 240 TWh of storage and 30 TW of renewables, and lists its compact car model as "TBD."
  7. Tesla's cheaper car returned as stripped-down trims: the Model 3 and Model Y Standard of October 2025 trade cloth seats and shorter range for lower prices.
  8. Musk said in 2021 that a humanoid robot was not quite in Tesla's primary mission, in 2022 that it would be bigger than the car, and in 2024 that it was the biggest opportunity ever.
  9. Tesla's 2025 fourth master plan has no numbers or years and opens its principles with "Growth is infinite"; in 2026 Tesla tore out the Model S and Model X lines to install Optimus lines.
  10. Tesla repurposing a vision network built for self-driving as a robot's brain resembles exaptation in evolutionary biology, where a structure evolved for one use is co-opted for another.
