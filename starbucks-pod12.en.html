The Coffee Was the Conduit: Starbucks' Biggest Enemy Is Its Own Ubiquity

Howard Schultz × Ben Gilbert, David Rosenthal · "Starbucks (with Howard Schultz)"

Show Acquired · Season 14, Episode 5 · 2024-06-04 · Length 196 min · Guest Howard Schultz · Hosts Ben Gilbert, David Rosenthal · Video YouTube

The moment

In 1987, Starbucks founder Jerry Baldwin decided to sell the company for $3.8 million and gave Howard Schultz the first shot at buying it. Schultz had no money and went out to raise it. About halfway there, Baldwin came to him with bad news: one of Schultz's own investors had put an all-cash, no-diligence offer on the table to take the company for himself.

After a basketball game that night, Schultz nearly broke down in front of a lawyer friend, who brought him in the next morning to meet the senior partner at his firm: Bill Gates Sr., a six-foot-seven mountain of a man. Gates asked two questions. Is everything you told me true? Have you left anything out? An hour later he walked Schultz across the street into the investor's office, leaned on the desk with both hands, and said:

I don't know what you are planning, but whatever it is, it's not going to happen.— Bill Gates Sr. (as recounted by Howard Schultz) · [00:33:32]

The whole thing took five minutes. Schultz says Gates Sr. never spoke about it publicly; his son apparently heard the story for the first time around 2015, at one of Schultz's talks. The episode is full of stories like this, but the question tying them together is harder: a company built on human warmth grows to nearly 40,000 stores. How much of the warmth survives?

Who’s talking, and when

Acquired usually has its two hosts trade off telling a company's history: David Rosenthal tells the story, Ben Gilbert runs the numbers and pushes back. This time they made the subject their "third co-host" and recorded in person at the Schultz Family Foundation in Seattle. They declared their bias up front: Ben starts every day with an iced almond milk latte with whipped cream, and David exported his credit card history to find he had spent $23,000 at Starbucks since 2011.

Schultz grew up in public housing in Brooklyn, the son of a blue-collar veteran who never got respect. He sold Xerox word processors door to door, got a 3 out of 5 on his annual review, and decided on the spot to leave. In 1982 he joined a three-store Starbucks that sold only beans, and went on to serve as CEO three times, the last as interim CEO in 2022–23.

The timing was awkward. Starbucks' late-April 2024 results showed global comparable store sales down 4%, and the stock fell hard. Schultz was no longer on the board and had published an open letter on LinkedIn criticizing management. A little over two months after the episode aired, the board named Brian Niccol as CEO. So the episode is half founding story, half a founder shouting advice from the sidelines.

Glossary

  • Comparable store sales: year-over-year sales at stores open at least a year, stripping out growth from new openings. Retail's core measure of whether the underlying business is healthy.
  • Float: money customers have preloaded onto stored-value cards but not yet spent. For the company, effectively an interest-free loan.

The episode, worked through

1. Not selling coffee: selling a repeat habit at an 80% gross margin

In 1983 Schultz went to a housewares show in Milan and walked into his first Italian espresso bar. He says it was like a black-and-white movie turning into color. Back in Seattle it took him two years to talk the founders into letting him try lattes in 100 square feet of the sixth store. A store that had seen two to three hundred customers a day reached 500 within a week. But Baldwin did not want to be in "the restaurant business," so Schultz left to start his own shop. By the hosts' count he pitched 242 investors for $1.6 million, and 217 said no.

When he pitched, he made sure to bring investors in when the store was busy. What he wanted them to see was not the drink itself:

It's not just the coffee. The coffee was the conduit.— Howard Schultz · [00:29:40]

Translated into a ledger, that romance becomes the numbers the hosts kept pressing on. Sourcing and roasting its own beans and selling them as beverages gave Starbucks roughly an 80% gross margin back then, Schultz says. Its most loyal customers in the Northwest came 18 times a month, a frequency no restaurant can touch. Every new store was held to first-year sales of twice the investment and an operating margin above 20%. That is why Schultz insisted on the IPO roadshow that Starbucks was not a restaurant but a store, and its people merchants.

Better still, it barely spent on advertising. Schultz says the company never spent a dollar on marketing; the hosts sum up his expansion as "everything is a billboard." Selling beans at Costco produced a measurable lift at nearby stores. A handshake with Pepsi over a napkin became a multi-billion-dollar bottled Frappuccino joint venture. Customers walking down the street with the cup were advertising too. Each move drew an internal revolt, and each one made the brand bigger than the company behind it.

2. Exceed your people's expectations so they exceed the customer's

Schultz traces his wish for "a different kind of company" to what his father went through. In 1988, at 33 stores, Starbucks offered health insurance to part-time employees. In 1991, a year before the IPO, he pushed through Bean Stock: stock options worth 14% of base pay for everyone, including part-timers working 20 hours a week. Two venture capitalists on the board initially fought it. He calls the day it was announced the cultural turning point; from then on, employees were "partners."

One host admits he grew impatient while preparing. He had heard too many feel-good stories: the store manager with AIDS whose medical bills the company kept paying, the health insurance for partners' parents in China. They sounded like one-off anecdotes, when the answer to why a company works should be in its business model. By the end he reversed himself: there are thousands of these stories, and they are the model. The hosts added the ledger view: keeping a trained employee is far cheaper than training a new one, the same lesson as Costco next door. Fittingly, Costco co-founder Jeff Brotman invested in the 1987 buyout. Schultz's own summary is that Starbucks is "a performance-driven company through the lens of humanity," and every time it lost its way, it was because the people in power forgot that equation.

The strongest evidence in this thread is China. Starbucks lost money there for nearly a decade, with steady pressure inside to pull out. Belinda Wong's condition for taking over was full decentralization: take Seattle out of the loop, because people who had never been to China were designing a breakfast menu around blueberry muffins. Schultz credits her with single-handedly building the business.

3. Getting smaller as you get bigger

The real tension of the episode is a puzzle Schultz set for himself:

The ubiquity of Starbucks was an enemy to the company, and the challenge was we have to figure out a way to ensure the fact that we are getting smaller as we're getting bigger.— Howard Schultz · [01:02:17]

His reasoning: scale breeds complexity, complexity demands efficiency, and the first thing efficiency shaves away is the moment between customer and barista. When he returned as CEO in 2008, he found that his predecessors had quietly reduced the amount of coffee per batch of brew to improve yield. Each such cut was harmless alone; together they thinned the experience. In New Orleans he gathered 10,000 store managers and, instead of big numbers, broke the turnaround down to roughly ten more customers per store per day:

If you reduce it to the lowest common denominator: one store, one cup of coffee, one customer, one partner.— Howard Schultz · [01:49:08]

The same logic turns on him with Mobile Order and Pay. The app first launched in 2009, on his watch, and the hosts spelled out its appeal: well over a billion dollars sitting as float on stored-value cards, and card payments that avoid interchange fees. Schultz calls it the company's weak spot:

It is the biggest Achilles heel for Starbucks.— Howard Schultz · [01:59:52]

His example is Chicago at 8 a.m.: trains arrive on the Loop, everyone's phone says "ready in seven minutes," everyone shows up at once, and the counter becomes a mosh pit. Asked what he would design differently, he is specific:

I don't think I would have allowed the mobile app to be on demand 24 hours a day.— Howard Schultz · [02:05:10]

One host pushed back on the spot: to him ubiquity is Starbucks' best feature, the same order anywhere in the world, the way you trust Chase or Apple. Schultz's answer is that reliability can come from scale, but intimacy can only come from each single store. They never quite settle it.

Near the end, Schultz turns the critique on himself. Dependence on the founder, he admits, covered up a lot of mistakes:

There's a burden that the organization has and a reliance on the founder that over time can become unhealthy.— Howard Schultz · [02:57:41]

His biggest regret, he says, is not believing Orin Smith when Smith kept saying he only wanted to be CEO for a couple of years, and not developing internal successors early. The prescription in his 2024 letter is the old one: Starbucks is not a beverage company serving coffee, but a coffee company serving people.

Fact-check

Schultz says that in 2008 he told store managers in New Orleans the company had seven months before it would be insolvent. [01:49:08]

Insufficient evidence

Starbucks' fiscal 2008 10-K still shows $316 million in net earnings and $1.26 billion in operating cash flow. The real squeeze was short-term funding: $270 million in cash against $713 million in commercial paper and revolver borrowings. "Seven months" would need an internal projection that was never made public.

Schultz says 18% of Starbucks' revenue today comes from China. [02:17:13]

Contradicted by the evidence

In the last quarterly report before the recording, China revenue was $706 million out of $8.6 billion, about 8%. The 18% is China's share of stores (7,093 of 38,951), which is what a host cited earlier. China has many stores with lower revenue per store; the two figures are not interchangeable.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Thundering herd: Chicago's morning rush is a textbook thundering herd. One clock (the train arriving) wakes every client, every client gets the same "seven minutes," and every request hits the same resource at the same instant. The engineering fixes are jitter and admission control; Schultz's "not on demand 24 hours a day" is rate limiting. The difference: a system throttles to protect throughput, while what he wants to protect is a few seconds of human contact at the counter.
  • Economics · Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Put a little less coffee in each batch and cost per cup improves while the unmeasured experience drains away. When a host asked whether values could get a KPI, Schultz said he hadn't been smart enough to figure that out, which is exactly the problem.
  • Sociology · Ray Oldenburg's "third place": the term was popularized by Oldenburg's 1989 book The Great Good Place, meaning public places beyond home and work where people linger. His examples were taverns, cafés and barbershops, and their core was conversation among regulars. Mobile ordering turns the store into a pickup counter, the opposite of his definition.
  • Botany · Apical dominance: the shoot tip releases auxin that suppresses the buds below it. That resembles Schultz's account of everyone "leaning on" the founder. The difference: cut the tip off a plant and side branches flourish, but when Starbucks' founder stepped away, the company went on defense.

Ten takeaways

  1. Howard Schultz's 1987 buyout of Starbucks nearly went to one of his own investors, until Bill Gates Sr. shut the rival bid down in one sentence.
  2. What Schultz took from Milan's espresso bars was community more than espresso; he calls coffee the conduit for human connection.
  3. Early Starbucks, roasting its own beans and selling beverages, ran at roughly an 80% gross margin, with its most loyal customers visiting 18 times a month.
  4. New Starbucks stores had to hit first-year sales of twice their investment and 20%+ operating margins, which is why Schultz called Starbucks a store, not a restaurant.
  5. Starbucks spent almost nothing on advertising, spreading its logo through Costco, United Airlines and bottled Frappuccino until the brand outgrew the company.
  6. The 1991 Bean Stock program gave every employee, part-timers included, options worth 14% of base pay, and employees became "partners."
  7. Starbucks lost money in China for nearly a decade until Belinda Wong insisted on moving decision-making from Seattle to China.
  8. Schultz argues that scale breeds complexity, complexity demands efficiency, and efficiency first erodes contact between customer and barista.
  9. Schultz calls Mobile Order and Pay Starbucks' biggest Achilles heel and says he would not have made it available on demand around the clock.
  10. The Chicago rush-hour mobile-order crush has the same structure as a thundering herd in distributed systems, and both are fixed by rate limiting.
