The Joe Rogan Experience · 2020

Brian Greene: the multiverse is as weird as a channeled sage, but the weirdness comes from somewhere else

Brian Greene × Joe Rogan · "Joe Rogan Experience #1428 - Brian Greene"

Show The Joe Rogan Experience · #1428 · 2020-02-19 · Length 147 min · Guest Brian Greene · Host Joe Rogan · Video YouTube

The moment

A couple of years before this episode, Brian Greene accepted a speaking gig. The fee looked good and he didn't vet it. A few days out he realized the host was JZ Knight's school, and Knight claims to channel Ramtha, a 35,000-year-old sage. The lecture agent told him it was too late to back out. [00:47:10]

First came the tour. Blindfolded people groped across a field for a card bearing their life's dream; find it, and the dream comes true. There were blindfolded archers. A woman predicted the next card in a shuffled deck, missed, and then claimed the following card was the seven she had meant. When Greene finally spoke, he told them plainly that what he'd seen was nuts: guess the suit and you'll be right one time in four on average. They gave him a standing ovation. He asked why they were applauding someone telling them they were wasting their time. They applauded again.

At the book signing, people confided that they knew a lot of it was silly, but they'd keep coming, because they wanted to be around others searching for a deeper truth. Greene said he felt a degree of sympathy for them. That sympathy runs through the whole conversation.

Who's talking, and when

Greene is a Columbia physicist who works on string theory and cosmology; he says he got his first faculty job at 27. His first teacher was his father, a vaudevillian who dropped out in tenth grade and toured with a harmonica act. He taught Brian arithmetic at five, then set him thirty-digit-by-thirty-digit multiplications that the boy worked through over whole weekends on sheets of construction paper. Greene is also candid about himself. On the widely repeated line that quantum mechanics accounts for about 35% of GDP, he says he went looking for the source:

"Apparently I'm the source of this number, and I assure you that I've not done a calculation that really fully justifies this."— Brian Greene · [00:29:15]

Joe Rogan is a comedian and UFC commentator whose show runs without an outline and drifts toward psychedelics, aliens and fitness. This one does too: the last twenty minutes turn into Rogan recommending treatments for Greene's herniated disc.

The episode went up as Greene's book Until the End of Time was hitting shelves. Unlike his earlier books on string theory and the multiverse, it runs from entropy and evolution through language, religion and art. He says its backbone is Ernest Becker's The Denial of Death: we know we will die, and much of what we do is a response to that. Greene scales it up to the cosmos, where even protons eventually decay.

Glossary

  • Entropy: roughly, disorder. Total entropy only rises, but local pockets can become more ordered by pushing more disorder into their surroundings; stars and living things are such pockets.

The episode, thread by thread

Before the Big Bang, and two kinds of weird

Rogan asks what came before the Big Bang. Greene offers two answers. Maybe a larger realm has hosted countless big bangs and ours wasn't the first event. Or maybe the question has no meaning. He borrows Hawking's image: anywhere on Earth you can ask which way is further north, but at the North Pole the question dissolves, because that is where north begins. Time may have such a starting point. He also suggests time might be emergent, like temperature: a single molecule is neither hot nor cold, and perhaps time only exists when matter is arranged in certain ways.

For the "bang" itself, Greene turns to inflation. Einstein's equations allow gravity to repel: energy spread uniformly through space pushes outward. The early universe was like a pot of boiling water; wait long enough and some small patch happens to go calm, and that patch swells from sub-atomic size to larger than the observable universe in around 10-35 seconds. This can happen again and again elsewhere, and each bubble can have different physics. Push the math far enough and there must be exact copies of us somewhere, perhaps a "Joe Greene and Brian Rogan."

"This is not an overworked theorist's imagination, this is the careful dispassionate analysis of the mathematical equations."— Brian Greene · [00:43:54]

He concedes that some physicists think a theory predicting endless universes we can never touch has thereby imploded. Rogan half-jokingly asks how channeling an ancient sage could be any weirder than the Big Bang. Greene's answer: however strange the Big Bang is, he can follow the deductive chain there with mathematics he understands. For a 35,000-year-old sage he can't even name a physical process, and it doesn't fit the archaeological record.

"There's a vital distinction between weirdness that emerges from careful mathematical analysis and weirdness that emerges from an overworked imagination that possibly sees a business model."— Brian Greene · [00:54:47]

That distinction is the pivot of the episode, and it has a soft spot: the copies are deduced, but the premises of the deduction haven't been tested. The claim check below takes this up.

Consciousness is just particles, and one night in Amsterdam

Rogan asks whether consciousness is a product of brain tissue or whether the brain tunes into it like a radio. Greene picks the plainest option:

"Consciousness is nothing more than the choreographed motion of particles in various quantum states inside a gloppy gray structure that sits inside this thing that we call a head."— Brian Greene · [01:07:25]

He immediately adds that he has no proof, and neither does anyone else. His reason is historical: people in the 1800s also thought lifeless particles couldn't add up to life without an injected "life force," and no serious scientist believes that now. He expects consciousness to go the same way, and he rejects panpsychism, the idea that electrons carry a sliver of experience: a physicist should take the smallest step needed to explain the phenomena, and conscious quarks are far too big a step.

Rogan pushes back with a decent argument. Eyes need light to see what is already there; maybe psychedelics, including DMT, which the body itself makes, let the brain perceive what it normally can't. Greene doesn't dismantle the analogy. He tells a story instead. He says he was in the Netherlands to give a lecture to the queen, and afterwards he and his wife visited an Amsterdam coffee shop. The beginner option did nothing the first night, so the second night he went straight to the bottom of the menu.

"My brain started manufacturing versions of myself that would converse with me and convinced me that the reality that I was experiencing was real, and then that version of me would destroy that reality."— Brian Greene · [01:17:16]

He clung to the hotel bed and asked his wife to tie him up; on the flight home the next day he listened to a Beatles channel for seven hours. His conclusion: the experience made it intuitively obvious that his awareness depends on a few chemicals. Notice that the two men read the same fact in opposite ways. Rogan sees chemicals opening a door; Greene sees chemicals as the room itself. That night can't settle who is right. It just let Greene's intuition catch up with what he already believed.

A physicist makes room for religion

Greene once attended a closed-door meeting called "Science and the Spiritual Quest." He expected a roomful of scientists to say one thing: there could be a God, there's no evidence, so we do physics. He turned out to be the only one in the room who thought that. A Nobel laureate sang Psalms during his talk and afterwards put an arm around Greene and told him he was smart but didn't yet understand the true reality, and eventually would. That sent Greene to William James's The Varieties of Religious Experience, which, he says, didn't change his view on whether God exists but did change his view of what religion does for people.

His account of religion is evolutionary. The brain runs agency detection: mistake a wind-blown branch for a jaguar and you've lost nothing; mistake a jaguar for a branch and you get eaten. So we over-assign minds to the world, a mind in the wind, a mind in the gurgling river, and that is the seed of many religions.

Yet he doesn't stand on the cold side himself. When he was 23 his father died suddenly. His father wasn't religious, but the family knew he'd want a religious service, so they gathered a minyan, the quorum of ten Jewish men, to recite the Kaddish at the house.

"I had no idea what these men were saying, but it was deeply comforting. In fact, I didn't want to know what they were saying."— Brian Greene · [02:05:07]

He admits he knocks on wood and talks to his dead father. He also floats a bolder thought experiment: pit a race of purely rational Vulcans against crazy humans who invent gods and demons, and the humans win, because freedom of thought breeds novelty and creativity. The thread lands on his "many stories" view: physics, chemistry, biology and psychology each tell one layer, and religion and art are layers too. The trouble starts only when religion competes with science over facts.

Claim check

Greene says inflation is the picture most physicists accept, and that followed through, the universe must contain exact copies of us, as a matter of "dispassionate analysis of the mathematical equations." [00:35:36]

Supported, but overstated

Inflation is mainstream: in 2017, 33 physicists including Guth and Hawking called it the dominant paradigm. But copies need two further premises, eternal inflation and infinite space (Tegmark puts the nearest copy about 101029 meters away). That is exactly the layer Ijjas, Steinhardt and Loeb attack: infinitely many outcomes means no predictions. The reasoning is rigorous; the premises are untested.

Greene says that even if beings could keep thinking with the particles left after protons decay, rising entropy lets you "pretty much establish" that thought will end in this universe. [01:32:46]

Supported, but overstated

Krauss and Starkman argued in 2000 that if cosmic acceleration is driven by a cosmological constant and consciousness is physical computation, life cannot be eternal. But Freese and Kinney found that under some other dark-energy models life can persist indefinitely. The conclusion hangs on what dark energy is, which is still open.

Rogan recommends Regenokine (autologous conditioned serum: blood drawn, processed and injected back at the site) and says cortisone only gives temporary relief, while "what this does is actually heals the area." [02:22:08]

Insufficient evidence

The most-cited double-blind trial (Becker et al., 2007) had 84 patients with lumbar nerve-root compression. Pain fell clearly in both the serum and steroid groups; serum was significantly better only at week 22 and only against the low-dose steroid. It measured pain scores, not tissue healing, and a 2024 systematic review found just six studies. Pain relief: supported. "Heals": no evidence.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Lamport's logical clocks: Greene guesses time emerges from something more basic. Lamport's 1978 paper says a distributed system has no global clock; "before" can only be built from causal relations between events. The difference: Lamport's time is engineered, while emergent time in physics is still a conjecture.
  • Physics of computation · Landauer's principle: Greene's thinker frying in its own waste heat runs on the same ledger: erasing one bit costs at least kT ln 2 of heat dumped into the environment. But Bennett showed computation can in principle be reversible, and no erasure means no heat bill; Dyson in 1979 imagined life thinking ever more slowly and hibernating to save heat. How long thought lasts depends on whether that bill can be deferred forever.
  • Philosophy · Parfit on personal identity: "Infinitely many yous" gave both men pause: if someone elsewhere has your exact memories, which one is you? In Reasons and Persons Parfit used the teletransporter to argue that identity isn't what matters; psychological continuity is. The multiverse turns his thought experiment into a cosmological corollary.
  • AI · Agents trained in dreams: Greene says storytelling is the brain rehearsing reality in safety. Ha and Schmidhuber's 2018 World Models trained an agent inside its own learned model of the environment, then returned it to the real one. The difference: human stories are shared, and, as Rogan notes, they can leave unrealistic expectations.

Ten takeaways

  1. Brian Greene's Until the End of Time builds on Becker's The Denial of Death, scaling "we know we will die" up to the whole cosmos.
  2. Greene admits the popular claim that quantum mechanics drives 35% of GDP traces back to him, with no rigorous calculation behind it.
  3. Greene says "before the Big Bang" is either more big bangs or a meaningless question, like asking what lies north of the North Pole.
  4. Greene compares time to temperature: one molecule is neither hot nor cold, and time may likewise emerge only from certain arrangements of matter.
  5. Inflation says uniform energy produces repulsive gravity, letting a calm patch swell beyond the observable universe in an instant.
  6. Greene separates weirdness deduced from mathematics, like the multiverse, from weirdness invented by imagination and business models, like the channeled sage Ramtha.
  7. Greene holds that consciousness is only particles moving in the brain, and that its mystery will fade like the nineteenth-century "life force."
  8. Rogan likens psychedelics to light that reveals hidden things; Greene treats his terrifying Amsterdam night as evidence that consciousness depends on chemistry.
  9. Greene explains religion's origin by agency detection: mistaking a branch for a jaguar is harmless, the reverse is fatal, so humans see minds everywhere.
  10. Lamport's logical clocks and Greene's emergent time share one intuition: "before" is not a backdrop but something built from relations between events.
