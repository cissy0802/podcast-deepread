The Diary of a CEO · 2023

The Boss's Stress, the Assistant's Waistline

Tara Swart × Steven Bartlett · "Leading Neuroscientist: Stress Leaks Through Skin, Is Contagious, Gives You Belly Fat!"

Show The Diary of a CEO · 2023-09-25 · Length 125 min · Guest Tara Swart · Host Steven Bartlett · Watch YouTube

The moment

Tara Swart was giving a talk at a bank. Her point was that a leader's stress leaks down through the organisation, and that stress makes people store belly fat they cannot shift. The CEO's personal assistant was in the audience, and she called out: "So he's the reason I'm fat?" Nobody laughed, Swart says [00:18:46]. Bartlett asks whether they were scared. No, she says: it wasn't a joke, it was the truth.

It is a story that tells itself: stress is contagious, the source is the boss, and the evidence sits on your waist. The episode's title comes straight from it. But each link in the chain, from stress leaking out through the skin, to it entering someone else's skin, to fat that will not budge, deserves a look on its own.

Who's talking, and when

Swart trained in medicine, did a PhD in neuroscience, worked as a junior doctor and psychiatrist in the NHS, and then moved into advising bank executives. In those years, she says, traders were dropping dead of heart attacks on the floor, and the bank wanted her to deal with the physical side as a former doctor. Her answer was that you cannot fix the body without addressing the mental and emotional side first. Near the end she tells an old story: an English teacher said she was so good at drama she should read English at Oxford and go to RADA. Her father said "over my dead body" and sent her to medical school. She says she still wants to feel like a truly creative person.

Steven Bartlett hosts the show and is an investor on Dragons' Den. He habitually pulls the conversation back to his own life (his girlfriend, learning Portuguese, learning to DJ) and often asks "is that true?" He casts himself as the evidence guy:

If there's even a slither of science that could justify it, then I come around to ideas, but I do need the science.— Steven Bartlett · [00:35:05]

The episode was recorded in September 2023. Swart's own podcast was about to launch its second season on October 4th; she says she started reading about spirituality and ancient cultures during the pandemic, and the new season leans on indigenous wisdom. That thread runs quietly through the conversation and nearly takes over in the second half, when the talk turns to manifestation.

The episode, worked through

1. "Stress leaks": a causal chain held together by cave stories

When Bartlett asks whether stress is contagious, Swart takes a detour through menstrual synchrony first: women who live together or sit next to each other, she says, synchronise their periods within two or three months. Her explanation is set in the cave. The men went hunting for months at a time, so the "alpha male" needed at least five women pregnant at once, so that if famine or miscarriage struck, at least one child would survive. That required the women to be fertile together. Oestrogen and progesterone leak out in sweat "about this far" and pass into another woman's skin. The synchrony, she adds, is led by the "alpha female", through testosterone levels.

Tellingly, she explains where this style of reasoning comes from:

Whenever I want to explain something that's complex or I don't actually know the current neuroscience, I always take it back to what happened in ancient times.— Tara Swart · [00:12:55]

Only then does she get to the point. Cortisol, the main stress hormone, leaks the same way, and here sex does not matter but hierarchy does. The person who walks out of a meeting feeling drained is usually the more junior one, so a CEO's stress affects the company more than everyone else's combined. Some CEOs and CFOs tell her they simply won't let it show: they won't share the numbers, won't display emotion. Her reply:

They're still going to know physiologically. It's going to impact them.— Tara Swart · [00:17:46]

Then the belly. Cortisol, she says, is a survival mechanism that stores fat around the abdomen so a cave-dweller could last through a month without food. Her clients in finance took to lifting their T-shirts when she walked in: now you know how my month went. Her remedies are aerobic exercise, to literally sweat out excess cortisol, and journaling or talking it out.

Every link in the chain comes with a cave story underneath. Bartlett's reaction is the most revealing part. Years ago, he says, he heard about menstrual synchrony, checked it, found it was true, and it broke his "if I can't see it, it doesn't exist" frame. The host who needs the science is most completely won over at precisely the point where the evidence is thinnest, as the fact-check below shows.

2. The brain can change, but only if you push hard enough

The second thread is the most textbook part of the episode. When she was at medical school, Swart says, everyone assumed the brain was fixed once you stopped growing. Now we know it keeps actively changing until about 25; between 25 and 65 it plateaus unless you do something about it. Change takes something intense, like learning a language, and the payoff goes beyond the language itself to what she calls global benefits: better emotional regulation, complex problem-solving, flexible thinking. Bartlett happens to have promised his girlfriend he would learn Portuguese by year's end. It is September and he knows six words.

He asks the obvious follow-up: I interview new people and learn new things every day; doesn't that count? Swart does not soften it:

But it's probably not what we would call attention intense enough to actually be shifting pathways in your brain.— Tara Swart · [01:02:13]

So how do you break a bad habit? She offers three steps. First, spot the pattern; that alone is half the battle. Second, focused attention: go back through every past instance and connect each decision with its consequence. Third, deliberate practice. Her own example is the voice in her head during podcasts that wonders what the host is really after: hold it off half the time this week, three-quarters next week, until the new pathway outgrows the old one. On top of the three steps you need someone to hold you accountable, because most people give up at step three.

3. From plasticity to manifestation

The third thread grows out of the second, but somewhere along the way it changes foundations. As an example of mind over matter, Swart describes a weightlifting experiment: one group trained for two weeks, the other only sat and imagined lifting, and the imagining group gained 13% in muscle mass. Bartlett jokes that he could just watch the football and imagine lifting. She says the visualisation and focused intention are what matter, and that athletes use visualisation all the time.

The examples get bolder. A group of people in their eighties spend a week in houses arranged as they were twenty years earlier and come out standing taller and looking younger. Her optician told her she would need reading glasses within a year; she refused, kept her phone where it was instead of holding it further away, and a year later her eyes had improved. She also describes an experiment in which, as she tells it, medical students who saw words like "Florida" and "bungalow", associated with retirement, walked more slowly. The original is Bargh and colleagues' 1996 study of New York University students. In 2012 Doyen and colleagues reran it with automated timing and a larger sample and failed to replicate it; participants walked slower only when the experimenters expected them to.

On affirmations the two actually agree. Bartlett says that if he stood in front of a mirror saying "I'm going to be rich", his brain would know he was kidding himself. Swart agrees those lines are shallow and prefers telling yourself "I'm safe". As for manifestation, she says she spent a summer researching the law of attraction and found it could all be explained by cognitive science:

it's not magically like attracting something in the atmosphere. It's to do with the changes that you make based on your thought process.— Tara Swart · [01:37:59]

Hence she calls vision boards "action boards". If you want an ideal partner, list the qualities you want, then make sure you have all of them yourself. That part is practical. But when Bartlett asks for the very first step, her answer is to spend five minutes a day visualising your goals as already true, seeing it, feeling it in your body, tasting it, and then being grateful for it:

Essentially, what you're doing is moving your brain from a fear state to a trust state, and that is the gateway to making these changes.— Tara Swart · [01:58:18]

The bridge from "repetition rewires pathways" to "imagine it true and give thanks" is built from the weightlifting and ageing experiments. How sound that bridge is decides how far this thread can carry you.

Fact-check

"Cortisol, like the sex hormones, leaks out in sweat, enters the skin of the people around you, and affects them." [00:16:14]

Insufficient evidence

The analogy she builds on does not hold: Yang and Schank followed 186 women living in dorms for over a year and found no synchrony, and their reanalysis of the original study put its synchrony at chance level. Stress contagion itself is real: in Engert and colleagues' study, 26% of 211 observers watching someone get stressed through a one-way mirror showed a significant cortisol rise. But the channel was the eyes. We found no study showing cortisol passing from one person's sweat into another's body.

"As long as you're still leaking out extra cortisol, eating less or exercising more won't shift the belly fat." [00:18:46]

Supported, but overstated

The link exists: a meta-analysis of 146 cohorts and 34,000 people found that higher hair cortisol goes with a larger waist. But the correlations are only 0.1 to 0.2, the data are cross-sectional, and the authors suggest abdominal fat may itself shift the stress axis. "Exercise won't shift it" gets it backwards: a pooled analysis of 15 trials and 852 people found aerobic exercise alone, without dieting, reduced visceral fat within 12 weeks.

"They just sat there and visualised lifting weights, and gained 13% in muscle mass." [01:27:48]

Supported, but overstated

She did not name the study, but the matching figure comes from Ranganathan and colleagues (2004): groups of eight young adults trained with imagined contractions for 12 weeks, not two. Elbow-flexion strength rose 13.5% and little-finger abduction 35% (53% in the group that physically trained). They measured strength, not muscle mass, and credited the gains to a stronger drive signal from the brain. Imagery can train a brain that recruits muscle better; there is no evidence it builds muscle.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · spandrels: Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco" took aim at the habit of inventing an adaptive story for every trait. Swart openly reaches for the cave when she does not know the current neuroscience. A cave story can explain almost any outcome, which is exactly why it cannot serve as evidence.
  • Physics · beats and clock drift: two unconnected clocks with slightly different periods will now and then appear aligned, then drift apart. That is Yang and Schank's account of perceived synchrony: cycle lengths vary, so onsets naturally converge and diverge. Anyone who runs distributed systems knows the pattern: two machines without NTP occasionally agreeing on the time are not synchronised. Where the analogy breaks: truly coupled oscillators do synchronise, as Huygens saw in 1665 with two pendulum clocks on the same beam. To prove synchrony, you first have to find the beam.
  • Reinforcement learning · Dyna: Sutton's Dyna architecture lets an agent update its policy on experience imagined by a model, without acting out every step for real. Imagery training gains strength the same way: it trains the controller, not the actuators. Dyna does not make a robot's motors bigger either.
  • Buddhism · mettā: Swart recommends telling yourself "I'm safe", and modern loving-kindness practice uses phrases like "may I be safe". The difference is the mood: mettā is a wish that does not pretend the thing is already true, while Swart's five-minute exercise asks you to treat the wish as already fulfilled and give thanks for it.

Ten takeaways

  1. Tara Swart argues that a leader's stress flows down the hierarchy, with more junior people affected the most.
  2. Swart uses menstrual synchrony as an analogy for stress contagion and explains it with the cave-era breeding needs of an "alpha male".
  3. Swart says that when something is complex or she does not know the current neuroscience, she goes back to ancient times for an explanation.
  4. Swart says cortisol makes the body store fat around the abdomen, and recommends aerobic exercise and journaling as outlets for stress.
  5. Swart holds that the brain plateaus after 25 and changes only under sufficiently intense new challenges, such as learning a language.
  6. Swart tells Steven Bartlett that hosting a podcast every day is a well-worn path for him, not intense enough to rewire his brain.
  7. Swart's method for changing a habit has three steps (spot the pattern, link decisions to consequences, practise deliberately) plus someone to hold you accountable.
  8. Swart reframes manifestation as thoughts driving actions, and renames vision boards "action boards".
  9. The Dyna architecture in reinforcement learning updates a policy on experience imagined by a model, so the agent need not act out every step.
  10. Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique is a reminder that an adaptive story able to explain everything cannot serve as evidence.
