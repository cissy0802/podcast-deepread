Before you read him, apply four discounts

Era, seat, company, motive: where his advice is spoken from

Topic Before reading him: four discounts · Articles 18 free Level Up posts read, 11 cited (2023–2026) · Interviews The Peterman Pod, two episodes (Aug 2025, Sep 2026) · Video first · third

Four discounts

The next seven posts in this series cover how Ethan Evans teaches people to get promoted, handle their bosses and survive inside organizations. This one answers a single question first: where is he standing when he says it? Every piece of advice carries its speaker's era, chair, company and business. Know those four and you know how much to discount. His résumé appears here only as evidence.

Start with the timeline he has laid out himself (from 2024's “My path to Amazon Vice President”). Engineer to big-company VP took him twenty years, which he calls "about average."

1. Era: a company that grew a hundredfold

When Evans joined Amazon in 2005, he worried he was joining "the IT department of a bookstore." There was no AWS, no Kindle, no Prime Video. Offered two openings, he took the online video one, which grew into Prime Video. In his third interview he concedes that his pick left another manager, starting the following week, without the video job he had been promised.

The paperwork he saw on making Director said a Director should be one of the 100 most important leaders at Amazon, though by then there were already well over a hundred. For VP, his boss stayed put for two and a half to three years while they worked the process, then was reorganized out six months after Evans got promoted. [00:23:43] Six months later, he says, and he would have been starting over.

In that setting he arrived at the rule he most wants readers to follow:

Big wins and big losses work better than consistent middle performance.— Ethan Evans · “My path to Amazon Vice President,” 2024

The rule rests on two premises: losses are cheap, and new seats keep appearing. He sees this himself. Asked in 2026 how he would speedrun from Amazon's entry level to VP today, he starts by saying don't count on it at Amazon:

There is no speedrun of that today.— Ethan Evans · [03:03:32]

His prescription for today: learn at Amazon for 18 to 24 months, build a skill outsiders will pay for (AWS, say), jump to a high-growth company, take big swings, and if one fails, leave "the smoking ruins" behind and try again elsewhere. That prescription itself assumes a job market willing to catch job-hoppers. Discount one: for any advice about speed and risk, ask whether it only pays during hypergrowth.

2. Seat: the chair that decides who rises and who goes

His most revealing material comes from the VP chair, the side that decides who gets promoted and who leaves. He is frank about that side: with two roughly equal people, one threatening to leave and one waiting quietly, he often promoted the one agitating, and says nearly every manager does the same without admitting it. [00:20:09] He also says managers do not want to hold people back; a VP wants as many Directors as possible. When people ask why they were not promoted, his answer is:

You did not promote yourself.— Ethan Evans · [00:25:26]

From the chair that is true; from below, the cost of the same situation lands on someone else. Early at Amazon he promised a star engineer a promotion, did not know the company only ran two cycles a year, missed the window, and the engineer left, a year behind. In 2011 the Appstore feature he owned failed at launch in front of Bezos; he survived two years of doubt and made VP, while the young engineer who wrote the code quit. In 2023's “My Jeff Bezos Story” he wrote:

We failed him, not the other way around, and I do not think I made that clear enough to him at the time.— Ethan Evans · “My Jeff Bezos Story,” 2023

Beyond the chair there is a larger seat. In 2023's “Being "unreasonable" drove me to Amazon VP” he champions leading with a proposal and pushing people along, then immediately concedes:

I'm a highly educated white male in tech. For a minority woman, it might totally fail.— Ethan Evans · “Being "unreasonable" drove me to Amazon VP,” 2023

Asked whether he ever burned someone to get ahead, he says he is wary of answering no, because people are good at rationalizing (the third episode deepread covers this). Discount two: advice about "what managers really think" is the decider's view; supply the cost on the receiving side yourself.

3. Company: Amazon's rules are not laws of nature

Almost all of Evans's big-company experience is from one company. Before Amazon he worked at startups of a dozen to a few dozen people, where promoting someone meant a word with your boss and HR; at Amazon he found a fixed process, two cycles a year, and written documents. His Magic Loop (ask your manager what they need, deliver it, and ask them to make sure you are rewarded) was honed inside exactly that kind of system, with cycles, documents and a manager who can speak for you at the review.

He even helped edit the company's rules. In 2011, when a revised draft of Amazon's Leadership Principles (LPs, the dozen-plus behavioral tenets Amazon uses in interviews, reviews and promotion documents) dropped Ownership, he helped draft its restoration and supplied the line "An owner never says that's not my job." A 2024 conversation on the LPs with his former report Dave Anderson summed up "the Amazon deal": as long as you do not fail, explode or say no, the company keeps giving you more; at some companies the manager decides how full your plate is, while at Amazon that is your job.

"Take everything" is a survival rule at Amazon, yet even there it tripped him. In “Struggles scaling as a Director at Amazon” he recalls that as a Director chasing VP he ran game tech, software downloads, the first Appstore, even bulk printer-paper sales to businesses, a charter he now calls "crazy," and the Appstore failure happened in that stretch. He also argues that non-Amazonians can learn from the LPs, which work like software design patterns, a shared shorthand. Discount three: for tactics that depend on review cycles, promotion documents or LP vocabulary, first ask what the equivalent machinery is at your company.

4. Motive: this is now how he makes a living

He recalled in 2026 that when he left Amazon at 50 in 2020, he genuinely meant to retire. He started writing on LinkedIn, people asked for résumé reviews, then one-on-one coaching, demand outgrew him, and he turned the common questions into classes. Today it is a full business: a paid newsletter, live and on-demand courses, "Ethan Evans AI," sponsorships. Nearly every free career-story post ends in a course ad, and résumé numbers like "promoted 8 Senior Managers to Director" double as course credentials.

His brand is saying what others won't, and that brand rewards certainty and edge. He has demonstrated how well certainty sells: in his third interview he coined a figure on the spot, that statements with numbers are 62% more believable, then admitted he had made it up (the third episode deepread covers it). The "80% !!" under Limits below is the same register.

Yet he does not only speak along the grain of his business. In 2025 he broke his own rule and wrote about his political beliefs, naming his incentive up front:

As a writer and teacher on LinkedIn and Substack, the most effective and self-serving thing I can do is remain silent on my beliefs.— Ethan Evans · “My Beliefs and Why a Leader’s Beliefs Matter,” 2025

One easily missed detail: his newsletter runs many guest posts. “Why I Left Amazon at My Peak,” on this series' reading list, was actually written by former AWS architect Rajdeep Saha; Evans appears in it only to say "You should leave." Discount four: trace the numbers, discount the tone, and check who actually wrote it.

How the discounts fit together

The four discounts do not apply evenly; each has its own target. Era hits advice about speed and risk; seat hits "what managers really think"; company hits specific mechanisms; motive hits tone and numbers. Advice that leans on several at once compounds them: "take more risk, and if you lose, switch companies" depends on hypergrowth, the VP's view and a confident, sellable voice all at once.

This also sets up how to read the next seven posts. "Promotion mechanics" and "Stuck at L7" (L7 is Amazon's Senior Manager level) lean mostly on era and company; "Managing up" and "How organizations really run" lean on seat; "Amazon and Bezos" sits entirely inside the company discount. "Career and life" needs a discount besides motive, and he states it plainly himself:

However, the opportunity to “wander” my way to a successful business model came from my unique situation. Most people do not have that.— Ethan Evans · “11 Questions You Must Answer Before Leaving Your Corporate Job,” 2026

Plenty remains after the discounts. He writes about his failures, regrets and blind spots more than most in his genre. Discounting him does not mean disbelieving him; it means knowing how far to trust each sentence.

Limits

"Amazon grew from 14,000 people to 1.4M people while I was there." — “My path to Amazon Vice President,” 2024; repeated in the third interview

Supported

Amazon's annual reports give about 12,000 employees at the end of 2005 and about 1,298,000 at the end of 2020. Both ends are a bit lower than he says, but "grew 100x" is right. What matters is his own inference: it will not happen again. The "about average" pace he reports was measured in a company constantly creating new seats, and it does not transfer directly to big tech today.

"Most jobs (80% !!) come through networking connections." — “Layoffs will continue all year,” 2026

Supported, but overstated

Contacts being the top job channel has primary evidence. Granovetter's classic 1973 paper surveyed professional, technical and managerial job changers in a Boston suburb; of the 100 interviewed in person, 54 found their new job through a personal contact, more than any other channel. That is just over half, not 80 percent, and the widely repeated 80 percent figure traces to no original study.

Crossovers

  • Statistics · Abraham Wald and survivorship bias: In WWII Wald pointed out that bullet holes on returning planes only showed where a plane could be hit and still fly home; armor belonged where there were no holes. "Big wins and big losses beat the middle" comes from someone whose bets paid off; people knocked out by one big loss do not write newsletters. Where it differs: Evans does not hide his losses, from two layoffs to a failure in front of Bezos. But every loss he took was one he could recover from, which is itself what puts him among the survivors.
  • Economics · the Lucas critique: In 1976 Robert Lucas argued that relationships fitted to data from one policy regime can break when the regime changes, because behavior adapts to the rules. Evans's "average promotion pace" was fitted inside a hundredfold-growth regime; saying there is no speedrun today is him applying the Lucas critique to himself.
  • Social psychology · actor–observer asymmetry: Jones and Nisbett (1971) proposed that people explain their own behavior by circumstances and others' by disposition. "You did not promote yourself," said from the VP chair, is the observer's attribution; the person who is stuck sees cycles, headcount and a boss who just changed.
  • Behavioral economics · disclosing conflicts of interest: Cain, Loewenstein and Moore (2005) found in experiments that advisors who disclosed a conflict of interest gave more biased advice, and their audiences did not discount enough. Evans puts his course ads in plain view, which makes readers feel he is transparent; transparency is not the same as advice that has been corrected.
  • Sociology · Merton's Matthew effect: In 1968 Robert Merton showed that already-eminent scientists receive more credit for the same work. Evans's high-potential list has the same shape: the favored get resources, grow faster, and so confirm they were favored correctly.

Ten takeaways

  1. Before taking Ethan Evans's career advice, apply four discounts: era, seat, company and motive.
  2. Evans's fifteen years at Amazon coincided with the company growing from about ten thousand employees to over a million, with new seats constantly opening.
  3. Evans's rule that big wins and big losses beat steady middling performance depends on losses being affordable and opportunities being plentiful.
  4. Evans himself says there is no longer a speedrun from Amazon's entry level to VP, and advises learning for 18 to 24 months, then joining a high-growth company.
  5. Evans's most revealing promotion insights come from the VP chair; readers must supply the costs borne by the people being decided about.
  6. Evans admits his push-your-proposal-first style might totally fail for a minority woman.
  7. Evans's Magic Loop and his fluency in the Leadership Principles were honed inside Amazon's twice-yearly, document-driven promotion system.
  8. Evans now earns his living from promotion courses and executive coaching, and a "straight truth" brand rewards confident tone and eye-catching numbers.
  9. Evans's newsletter carries many guest posts; "Why I Left Amazon at My Peak" was written by Rajdeep Saha.
  10. By the logic of the Lucas critique, promotion rules learned during hundredfold growth may stop working once the environment changes.
