Huberman Lab · 2021

Huberman on dopamine: pleasure is relative, and the higher the peak, the deeper the trough

Andrew Huberman, solo · Controlling Your Dopamine For Motivation, Focus & Satisfaction

Show Huberman Lab · solo episode · 2021-09-27 · Length 137 min · Speaker Andrew Huberman · Video YouTube

The moment

In college, Huberman caught giardia. Violent diarrhea, severe dehydration, a trip to the emergency room. He begged for something to stop up his gut, and a nurse injected something into his saline bag. Within minutes he fell into the lowest mood of his life, crying without knowing why.

"Within minutes I felt more sadness, more overwhelming sense of depression, basically lower than I'd ever felt in my entire life."— Andrew Huberman · [00:33:00]

He asked what it was. Thorazine (chlorpromazine), an antipsychotic that blocks dopamine receptors. He demanded L-DOPA, the precursor to dopamine. It went into the bag, and within minutes he felt fine again.

That one-two of injections is the most vivid piece of evidence in more than two hours of lecture: dopamine is not just the "feel-good" chemical. Take it away and the will to engage with life goes with it. The rest of the episode is about how to look after that system.

Who's talking, and when

Andrew Huberman is a professor of neurobiology and ophthalmology at the Stanford School of Medicine; his own research is on the visual system. This is a solo lecture, no guest. Midway through he holds up the Pilot V5 pen in his hand and says that if he talked about how much he loves it for long enough, he would probably get a dopamine bump. The aside lands on a real point of the episode: how you appraise something changes how rewarding it is.

The show is known for handing out protocols, and this one does too: how cold the water, how many milligrams, whether the phone comes to the gym. What is different is that the first hour is almost entirely mechanism; the protocols come late.

It aired in September 2021, in the show's first year. Shortly before, he had hosted Stanford addiction psychiatrist Anna Lembke, whose book Dopamine Nation came out that summer, just as "dopamine" was becoming a pop-culture catchword. Much of this episode is a response to that catchword.

Glossary

  • Tonic vs. phasic release: the steady low-level background versus the brief spikes when something happens. Huberman calls them "baseline" and "peaks."
  • Mesolimbic pathway: from the ventral tegmental area at the floor of the brain to the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex; it carries motivation and craving. The other pathway, nigrostriatal, is for movement, and is the one that degenerates in Parkinson's.
  • Microdialysis: a thin probe placed in an animal's brain that samples local fluid so dopamine can be measured directly.

The episode, worked through

1. Dopamine is a relative currency

Early on, Huberman takes aim at a popular phrase.

"Actually, there's no such thing as a dopamine hit."— Andrew Huberman · [00:08:52]

He does not mean dopamine never rises. He means the phrase leaves out the reference point. How exciting something feels depends not on how high the peak is, but on how far it rises above where your baseline was at that moment. His example is a social feed: one post you love makes the next one feel flat, yet if you met that second post three days later you might find it fascinating.

Then comes the line he flags as the one to remember if you remember nothing else:

"When you experience something or you crave something really desirable, really exciting to you, very pleasurable, what happens afterwards is your baseline level of dopamine drops."— Andrew Huberman · [00:10:24]

He gives two reasons. Evolutionarily, dopamine has to fall after you find the berries, or our ancestors would have stayed put, satisfied, and stopped foraging. Mechanistically, he points to the "readily releasable pool": the pre-packaged vesicles of dopamine at the terminals are finite, a big release empties them, and the baseline recovers only as synthesis restocks them. The drop, he says, is proportional to the height of the peak. Cross the finish line merely happy and you will barely notice the next day; cross it ecstatic and a day or two later you will feel noticeably lower.

The most useful consequence of this model is his "work hard, play hard" person: swimming a couple of miles midweek, eating especially delicious food midweek, drinking on two weekend nights, dancing once. None of it looks excessive on its own.

"Dopamine is one currency of craving, motivation and desire and pleasure. There's only one currency."— Andrew Huberman · [01:02:19]

Every one of those activities draws on the same account. Over a few years the baseline sags, and the person starts calling it burnout. Push it to the extreme and you get addiction.

"I always say addiction is a progressive narrowing of the things that bring you pleasure."— Andrew Huberman · [01:00:43]

He tells of a friend's child, hooked on video games and being treated for ADHD, who cut out phone, games and social media for thirty days and got his concentration and mood back. From this he says he thinks a lot of ADHD is misdiagnosed. That is his view; the episode offers no data for it.

2. Don't stack your dopamine

If higher peaks mean deeper troughs, the most common mistake is stacking. Huberman describes a familiar gym routine: your favorite coffee, then a pre-workout drink, then your favorite music in the headphones, with texting and scrolling in between. Each makes the workout better, but the combined peak also raises the bar for enjoying the next workout.

He uses himself as the example. He has always loved training, but in recent years he noticed that with his phone in the gym he started to lose interest, and even began to question his motivation. So he made a rule for that year: no phone in his workouts. The first week, he says, was hard; now he enjoys training more than before.

His fix is borrowed from casinos. What keeps gamblers coming back is an unpredictable, intermittent reward: sometimes a lot, sometimes a little, sometimes nothing. He wants you to import that uncertainty into your own life, and to make it genuinely random. Skipping music every third session is a predictable rule, not an intermittent one. Hence his tool: flip a coin before the gym; heads, music comes along, tails, it doesn't.

By the same logic he warns against using stimulants every time you study or train, from pre-workouts all the way to Adderall and modafinil. His one exception is caffeine, because, he says, caffeine upregulates dopamine receptors. The fact-check below shows that this rests on narrower evidence than he suggests.

3. Make the effort itself the reward

The last thread is the most ambitious. Huberman describes an old Stanford experiment: preschoolers who already loved drawing were rewarded for drawing, and once the rewards stopped, they drew less on their own. His explanation is that the dopamine peak from the reward lowers the baseline, and the brain reinterprets the activity: I drew for the prize, not because I liked it.

He adds a second layer: dopamine helps carve up our perception of time. Fixate on the prize at the end and the whole effort gets lumped into one long "time bin," with all the reward crammed at the finish. The process grows joyless, and next time you need twice the coffee and three times the music just to get out the door for a run. His answer is to bring in the prefrontal cortex: in the moment of greatest strain, tell yourself that this hard part is the good part, and that you are doing it by choice. He concedes how that sounds.

"I know that sounds like lying to yourself, and in some ways it is lying to yourself, but it's lying to yourself in the context of a truth, which is that you want it to feel better."— Andrew Huberman · [01:45:53]

His exemplar is David Goggins, who he says built a post-military career on turning effort into reward (Goggins himself came on the show three years later; see our issue 4). He also ties his Stanford colleague Carol Dweck's "growth mindset" to this mechanism. Another example is intermittent fasting: some people feel clearer the longer they go, come to enjoy "sixteen hours in," and tell themselves their insulin sensitivity is improving as they go, and those thoughts themselves add reward to the fast.

"So telling yourself that exercise or fasting or studying or listening better or any kind of behavior is good for you will actually reinforce the extent to which it is good for you at a chemical level."— Andrew Huberman · [01:52:19]

Right after, he cites a study reporting that hearing information that confirms a belief you already hold can itself trigger dopamine release, an example he himself calls "somewhat eerie." Put those two side by side: the prefrontal cortex can re-price primitive circuits, which makes it both a handle for self-training and an entry point for persuasion and manipulation. The thread lands on the episode's conclusion: where your dopamine goes is, to a real degree, in your hands.

Fact-check

Cold water raises dopamine to 2.5 times baseline, "rivaling cocaine," with no crash. [01:32:52]

Supported, but stretched

In Šrámek et al. (2000), young men immersed for an hour at 14°C showed a 250% rise in plasma dopamine. But blood dopamine does not get into the brain; comparing it with cocaine numbers from rat brain dialysis uses two different rulers. The same abstract reports adrenaline unchanged and cortisol not rising, unlike the episode's description.

Regular caffeine intake upregulates D2/D3 dopamine receptors, making coffee the exception among stimulants. [00:46:27]

Supported, but stretched

Volkow et al. (2015) gave 20 men a single 300 mg dose; 17 of them did not normally drink coffee at all. PET showed increased receptor "availability," which the authors say could reflect more receptors or changed affinity. The long-term effect of regular use was not tested.

Rewarding an activity makes you like the activity itself less, because the reward's dopamine peak lowers your baseline. [01:39:15]

Supported, but stretched

Deci et al.'s 1999 meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected tangible rewards do undermine free-choice interest (d about −0.3 to −0.4), but positive verbal feedback increases it (d = 0.33). None of these studies measured dopamine; "the peak lowers the baseline" is Huberman's own gloss.

Crossovers

  • Reinforcement learning · reward prediction error: Huberman mentions the term only once, yet it is the hardest version of "dopamine is relative." Wolfram Schultz found that monkey dopamine neurons fire for unexpected rewards, go quiet once the reward is predicted, and dip below their background rate when an expected reward fails to arrive; that signal was later recognized as the error term in temporal-difference (TD) learning. Where it matches: pleasure is relative. Where it doesn't: here the reference point is a learned expectation, not a depleted stockpile. The post-race slump may not mean empty vesicles; it may just mean raised expectations.
  • Neuroscience · automatic gain control: sensory systems routinely normalize signals against their recent average; it is why your eyes recover after you walk indoors from bright sun. Huberman's peaks and baselines are the same structure with the same cost: adapt to strong stimuli and you go blind to weak ones, which is why, he says, two days off hyper-palatable food makes broccoli taste good again.
  • Behavioral ecology · the marginal value theorem: Eric Charnov's 1976 foraging model says an animal should leave a patch when its intake rate there drops below the environment's average. Satisfaction must fade for the animal to move on, which is Huberman's berries story exactly. The difference: Charnov describes an optimal decision rule, with no stockpile being drained.
  • Buddhism · taṇhā (craving): the Four Noble Truths locate the origin of suffering in craving; satisfaction does not stay, and we chase the next thing. Huberman describes the same loop but prescribes scheduling craving more cleverly; Buddhism aims to see through the loop itself.

Ten takeaways

  1. Huberman argues that "dopamine hit" misleads: how much dopamine something gives depends on how far the peak rises above your baseline at that moment.
  2. Huberman's flagged key point: after you experience or crave something very pleasurable, your dopamine baseline drops below where it started.
  3. Huberman explains the drop with the "readily releasable pool": dopamine vesicles are finite, a big release must wait for resynthesis, and the drop scales with the peak.
  4. Huberman says craving, motivation and pleasure share a single currency, dopamine, so "work hard, play hard" people can burn out as many activities jointly lower their baseline.
  5. Huberman defines addiction as a progressive narrowing of the things that bring you pleasure, until only one is left.
  6. Huberman's stacking trap: coffee, pre-workout, music and phone layered onto one workout raise the bar for enjoying the next one.
  7. Huberman borrows casinos' intermittent rewards and suggests truly random boosts, such as flipping a coin before the gym to decide on music.
  8. Huberman holds that end-of-task rewards drain the joy from the process, and that the way out is telling yourself, at peak strain, that the effort itself is good.
  9. Huberman argues the prefrontal cortex can re-price primitive dopamine circuits, so telling yourself fasting or exercise is good for you reinforces that benefit chemically.
  10. Reward prediction error in reinforcement learning makes "pleasure is relative" more rigorous: the reference point is a learned expectation, not only a depleted stockpile.
