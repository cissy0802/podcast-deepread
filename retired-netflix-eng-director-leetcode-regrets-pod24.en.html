David Ronca: long hours are often the bill for bad leadership, and hiring should look for engineering instinct

David Ronca × Ryan Peterman · "Retired Netflix Eng Director: Leetcode, Regrets, Hiring Stories"

Show The Peterman Pod · 2026-02-16 · Length 79 min · Guest David Ronca · Host Ryan Peterman · Video YouTube

The moment

In January 2004, David Ronca had been running flat out for four years at InterVideo, a video software company. He usually got in around ten or ten-thirty in the morning; an early night meant leaving at ten, a late one meant two or three a.m., often seven days a week. The company had just gone public and needed new deals to hold up the stock price. Nobody thought the pace was strange.

One Sunday he started feeling ill. He did not get better all week, and on Friday his wife drove him to the emergency room. The doctor told him it was colon cancer before the tests had even confirmed it; his digestive tract was blocked. It was stage three, with lymph-node spread, and he remembers a five-year survival prognosis of about 25%. He spent three weeks in hospital and had two surgeries. He had three children, aged seven, four and two, and the youngest knew him mostly as the man who came in late. Lying there, he worked something out:

"And they were actually using work hours to compensate for really bad leadership."— David Ronca · [00:53:44]

The episode is billed as LeetCode and hiring stories, but that hospital bed is what holds it together. How he later ran teams, judged candidates and compared Netflix with Meta almost all traces back to it.

Who's talking, and when

Ronca's career ran thirty-six years. He joined Netflix in 2007 at a $175K salary, which he stresses was a strong number at the time. There he built the encoding technology team from one person to more than fifty and helped drive the VMAF video-quality metric. In July 2019 he moved to Facebook to run a video processing team of roughly sixty, later stepped back from director to individual contributor, and is now retired. Retirement, as the intro puts it, has left him completely unfiltered.

Host Ryan Peterman also came up at Meta; his show interviews senior engineers and likes to walk level by level through promotions, performance and regrets. In this one Peterman asks little and mostly lets Ronca tell his story.

The episode came out in February 2026. The Netflix Ronca describes is the Netflix of 2007 to 2019, when the company had a single engineering level; Netflix only introduced levels in 2022. Much of his critique is aimed at a system the company has since changed itself.

Glossary

  • Personal top of market: Netflix's pay principle. Salary tracks what this person could earn elsewhere, not a band attached to a level.
  • PSC and calibration: Meta's performance cycle. Managers turn self-reviews and feedback into a packet, then defend it before a room of other managers who decide the rating.
  • Overleveling: being hired at a level above what you can actually perform.

The episode, unpacked

1. Hours are not commitment; they are the invoice for a management failure

Out of hospital, Ronca told his boss he wanted to stay but could not work that way again. He says some of his best engineering at InterVideo came afterwards, while he was ramping back to full time. Three years later he interviewed at Netflix, and chief talent officer Patty McCord told him the company did not value 24/7 work and would not be impressed by people who worked all the time. As Ronca recalls her words:

"If you want to impress us, blow us away with what you can do in an 8 hour day."— David Ronca, recalling Patty McCord · [00:01:58]

He gives two Netflix examples. A manager said in a meeting that he could not keep working like this; McCord thanked him for trying so hard, then told him never to do it again. As a leader, his job was to set up his team so he did not have to work all the time. The second was Ronca's own. During Netflix's early move to continuous deployment, one team was keeping unstable systems alive through every weekend. Ronca took the lead engineer aside and told him to take a week off; if things broke, they broke. He went, the rest of the team took time off in turn, and once they stopped firefighting and redesigned the system their own way, it stabilized quickly.

His advice to young engineers comes straight from this:

"If they can't survive without you for seven days, that's not your problem, that's their problem."— David Ronca · [00:55:56]

He opens the episode with the counter-example. At a 1990s company, the engineer everyone considered most critical had a cubicle so full of junk the walls bulged out. Managers could not say what he did; they only knew that if he quit, they were finished. He did quit, the company did not die, and the people who moved in discovered the problem had simply been that nobody could work with him. Ronca does not deny crunch exists: heads-down before a launch is normal, but it has to be the exception, followed by stepping back and taking a vacation. He sums it up with a poster he loved at Meta, a silhouetted rocking horse over the line "don't mistake motion for progress."

2. LeetCode measures practice; he was looking for engineering instinct

Ronca says that when he made hire decisions himself at smaller companies, his batting average was close to a thousand. His method is the opposite of puzzle interviews. His verdict on LeetCode is blunt:

"It tells you how fast somebody can write code for a problem that they probably practiced a hundred times."— David Ronca · [00:21:19]

What he looks for instead he calls an engineering foundation: the ability to understand complex systems, strong technical intuition, and making good decisions without enough data. He tells two hiring stories. In the late 1990s a candidate who had built automation software for driverless trains got lukewarm marks on coding questions from the other interviewers. In Ronca's slot, a 45-minute interview ran an hour and a half as she filled two whiteboards with the system's state transitions. He pushed the hire past his boss's hesitation, and she excelled.

The second is stranger. Shortly before joining Meta, Ronca was taking graduate classes at San Jose State, and his project partner was a civil engineer with a fresh master's degree who worked in wastewater treatment. He wanted her at Netflix. Given a video coding take-home, she had never really written code; she took three weeks and came back with rookie mistakes but impressive work. Ronca suggested she come in and teach the team something. All she knew was wastewater, so she spent two hours at a whiteboard on that, and halfway through he realized it was a very complex finite state machine. The hiring manager walked out muttering about what sewage had to do with software. Years later he ran into Ronca at an event and told him she was killing it, and asked how he had known.

The catch is how he defines the trait:

"You're born with it. You have it or you don't."— David Ronca · [00:23:37]

He admits the approach does not scale. Companies hiring at the volume of Meta or Google must hold everyone to the same standard, so they need a single bar. Within that world his advice is practical: when unsure, take the lower level. Come in one level down and perform one level up, and the bonus multipliers and stock refreshers will show it; get overleveled and struggle, and big companies have almost no way to correct it.

3. Where a small company's culture cracks as it grows

Ronca clearly loves Netflix, but he calls its famous culture memo aspirational rather than descriptive. It was written for a small, aggressive, engineering-centered company, and as the company grew it cracked in three places.

The first was levels. There was a single engineering level, and your business card could say anything except manager, director, VP or chief. With only senior people that worked; once juniors arrived, teams had strong and weak engineers and no objective way to tell them apart, and the best people left first. The second was pay. Personal top of market meant that an outside offer moved your salary. Three years on, someone who had doubled their pay on one offer might earn three times a peer at the same level, and leaders saw only numbers with no context.

The third was credit. Netflix celebrated the team, not individuals. The strongest engineer Ronca ever worked with was Ioannis Katsavounidis; their work around 2010 led to content-based encoding, and Katsavounidis later carried it a step further into the convex-hull encoding optimization that both Netflix and Meta now use. Ronca felt that in a culture without individual credit he never got the recognition he deserved. Around 2018 Katsavounidis got a Facebook offer and had a spark in his eye Ronca had not seen in a while, and asked him:

"Aren't you going to try to talk me out of it?"— Ioannis Katsavounidis, as recalled by David Ronca · [00:35:34]

As his boss, Ronca said, he did not want to lose him; as his friend, he could not see how he could say no. Within about a year and a half Ronca followed. At Meta he found that the performance system outsiders describe as frightening supplied exactly what Netflix lacked: self-reviews turned into packets, calibration meetings where other managers challenge them, and arguments over who actually did what settled with data rather than volume. Handing a direct report a "Redefines Expectations" rating, he says, was the greatest joy he had as a leader. Looking back, he says he learned more about leadership at Meta than at Netflix, and the thing he would bring to any next job is an objective system for planning, review and performance.

Fact check

Ronca: LeetCode only shows how fast someone solves a problem they have practiced a hundred times, and "does not tell you anything about an engineer." [00:21:19]

Supported, but overstated

A 2020 randomized trial with 48 computer science students found that solving the same problem in front of an interviewer cut performance by more than half; whiteboard interviews do measure anxiety along with skill. But personnel-selection meta-analysis still finds cognitive ability tests and work samples correlate around 0.3 with job performance. A weak, noisy signal is not the same as no signal.

Ronca: engineering foundation is innate; you have it or you don't. [00:23:37]

Opinion, not fact

His evidence is two successful hires, with no record of the people he rejected or the calls he got wrong. The same meta-analysis ranks structured interviews (same questions, same scoring rubric for everyone) first, and his habit of interviewing the person rather than following a process is close to the opposite of structure.

Ronca: in his Netflix years there was one software engineering level, pay followed outside offers, it broke down at scale, and levels came later. [00:11:39]

Supported

The Pragmatic Engineer reported in 2022 that nearly all of Netflix's roughly 2,000 software engineers held the title Senior Software Engineer, a single level kept for about 25 years, replaced in August 2022 by levels E3 to E7. The report names cost as the main driver, consistent with the pay drift Ronca describes.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Chaos Monkey: Ronca forcing an engineer onto vacation and letting things break is Netflix's own Chaos Monkey applied to people. Chaos Monkey randomly kills servers in production to prove there is no single point of failure; mandatory leave proves a team does not depend on one person, and the engineer with the bulging cubicle was a primary with no replica. The difference: Chaos Monkey is small doses, every day, while human failure tends to be one large crash, and the cancer allowed no rehearsal.
  • Neuroscience · stress and the prefrontal cortex: acute stress impairs prefrontal working memory, and a whiteboard interview tests exactly that, holding an algorithm in mind while talking. It offers a mechanism for why being watched halves scores.
  • Social science · Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. LeetCode began as a sample of programming ability; once the problem banks were public and drilled a hundred times, it increasingly measures hours of practice, which is Ronca's complaint.
  • Evolutionary anthropology · Dunbar's number: people can sustain roughly 150 stable relationships. McCord interviewing every hire and Reed Hastings meeting new staff four at a time is a trust-based culture that works at small-group scale. The difference: Netflix's later problem was not only knowing too many people, but pay numbers that had lost their context.

Ten takeaways

  1. David Ronca was diagnosed with stage three colon cancer in 2004 after four years of seven-day weeks at InterVideo, and made work-life balance a fixed rule for how he led teams.
  2. Ronca argues that a company that runs on employees' long hours is using work hours to compensate for bad leadership.
  3. Patty McCord told Ronca in his Netflix interview that Netflix did not value 24/7 work, only what someone could do in an eight-hour day.
  4. Ronca ordered an exhausted firefighting engineer to take a week off, and after the team rotated through vacations it stabilized the system quickly.
  5. Ronca's advice: if a company cannot survive seven days without you, that is the company's problem, and crunch must stay the exception.
  6. Ronca sees LeetCode as mostly measuring practice, and values understanding complex systems, technical intuition and deciding well without enough data.
  7. Ronca hired a civil engineer who knew only wastewater treatment because her explanation of the plant was really a complex state machine.
  8. Ronca advises candidates who are unsure to accept the lower level, because large companies have almost no way to fix overleveling.
  9. Netflix's early single engineering level and offer-matching pay led to pay drift and lost talent as the company grew.
  10. Netflix's Chaos Monkey kills machines to remove single points of failure, and mandatory vacation applies the same idea to a team.
